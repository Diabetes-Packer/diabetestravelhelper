Canada Travel Guide

Traveling with Diabetes in Canada: The Pharmacy, the Food, and the Trip (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Toronto in January, Vancouver in February, Banff in March: Canada is the closest big trip an American with diabetes can take, and it still has rules that catch people out. It is also the seventh country on this site to get its own destination guide, after Japan, Mexico, Spain, Italy, France, and the United Kingdom, and the reason is simple. Canada is the one country in this series where the pharmacy rule flips: insulin sits behind the counter with no prescription legally required, the border allows a 90-day supply, and the winter cold is the real diabetes risk. The differences are specific, and they are the ones this guide covers: a personal use import limit that turns on whether you are a visitor, a controlled medicine rule that changed in October 2026, a pharmacy system where the pharmacist runs the sale, and a food label that puts the carbs right where you can read them. None of it is hard. All of it is specific. This guide covers the import rules, the pharmacy, and the trip itself, in that order, so you can sort the paperwork at home and enjoy the trip once you land.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. I have not traveled to Canada with diabetes. This guide is not a trip report, and it does not pretend to be one. It is built from the published guidance of Health Canada, the federal health department, the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), the agency that runs the border, the Canadian Air Transport Security Authority (CATSA), the agency that runs airport security screening in Canada, the United States Department of State, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Diabetes Canada, the charity for people with diabetes in Canada, Dexcom, Insulet, and Environment and Climate Change Canada. Where a rule comes from one of those sources, this guide says so. Where something is my own travel habit, it is labeled as mine, and it is kept separate from the official rules. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own travel plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not going, tell your care team.

1. The import rule in one paragraph, then the details

Canada does not run a certificate system like Japan's. There is no import form to file before you fly, and no office that stamps your insulin. The paperwork is a prescription and a letter, and the rule turns on whether you are a visitor. Health Canada's guidance on bringing health products into Canada for personal use sets the traveler rule: a personal use quantity is no more than a 90-day supply or a single course of treatment, whichever is less, based on the directions for use on the label. Visitors to Canada may bring a personal quantity of a prescription drug for their own use, or for a person or animal under their care and travelling with them. If you are staying more than three months, you may bring in an additional 90-day supply by mail or courier every three months, and you must be ready to provide evidence of your visitor status, such as a passport copy or a study or work visa. The medicine must arrive in the package the pharmacy dispensed it in, in its original retail package, or with its original label attached and easy to read, and the label must clearly say what the product is and what it contains. Our customs and border rules guide covers the general version of this system for any country, and our international travel guide covers the wide version of traveling abroad with diabetes. Canada is where the general version gets specific.

Here is the part that matters most for insulin: insulin is a prescription drug, but it is not a controlled substance, so the controlled medicine rules in section 3 do not apply to it. The 90-day personal use limit does apply, so pack for the trip length plus the 20 percent buffer in section 2, and do not carry a six-month stockpile across the border. If the trip runs longer than the supply you can carry, our long-stay abroad playbook covers the planning for a trip that outlasts the supply in your bag, and the mail rule above is the piece that makes a long Canada stay work.

Two more rules from the State Department page. First, the entry rules are the simplest in this series. United States citizens do not need a tourist visa to visit Canada for less than 180 days, and no vaccinations are required. Your passport must be valid at time of entry. There is no six-month validity rule on the State Department page: it says only that the passport must be valid when you enter. You can also enter with a valid United States passport card or a NEXUS card, although a passport card cannot be used for domestic flights within Canada, so fly with the passport book. Declare 10,000 Canadian dollars or more on entry and exit. Second, a warning that surprises Americans who drive across: Canadian immigration law includes provisions that bar entry for travelers with convictions for driving under the influence, so if that applies to you, sort it out with the embassy before the trip, not at the border. Our travel letter guide covers what the letter should say, and our pre-trip appointment guide covers the full appointment, including asking for generic-name prescriptions before you leave. A prescription that lists the generic name is the one that travels. A brand name that only exists in your home country does not.

2. Insulin at the border: pens, syringes, and what to carry

Insulin travels the same way it travels everywhere: in the bag that stays with you, in its original box with the pharmacy label on it. Syringes have one packing rule of their own. Keep the syringes with the injectable medicine they accompany, and make sure the doctor's letter mentions them. Do not plan to bring a loose syringe on its own. Pack the syringes with the insulin they belong to, and list them in the letter, so the letter and the bag agree with each other. Canada's airport security rules, covered in section 5, are friendlier than most on this point: insulin is exempt from the liquid limits, so a larger volume is fine in the carry-on.

Two habits from my own travel routine, kept separate from the border rules. Everything diabetes-related rides in my carry-on, never in checked luggage, and I pack 20 percent extra of everything: pods, sensors, insulin, test strips, lancets, ketone strips, and the low kit. Our packing list does the counting for the trip length. The 20 percent buffer covers the pod that fails on a ski slope in Banff and the sensor that peels off in the cold on a January walk in Toronto. Bring backup insulin for injections in case a pod fails mid-trip, and keep your insulin plan written down somewhere that is not the pump. The flight there follows the same airport rules as any trip, covered in our TSA rules guide for the United States departure and our airport security abroad guide for the arrival side, and the Canada arrival has its own rules, covered in section 5.

3. What needs extra paperwork, and what cannot cross the border

Some medicines need extra paperwork before you travel, and the rule changed recently, so an older guide will give you the old number. Health Canada's traveller page for prescription medications that contain controlled substances sets the current rule: you can bring up to a 90-day supply of a controlled substance, based on the usual daily dose. The medication must be prescribed by a practitioner, such as a doctor, dentist, or nurse practitioner. It must be in a container obtained from a health care provider, and the label must show your name, the name of the practitioner who prescribed it, the name or brand name of the medication, the daily dose, the form, quantity, and strength, and the drug identification number, if any. It must be for you, or for a person you are responsible for who is travelling with you. You cannot send a product containing a controlled substance into Canada by mail. You must declare the medication to customs when you arrive in Canada. The same requirements apply when you enter or leave Canada. If your stay is longer than 90 days, contact the Exemptions Section at Health Canada before you travel. That 90-day figure is new: the new Controlled Substances Regulations took effect on 1 October 2026 and raised the traveller limit from 30 days to 90 days, so a guide written before that date will tell you 30 days, and it is out of date. Check every medicine by its active ingredient, not its brand name, before you pack. The active ingredient is what the law sees. The brand name is not.

Adderall is the one that catches travelers by surprise, and in Canada the surprise runs the other way from the United Kingdom. Adderall XR, the mixed amphetamine salts used for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), is sold in Canada: Health Canada's Drug Product Database lists it as marketed, with Takeda Canada as the company and an original market date of 30 January 2004. It is a controlled substance, and amphetamines and methylphenidate sit in Schedule 2 of the new Controlled Substances Regulations. That means the traveller rule above is the rule for it: up to a 90-day supply, prescribed, in a labelled container from a health care provider, declared to customs on arrival. Methylphenidate is sold in Canada too, as Ritalin, Concerta, and Biphentin. If you take a stimulant medicine, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about the paperwork, and carry the prescription and the letter with the medicine. The answer cannot be found at the border.

Cannabis is a separate problem, and it is the one that catches Americans who assume the border works like the dispensary. Cannabis is legal in Canada for adults, but you cannot take it across the border in either direction. The State Department page says it plainly: it is illegal to cross the border with undeclared weapons, ammunition, or cannabis related items, including edible cannabis, cannabis extracts, and items containing CBD. Do not bring cannabis, edibles, extracts, or CBD products into Canada, and do not try to take any home. Leave them at home.

4. The pharmacy: insulin needs no prescription by law, and a prescription in practice

The pharmacy on the main street is a drugstore, and you will find one in every mall and on most main streets in Toronto, Montreal, Vancouver, and every town in between. Chain pharmacies such as Shoppers Drug Mart and Rexall are the familiar names, and most stay open late. The first thing to understand is the rule that matters most, and it is the opposite of every other country in this series: insulin is not on the Health Canada Prescription Drug List, so no prescription is legally required to buy it. Canada's National Drug Schedules put insulin in Schedule II, which means it is kept behind the pharmacy counter, the pharmacist must be involved in the sale, and there is no public access and no self-selection. You cannot pick it off a shelf. You ask the pharmacist, and the pharmacist decides.

So the pharmacy visit, if you need one, has a fixed sequence, and the honest version has two steps, not one. First, bring your prescription and your doctor's letter anyway. In practice, most pharmacists will ask for a prescription, especially for modern analog insulins, and will set up a patient profile before selling it, to confirm the insulin is appropriate for you. A pharmacist can sell insulin without a prescription in an emergency, to keep your current insulin therapy going when you have run out or cannot see a doctor, but do not plan a trip around that. Second, if you need a prescription written in Canada, see a doctor in Canada. A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in Canada: a Canadian pharmacy does not work from American paper. A walk-in clinic is the fastest route for a straightforward replacement. Bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product. Tell the doctor the generic name of your insulin, not just the brand: insulin aspart, insulin lispro, insulin glargine, insulin degludec. The pharmacist and the doctor both work from the generic name, and the generic name is what survives the brand change at the border. The good news is that the brand names are the same as at home. Diabetes Canada lists NovoRapid and Humalog among the rapid-acting insulins, and Lantus, Basaglar, Toujeo, and Tresiba among the long-acting insulins, so NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid in Canada, and both are insulin aspart. Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine. Our finding insulin abroad playbook covers the name game in full, including the concentration check that matters more than the brand name.

Two more things to know before you walk in. First, most insulin pens in Canada use the same U-100 standard, 100 units in each milliliter, so a pen dial counts the same way it does at home. Tresiba is the exception to watch for: it also comes in a pen at 200 units per milliliter, and so does the Humalog KwikPen at 200 units per milliliter, so check the concentration printed on the box before you use it, because a syringe is calibrated for one concentration only. Second, visitors pay. Canada's provincial health plans cover eligible residents of that province only, so a visitor has no health card and pays the full amount for a clinic visit and for prescriptions. United States Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for medical care outside the United States. Keep every receipt for your travel insurance claim, and check your policy before the trip so the clinic visit is not the first time you read it. Our travel insurance guide covers what to declare and what to look for.

5. Your devices in Canada: the app, the sensors, and the airport

Start with the phone, because it holds the one trap that ruins more international trips than any other. The Dexcom G7 app is region-locked at install: Dexcom's own user guide states the app must be installed in your country of residence, and the app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when you first install it. The check happens at install time. An app that is already installed and working keeps working abroad, and travelers report normal operation on international trips as long as nobody deletes the app mid-trip. So install the G7 app and update it to the latest version before you leave, turn off auto-updates before the trip so nothing about your diabetes setup changes mid-trip without your say-so, and do not delete the app while you are in Canada. A deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country, and that is a long trip without CGM data. Our G7 app region-lock guide covers the full prep list, and nothing in it is specific to Canada: the same rule applies everywhere you land.

The receiver is the backup that makes the app problem a non-issue. Dexcom's own international travel guidance says that if you have a receiver, it makes a great backup to your phone app. The receiver has no app store, no region check, and no install step. It pairs with your sensor and shows readings anywhere on earth. Pack it with its USB cable and charger. Canada uses the same plugs as the United States, so no adapter is needed, but the cable still belongs in the bag. Our devices charging guide covers plugs and power on the road. If you do not already own a receiver, ask Dexcom about getting one before a big international trip. Bring the fingerstick meter too, as the final fallback, and keep it in your day bag rather than buried in your hotel room.

Buying replacement sensors in Canada is a pharmacy problem, not a shop problem. The G7 is sold in Canada: Dexcom's Canada site says you can buy it at a local pharmacy with or without a prescription, and that it is covered by most private, provincial, and federal health insurance plans in Canada. The Omnipod 5 is approved in Canada and commercially available there since 7 April 2025, and it can connect with the Dexcom G6 and the Dexcom G7 in Canada. Public reimbursement is currently in place in Ontario and Nova Scotia, and availability in other provinces and territories varies, so a replacement bought outside those two provinces may mean paying the full amount. A replacement sensor bought in Canada still means a foreign pharmacy, a local prescription question, and regional compatibility questions you do not want to solve on vacation. Pack more sensors than the trip math says you need, with the spares in your carry-on, and do not plan to shop. The same applies to pump supplies. Carry spare Pods and a backup injection plan with syringes and a vial, and keep your basal rates written down somewhere that is not the pump. Our pod failure backup plan covers the sequence when a Pod fails away from home.

The Canada airport is the one place in this series where the screening rules are written down by the agency that runs them, and they are friendlier than the United States rules on liquids. The Canadian Air Transport Security Authority (CATSA), the agency that runs airport security screening in Canada, publishes a page for medication and medical items. Its guidance is plain: tell the screening officer you are wearing an insulin pump or a CGM, explain that the device is medically necessary and cannot be removed, and do not remove your pump or CGM. Request alternate screening, because pumps and CGMs may be sensitive to x-ray machines or full-body scanners. Diabetic supplies and equipment such as syringes, insulin auto-injectors, jet injectors, and pumps are permitted in the carry-on, and the page names the Omnipod 5 as an example. Liquid medications such as insulin are exempt from the liquid restrictions, including the gel or ice packs used to refrigerate the medication, and can be carried in carry-on baggage in volumes larger than 100 ml. Juice and gel for a diabetic condition are also permitted, and must be declared to the screening officer separately. Keep the medication properly labelled, with the pharmacy label or the manufacturer name on it. Tell the officer what you are wearing and where it is, and ask for the alternate screening if the scanner is the next step. Our Omnipod 5 airport guide covers the United States side of the same trip, and our scanner comparison guide covers which machine is safe for which device.

6. Time zones: six clocks, and the gaps that never change

Canada keeps six clocks, more than any other country in this series, and that is the first thing to sort before you fly. From west to east they are Pacific Time, UTC-8, Mountain Time, UTC-7, Central Time, UTC-6, Eastern Time, UTC-5, Atlantic Time, UTC-4, and Newfoundland Time, UTC-3:30, the only half-hour zone in North America. A trip that pairs Vancouver with Toronto needs two clock settings, not one, and a trip that adds St. John's needs three. Most of Canada changes its clocks on the same dates as the United States: forward on the second Sunday in March and back on the first Sunday in November. In 2026 that means 8 March and 1 November. Because both countries move on the same Sundays, the gap between a Canadian city and its United States counterpart in the same zone never changes. Toronto runs on the same clock as New York, and Vancouver runs on the same clock as Los Angeles, and that does not change when the clocks move. Halifax is 1 hour ahead of New York, and St. John's is 1 hour 30 minutes ahead, as the standard gap, and those gaps hold through the spring and autumn clock changes.

Two provinces break the pattern, and Saskatchewan is the one that catches travelers. Most of Saskatchewan stays on Central Standard Time and does not observe daylight saving time, a practice in place since 1966. In summer, Saskatchewan matches Alberta, and in winter it matches Manitoba, so the gap to a Saskatchewan city depends on the month you fly. Yukon stopped observing daylight saving time in 2020 and stays on Mountain Standard Time year round. A few areas in British Columbia, Quebec, Ontario, and Nunavut also stay on one clock through the year. If your trip crosses a provincial line, check the clock for each stop, not just the first one. Either way, a cross-country trip is a large enough jump that the insulin timing needs a plan before the flight, not after landing.

The plan follows the rule in our time zone insulin guide. If you use a pump, change the pump clock to local time when you land, so your basal rates, the background insulin the pump drips all day, run on local time. Most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, so this is one manual step at the gate or the hotel. If you take multiple daily injections, keep your long-acting insulin on home time on the travel day, then switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone when you arrive, for example bedtime. Meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch: take it when you eat, wherever you are. The guide covers the eastbound and westbound cases in full, and the timing shift for a cross-country Canada trip is large enough that the careful planning earns its keep. Run your route through our time zone planner before you fly, and confirm the plan with your care team. The planner adjusts timing only, never dose amounts. Dose changes are a conversation with your care team, not something a tool or a blog post should decide.

7. Eating in Canada: the label that puts the carbs first

Canada is one of the easiest countries in the world to count carbs in, because the label puts the number where you can read it, in both official languages. Almost all prepackaged foods must carry a Nutrition Facts table, in English and French, and the table shows Carbohydrate, in French Glucides, in grams, with Fibre and Sugars, in French Sucres, indented beneath it. The table states the serving size at the top, and the carbohydrate line is the number to use for the portion in front of you. Compare two products by the carbohydrate line, not by the front of the pack, because the serving sizes are whatever each maker chose. This guide gives no carb counts for specific foods, because the number on the package in front of you is the one that counts, and it is printed right there.

The front of the pack got a new job in 2026. Since 1 January 2026, Health Canada requires a front-of-package nutrition symbol on foods that are high in saturated fat, sugars, and/or sodium. The symbol is black and white, shows a magnifying glass, and reads High in, followed by whichever of the three applies, with Health Canada named at the bottom. The threshold is generally 15 percent of the daily value per reference amount, lower for small portions and higher for main dishes. For diabetes, the useful part is the Sugars bar: if the symbol says High in Sugars, the food is high in sugar by the government's own measure, and the Nutrition Facts table on the back gives you the grams. A food with no symbol is not automatically low in carbs. The symbol flags sugar, saturated fat, and sodium, not total carbohydrate, so the back of the pack is still where the carb count lives.

Restaurant food has no label, so it works the way eating out works everywhere. Ask what is in the dish, estimate the carbohydrate from the bread, the potatoes, or the pastry, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. I pre-bolus in my usual way before meals. That habit travels with me, and Canada does not change it. A diner breakfast often comes with toast and home fries on the side, so the side dishes count too: estimate them with the rest of the meal, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. Our eating out playbook covers the ordering and estimating routine in full, and our pre-bolus guide covers the timing habit. For lows, keep sugar packets in your day bag. I treat lows with sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, and that is the low kit that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day in any country. Do not let the low kit be something you buy after you need it. Pack it before you leave the hotel.

Water is the other half of eating safely in Canada, and the news is good in the cities. Health Canada states that Canadians generally have access to drinking water of very high quality, and municipal supplies are monitored under provincial and territorial requirements, against the Guidelines for Canadian Drinking Water Quality. Tap water from a municipal supply in Toronto, Montreal, or Vancouver is generally safe to drink. In a remote area, check for a local boil water advisory before you drink from the tap. A stomach bug is a diabetes problem, not just a comfort problem, because vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the plan for that day.

Vaccines are a question for the care-team appointment, not for the airport. No vaccine is required to enter Canada, and the appointment is the place to check that routine vaccines are up to date. Our travel vaccines playbook covers the appointment in full.

8. Winter in Canada: the cold is the diabetes risk

Winter is the season this guide is written for, and the Canadian winter is cold in a way that changes the diabetes routine, not just the packing list. Toronto January afternoons average a high of 30F, with nights near 16F, on the Environment and Climate Change Canada normals for the Pearson Airport station, and January brings about a foot of snow on average. July is the other end of the range: Toronto July afternoons average a high of 81F. Vancouver is the mild exception: January averages a high of 44F and a low of 35F, with rain instead of snow. Wherever you go, the cold rule for insulin is the same rule it follows everywhere. Unopened insulin belongs in the fridge at 36 to 46F, and in-use insulin is fine at room temperature below 77F. Insulin freezes at 32F, and frozen insulin must be discarded, so a January afternoon in Toronto is cold enough to freeze insulin left in a bag. Keep in-use insulin close to your body on a cold day, inside your coat rather than in an outer pocket, and never leave insulin or spare sensors in a freezing car overnight. Our insulin cooling guide covers the methods, and our hotel insulin storage guide covers the minibar trap in detail: hotel minibar fridges can freeze items at the back, so keep unopened insulin away from the cooling element and check that it has not frozen.

All-day walking in the cold is the other half of the winter plan, and it pulls glucose down. A day that starts at the CN Tower, crosses Toronto on foot, and ends with dinner across town can mean hours on foot without trying, in air well below freezing. Activity lowers glucose, so check more often than usual on a walking day, carry sugar packets in your pocket rather than in the bag you left at the hotel, and keep a snack in the day bag for the walk back. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. The low kit is the same one that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day: sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60.

Then there is the cold, which is the one place where your devices meet a real limit on a Canada trip. Dexcom publishes exact temperature numbers for the G7. The sensor's operating range is 50F to 107.6F, and its storage range is 36F to 86F. A worn sensor sits against warm skin under your coat, so a normal winter day does not push it out of range, but a spare sensor left in a cold car can drop below its storage floor overnight. Keep spares warm, inside, and check the readings against your fingerstick meter after a long cold day. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. Cold stiffens adhesive too. Press the sensor and pod edges down firmly after coming inside from the cold, and pack extra overpatches. Our G7 heat and cold guide has the full temperature table, and our adhesion playbook covers keeping a sensor and a pump stuck through holiday trips.

If the trip is a ski trip instead, Banff and Whistler bring the full cold routine: a worn sensor sits against warm skin under your layers, so normal ski days do not push it out of its operating range, but spare sensors must be stored between 36F and 86F, so do not leave them in the car at the lodge. Our skiing playbook covers the cold and altitude routine for a ski trip, and the same habits apply to a cold morning walking around Quebec City or Ottawa.

9. If something goes wrong: numbers, clinics, and the insurance call

Save these numbers in your phone before you land. Dial 911 for any emergency. It is the number for the ambulance, the police, and the fire brigade, and it is free from any phone. If you need health advice but it is not an emergency, dial 811 in most provinces and territories: a registered nurse will talk through the question for free, and Ontario's Health811 does not require a health card number to use. Manitoba and Nunavut use a different number instead, Health Links-Info Sante at 1-888-315-9257, so check the line for the province you are in before you need it. If you need the United States Embassy, call +1-613-688-5335 in Ottawa, or +1-613-286-7304 after hours. The consulates in Vancouver, Toronto, Montreal, and Quebec City are listed on the State Department page if your trip takes you there. Canada has no reciprocal healthcare agreement with the United States, so there is no card that covers you. Provincial health plans cover residents of that province only, so a visitor pays for medical care out of pocket, and the hospital will bill you at non-resident rates. Check your travel insurance before the trip, so the call is not the first time you read it, and keep every receipt from any clinic or pharmacy visit.

If you develop symptoms of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous complication where the blood turns acidic when insulin runs too low, get urgent help right away. Vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation, and that is a hospital visit, not a wait-and-see day. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the ketone checks and the plan for that day. Carry glucagon in your day bag, and make sure your travel companion knows where it is and how to use it. Our glucagon emergency kit guide covers what is in the kit and how to keep it travel-ready. If the trip goes wrong in a bigger way, a bag lost or stolen, our lost or stolen supplies playbook covers the exact call order, and the travel insurance emergency line is the call that arranges care, including a local doctor visit. If the trip involves driving, your United States licence works for a visit under 3 months in Ontario, and for 6 consecutive months in Quebec, and each province sets its own limit, so check the province you are driving in. No International Driving Permit (IDP) is needed for a short visit, because your licence is printed in English. Remember that Canada drives on the right, the same side as at home, and carry your licence and your passport with you. Our driving abroad guide covers licenses, rental cars, and the rules of the road.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I buy insulin over the counter in Canada?

A: Legally, yes, but do not plan on it. Insulin is a Schedule II drug in Canada, which means no prescription is legally required to buy it, but it is kept behind the pharmacy counter and the pharmacist must be involved in the sale. In practice, most pharmacists will ask for a prescription, especially for modern analog insulins, and will set up a patient profile before selling it. Bring your prescription and your doctor's letter anyway. A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in Canada, so if you run out, a Canadian doctor has to write the prescription.

Q: How much medicine can I bring into Canada?

A: As a visitor, you may bring a personal quantity of a prescription drug for your own use: no more than a 90-day supply or a single course of treatment, whichever is less, based on the directions for use. Carry your insulin in its original boxes with the pharmacy labels, carry a copy of your prescription and a letter from your prescriber that lists your medicines by their generic names, and keep the syringes with the insulin they accompany. Medicines that contain a controlled substance have their own rule: up to a 90-day supply, in a labelled container from a health care provider, and you must declare them to customs when you arrive.

Q: Can a Canadian pharmacy fill my prescription from home?

A: No. A prescription issued abroad cannot be filled in Canada. If you run out of insulin, see a doctor in Canada, at a walk-in clinic or a hospital clinic, show your prescription and your doctor's letter so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the Canadian prescription to a pharmacy. The brand names are the same as at home: NovoRapid, Humalog, Lantus, and Tresiba are all sold in Canada. Visitors pay the full amount for medical care and for prescriptions, because provincial health plans cover residents of that province only.

Q: What about Adderall and other controlled medicines?

A: Adderall XR is sold in Canada, so the surprise runs the other way from the United Kingdom: it is a controlled substance, and the traveller rule is what matters. You may bring up to a 90-day supply of a medicine that contains a controlled substance, based on the usual daily dose, if it was prescribed by a practitioner, it is in a container obtained from a health care provider with a label showing your name, the prescriber, the drug, the daily dose, and the strength, and you declare it to customs when you arrive. You cannot send it into Canada by mail. That covers opioid painkillers, benzodiazepines, and stimulants such as methylphenidate and amphetamines. Cannabis is legal in Canada for adults, but you cannot take it across the border in either direction, so do not bring cannabis, edibles, extracts, or CBD products into Canada.

Q: Will my Dexcom G7 app work in Canada?

A: Yes, if it was already installed and set up at home. The Dexcom G7 app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when it is first installed, so install and update it before you leave and do not delete it while you are abroad: a deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country. Bring the Dexcom receiver as backup. It has no app store and no install check, and it pairs with your sensor anywhere. The G7 is sold in Canada, and you can buy it at a local pharmacy with or without a prescription. The Omnipod 5 is approved in Canada and commercially available there since April 2025, and it can connect with the Dexcom G6 and the Dexcom G7 in Canada, although public reimbursement is currently in place only in Ontario and Nova Scotia.

Q: What are the emergency numbers in Canada?

A: Dial 911 for any emergency. It is the number for the ambulance, the police, and the fire brigade, and it is free from any phone. If you need health advice but it is not an emergency, dial 811 in most provinces and territories: a registered nurse will talk through the question for free. Manitoba and Nunavut use a different number instead, Health Links-Info Sante at 1-888-315-9257, so check the line for the province you are in. If you need the United States Embassy, call +1-613-688-5335 in Ottawa. Provincial health plans cover residents only, so a visitor pays for medical care out of pocket, and travel insurance is the cover that matters.
