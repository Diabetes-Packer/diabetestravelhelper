Germany Travel Guide

Traveling with Diabetes in Germany: The Pharmacy, the Food, and the Trip (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Berlin in January, Munich in July, the Rhine in between, and the Christmas markets in December: Germany is a big European trip an American with diabetes can take, and it still has rules that catch people out. It is also the eighth country on this site to get its own destination guide, after Japan, Mexico, Spain, Italy, France, the United Kingdom, and Canada, and the reason is simple. Germany flips the pharmacy rule the other way from Canada: insulin is prescription-only, a United States prescription cannot be filled, and a German doctor has to write a new one. The differences are specific, and they are the ones this guide covers: a three-month import limit, a certificate rule for controlled medicines that works differently for travelers from outside the Schengen area, a pharmacy system where only one kind of shop sells medicine, and a food label that puts the carbs per 100 grams where you can read them. None of it is hard. All of it is specific. This guide covers the import rules, the pharmacy, and the trip itself, in that order, so you can sort the paperwork at home and enjoy the trip once you land.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. I have not traveled to Germany with diabetes. This guide is not a trip report, and it does not pretend to be one. It is built from the published guidance of German customs (Zoll), the German Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM), the United States Department of State, the European Commission, Dexcom, Insulet, the German Federal Environment Agency, the 116117 medical on-call service, and the German Weather Service (DWD). Where a rule comes from one of those sources, this guide says so. Where something is my own travel habit, it is labeled as mine, and it is kept separate from the official rules. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own travel plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not going, tell your care team.

1. The import rule in one paragraph, then the details

Germany does not run a certificate system like Japan's. There is no import form to file before you fly, and no office that stamps your insulin. The paperwork is a prescription and a letter, and the amount turns on a simple rule from German customs. Travellers may bring medicinal products with them in quantities that meet their usual personal needs, and German customs treats usual personal need as a maximum of a three-month supply of the recommended dose of each medicinal product. It does not matter whether the medicine was bought abroad or carried out of Germany and brought back, and it does not matter whether the product is registered in Germany. The medicine must be for your own use. One warning from the same customs page: products that are sold freely in your own country, such as food supplements, vitamin preparations, or purely vegetable natural medicines, may be treated as medicinal products in Germany, so the same personal-use limit can apply to them. Our customs and border rules guide covers the general version of this system for any country, and our international travel guide covers the wide version of traveling abroad with diabetes. Germany is where the general version gets specific.

Here is the part that matters most for insulin: insulin is a prescription drug, but it is not a narcotic, so the controlled medicine rules in section 3 do not apply to it. The three-month personal use limit does apply, so pack for the trip length plus the 20 percent buffer in section 2, and do not carry a six-month stockpile across the border. If the trip runs longer than the supply you can carry, our long-stay abroad playbook covers the planning for a trip that outlasts the supply in your bag. Do not plan to have prescription medicine mailed to you in Germany: the State Department page says you cannot receive prescription medications by mail in Germany, because the customs rules need special permissions.

Two more rules from the State Department page. First, the entry rules. United States citizens do not need a tourist visa to visit Germany for less than 90 days, and no vaccinations are required. Your passport must have 3 months validity beyond your date of departure, and it must have 2 blank passport pages. Six months of validity is recommended, but it is not required. Declare over 10,000 euros on entry and exit. Second, the State Department's own tip for medicine: check with the German Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices to make sure your medication is legal in Germany before you travel. Our travel letter guide covers what the letter should say, and our pre-trip appointment guide covers the full appointment, including asking for generic-name prescriptions before you leave. A prescription that lists the generic name is the one that travels. A brand name that only exists in your home country does not.

2. Insulin at the border: pens, syringes, and what to carry

Insulin travels the same way it travels everywhere: in the bag that stays with you, in its original box with the pharmacy label on it. The State Department page says it plainly: always carry your prescription medication in its original packaging, along with your doctor's prescription. Syringes have one packing rule of their own. Keep the syringes with the injectable medicine they accompany, and make sure the doctor's letter mentions them. Do not plan to bring a loose syringe on its own. Pack the syringes with the insulin they belong to, and list them in the letter, so the letter and the bag agree with each other. Germany's airport security rules, covered in section 5, are friendlier than most on this point: liquid medicine you need during the trip is exempt from the 100 ml limit, so a larger volume is fine in the carry-on, with a prescription or a medical certificate to show for it.

Two habits from my own travel routine, kept separate from the border rules. Everything diabetes-related rides in my carry-on, never in checked luggage, and I pack 20 percent extra of everything: pods, sensors, insulin, test strips, lancets, ketone strips, and the low kit. Our packing list does the counting for the trip length. The 20 percent buffer covers the pod that fails on a December walk through a Christmas market and the sensor that peels off on a January day in Berlin. Bring backup insulin for injections in case a pod fails mid-trip, and keep your insulin plan written down somewhere that is not the pump. The flight there follows the same airport rules as any trip, covered in our TSA rules guide for the United States departure and our airport security abroad guide for the arrival side, and the Germany arrival has its own rules, covered in section 5.

3. What needs extra paperwork, and what cannot cross the border

Some medicines need extra paperwork before you travel, and Germany is the country in this series where the paperwork depends on where you are flying from. Medicines that fall under the Narcotic Drugs Act must be specifically prescribed by the attending doctor, and the certificate you need depends on your route. The United States is outside the Schengen area, so the rule for an American traveler is the one German customs sets for entry from a country outside the Schengen area: have your prescribing physician issue a multilingual certificate specifying the individual and daily doses, the name of the active ingredient, and the duration of the journey, and have it authenticated by the competent health authority of the country of provenance. There is no set form for this certificate. The State Department page adds the practical detail for Americans: there is no such issuing authority in the United States, so have your doctor certify the form, enter the practice information, and add an office stamp or seal if possible. The separate Schengen certificate, under Article 75 of the Schengen Agreement, is the one for travel inside the Schengen area, for up to 30 days, and it is certified before the journey by the highest regional health authority. That is not the document an American flying in from the United States uses. Either way, the State Department page sets the amount: for medicines Germany classifies as narcotics, you may only carry a 30-day supply. If your trip crosses another country on the way, check that country's transit rules too: German customs says so, because there are no uniform provisions for carrying narcotics outside the Schengen area.

Check every medicine by its active ingredient, not its brand name, before you pack. The active ingredient is what the law sees. The brand name is not.

Adderall is the one that catches travelers by surprise, and in Germany the surprise is that it is not sold there at all. Adderall, the mixed amphetamine salts used for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), is not approved in Germany, so a German pharmacy cannot sell it to you and there is no local replacement under that brand name. Methylphenidate is sold in Germany, as Ritalin, Concerta, and Medikinet, and lisdexamfetamine is sold as Vyvanse. Stimulant medicines are narcotics under German law, so if you take one, the certificate rule above is the rule for it: a multilingual certificate from your prescribing doctor, authenticated by the competent health authority in your home country, and no more than a 30-day supply. If you take a stimulant medicine, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about what happens if you run out, and carry the prescription and the letter with the medicine. The answer cannot be found at the border.

Cannabis is a separate problem, and it is the one that catches travelers who assume a border works like a dispensary. Do not bring cannabis, edibles, extracts, or CBD products into Germany, and do not try to take any home. Leave them at home.

4. The pharmacy: insulin is prescription-only, and the prescription has to be German

The pharmacy in Germany is called an Apotheke, and you will find one on most main streets in Berlin, Munich, Hamburg, and every town in between, marked by a big red A sign. The first thing to understand is the rule that matters most, and it is the opposite of Canada: insulin is prescription-only in Germany. The German word is verschreibungspflichtig, and the rule sits in section 48 of the Medicines Act, which says these medicines may be dispensed only where a doctor's prescription exists. Which medicines are in this tier is set by a federal regulation, and the pharmacist has no discretion to be nice about it. Do not confuse the Apotheke with a Drogerie. A drugstore such as DM or Rossmann sells no medicine at all. The Apotheke is the only place in Germany where you can legally buy medicine, and all medicines are kept behind the pharmacist's counter. You cannot pick insulin off a shelf. You ask the pharmacist, and the pharmacist works from the prescription.

So the pharmacy visit, if you need one, has a fixed sequence, and the honest version has two steps, not one. First, see a doctor in Germany. A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in Germany: German pharmacies generally do not accept prescriptions from foreign countries, so a German doctor has to write a local prescription. The usual first stop is a family doctor, a Hausarzt. Call first, say you are a visitor and a self-paying patient, a Selbstzahler, and ask whether they can see you the same day. Outside practice hours, dial 116117, the medical on-call service covered in section 9, and it will point you to the nearest on-call practice. Second, take the German prescription to the pharmacy. As a visitor you are a private patient, so the doctor writes a private prescription, a Privatrezept, traditionally on blue paper, and you pay the full amount for the visit and for the medicine, then claim it back from your travel insurance. The electronic prescription, the E-Rezept, is the system for people with German statutory health insurance, and it runs on the German health card, which a visitor does not have. Bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product. Tell the doctor the generic name of your insulin, not just the brand: insulin aspart, insulin lispro, insulin glargine, insulin degludec. The pharmacist and the doctor both work from the generic name, and the generic name is what survives the brand change at the border. The good news is that the brand names are the same as at home. NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid in Germany, and both are insulin aspart. Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine. Our finding insulin abroad playbook covers the name game in full, including the concentration check that matters more than the brand name.

Two more things to know before you walk in. First, most insulin pens use the same U-100 standard, 100 units in each milliliter, so a pen dial counts the same way it does at home. Check the concentration printed on the box before you use a new pen, because a syringe is calibrated for one concentration only. Second, mind the hours. German pharmacies have standard business hours and are often closed in the evenings, on Sundays, and on public holidays. The emergency pharmacy rota, the Notdienst-Apotheke, covers those hours, and the nearest one is listed on pharmacy doors and on apotheken.de. Do not wait until Sunday night to find it. Third, visitors pay. United States Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for medical care outside the United States, and most care providers abroad only accept cash payments, so keep every receipt for your travel insurance claim, and check your policy before the trip so the clinic visit is not the first time you read it. Our travel insurance guide covers what to declare and what to look for.

5. Your devices in Germany: the app, the sensors, and the airport

Start with the phone, because it holds the one trap that ruins more international trips than any other. The Dexcom G7 app is region-locked at install: Dexcom's own user guide states the app must be installed in your country of residence, and the app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when you first install it. The check happens at install time. An app that is already installed and working keeps working abroad, and travelers report normal operation on international trips as long as nobody deletes the app mid-trip. So install the G7 app and update it to the latest version before you leave, turn off auto-updates before the trip so nothing about your diabetes setup changes mid-trip without your say-so, and do not delete the app while you are in Germany. A deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country, and that is a long trip without CGM data. Our G7 app region-lock guide covers the full prep list, and nothing in it is specific to Germany: the same rule applies everywhere you land.

The receiver is the backup that makes the app problem a non-issue. Dexcom's own international travel guidance says that if you have a receiver, it makes a great backup to your phone app. The receiver has no app store, no region check, and no install step. It pairs with your sensor and shows readings anywhere on earth. Pack it with its USB cable and charger. Germany uses Type C and Type F plugs at 230 volts, so bring a plug adapter: United States plugs do not fit German outlets. Most modern phone chargers and laptops are dual voltage and only need the adapter, but check the label on each device before you plug it in. Our devices charging guide covers plugs and power on the road. If you do not already own a receiver, ask Dexcom about getting one before a big international trip. Bring the fingerstick meter too, as the final fallback, and keep it in your day bag rather than buried in your hotel room.

Buying replacement sensors in Germany is a pharmacy problem, not a shop problem. The G7 has been sold in Germany since October 2022, when Dexcom launched it there, and the Omnipod 5 launched in Germany in 2023 and works with the G7 there. A replacement sensor bought in Germany still means a foreign pharmacy, a local prescription question, and regional compatibility questions you do not want to solve on vacation. Pack more sensors than the trip math says you need, with the spares in your carry-on, and do not plan to shop. The same applies to pump supplies. Carry spare Pods and a backup injection plan with syringes and a vial, and keep your basal rates written down somewhere that is not the pump. Our pod failure backup plan covers the sequence when a Pod fails away from home.

The Germany airport is the one place in this series where the liquid rule is changing in stages, so plan for the strict version. Across the EU, liquids in hand luggage are limited to containers of up to 100 ml, in one transparent resealable bag of up to 1 litre. Liquid medicine you need during the trip is exempt from that limit: insulin and its accessories can be carried in the amount needed for the flight, with a prescription or a medical certificate to show for it. Keep medication in its original packaging with the patient information leaflet where possible, and carry the doctor's letter with it. Some German airports now use CT scanners that allow larger liquid containers at certain checkpoints. Munich Airport scrapped the 100 ml rule at all of its checkpoints from 18 September 2026, so a flight out of Munich may have no liquid limit at all. The 100 ml rule still applies at most other German airports, so pack for it and treat a relaxed checkpoint as a bonus. Tell the screening officer about the pump or CGM and where it sits, and ask for alternate screening if the scanner is the next step. Our Omnipod 5 airport guide covers the United States side of the same trip, and our scanner comparison guide covers which machine is safe for which device.

6. Time zones: one clock, and the gap that narrows twice a year

Germany keeps one clock for the whole country, which makes it the simplest time zone in this series, and the gap is still large enough to plan around. Germany runs Central European Time, UTC+1, in winter, and Central European Summer Time, UTC+2, in summer. In 2026 the clocks move forward on Sunday 29 March and back on Sunday 25 October. Berlin is 6 hours ahead of New York as the standard gap, and 9 hours ahead of Los Angeles. The gap narrows twice a year, because the United States and Germany do not move their clocks on the same Sundays. The United States moves forward on 8 March and back on 1 November 2026, so from 8 to 29 March and from 25 October to 1 November, Berlin is 5 hours ahead of New York and 8 hours ahead of Los Angeles. A trip that falls in one of those windows needs the narrowed gap in the plan, not the standard one.

The plan follows the rule in our time zone insulin guide. If you use a pump, change the pump clock to local time when you land, so your basal rates, the background insulin the pump drips all day, run on local time. Most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, so this is one manual step at the gate or the hotel. If you take multiple daily injections, keep your long-acting insulin on home time on the travel day, then switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone when you arrive, for example bedtime. Meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch: take it when you eat, wherever you are. The guide covers the eastbound and westbound cases in full, and a six-hour jump is large enough that the careful planning earns its keep. Run your route through our time zone planner before you fly, and confirm the plan with your care team. The planner adjusts timing only, never dose amounts. Dose changes are a conversation with your care team, not something a tool or a blog post should decide.

7. Eating in Germany: the label that puts the carbs per 100 grams

Germany is one of the easiest countries in the world to count carbs in, because the label puts the number where you can read it, in grams per 100 grams. Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 requires the vast majority of pre-packed foods to bear a nutrition declaration, and it must show the energy value and the amounts of fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, and salt. The amounts must be expressed per 100 g or per 100 ml, in a legible tabular format on the pack. The German label reads Kohlenhydrate, with davon Zucker, of which sugars, indented beneath it. The per-100-gram basis is the useful part: it lets you compare two products directly, and it lets you scale the number to the portion in front of you. Compare two products by the carbohydrate line, not by the front of the pack. Front-of-pack nutrition information is not mandatory under the current EU rules, so the back of the pack is where the carb count lives. This guide gives no carb counts for specific foods, because the number on the package in front of you is the one that counts, and it is printed right there.

Restaurant food has no label, so it works the way eating out works everywhere. Ask what is in the dish, estimate the carbohydrate from the bread, the potatoes, or the pastry, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. I pre-bolus in my usual way before meals. That habit travels with me, and Germany does not change it. A December Christmas market is the same routine in a colder setting: the stalls sell food with no label, so estimate the dish, pre-bolus in your usual way before eating, and keep the sugar packets in your pocket rather than in the bag you left at the hotel. Our Christmas markets playbook covers the market routine in full. Our eating out playbook covers the ordering and estimating routine, and our pre-bolus guide covers the timing habit. For lows, keep sugar packets in your day bag. I treat lows with sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, and that is the low kit that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day in any country. Do not let the low kit be something you buy after you need it. Pack it before you leave the hotel.

Water is the other half of eating safely in Germany, and the news is good in the cities. The Federal Environment Agency states that drinking water from large central systems in Germany is of very good quality, and that it can be drunk without any reservations. Tap water from a municipal supply in Berlin, Munich, or Hamburg is generally safe to drink. A stomach bug is a diabetes problem, not just a comfort problem, because vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the plan for that day.

Vaccines are a question for the care-team appointment, not for the airport. No vaccine is required to enter Germany, and the appointment is the place to check that routine vaccines are up to date. Our travel vaccines playbook covers the appointment in full.

8. Winter in Germany: the cold is the diabetes risk

Winter is the season this guide is written for, and the German winter is cold in a way that changes the diabetes routine, not just the packing list. Berlin January afternoons average a high of 38F, with nights near 29F, and July is the other end of the range, with afternoons averaging a high of 76F and nights near 59F. Munich runs a touch colder: January afternoons average a high of 37F, with nights near 25F, and July afternoons average a high of 74F, with nights near 55F. Wherever you go, the cold rule for insulin is the same rule it follows everywhere. Unopened insulin belongs in the fridge at 36 to 46F, and in-use insulin is fine at room temperature below 77F. Insulin freezes at 32F, and frozen insulin must be discarded, so a January night in Munich is cold enough to freeze insulin left in a bag. Keep in-use insulin close to your body on a cold day, inside your coat rather than in an outer pocket, and never leave insulin or spare sensors in a freezing car overnight. Our insulin cooling guide covers the methods, and our hotel insulin storage guide covers the minibar trap in detail: hotel minibar fridges can freeze items at the back, so keep unopened insulin away from the cooling element and check that it has not frozen.

All-day walking in the cold is the other half of the winter plan, and it pulls glucose down. A day that starts at the Brandenburg Gate, crosses Berlin on foot, and ends with dinner across town can mean hours on foot without trying, in air near freezing. Activity lowers glucose, so check more often than usual on a walking day, carry sugar packets in your pocket rather than in the bag you left at the hotel, and keep a snack in the day bag for the walk back. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. The low kit is the same one that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day: sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60.

Then there is the cold, which is the one place where your devices meet a real limit on a Germany trip. Dexcom publishes exact temperature numbers for the G7. The sensor's operating range is 50F to 107.6F, and its storage range is 36F to 86F. A worn sensor sits against warm skin under your coat, so a normal winter day does not push it out of range, but a spare sensor left in a cold car can drop below its storage floor overnight. Keep spares warm, inside, and check the readings against your fingerstick meter after a long cold day. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. Cold stiffens adhesive too. Press the sensor and pod edges down firmly after coming inside from the cold, and pack extra overpatches. Our G7 heat and cold guide has the full temperature table, and our adhesion playbook covers keeping a sensor and a pump stuck through holiday trips.

If the trip is a ski trip instead, the Alps bring the full cold routine: a worn sensor sits against warm skin under your layers, so normal ski days do not push it out of its operating range, but spare sensors must be stored between 36F and 86F, so do not leave them in the car at the lodge. Our skiing playbook covers the cold and altitude routine for a ski trip, and the same habits apply to a cold morning walking around Munich or Hamburg. Summer has the other edge: July afternoons in the mid-70s are mild, but heatwaves happen, and in-use insulin belongs below 77F, so keep it out of direct sun and out of a hot car.

9. If something goes wrong: numbers, clinics, and the insurance call

Save these numbers in your phone before you land. Dial 112 for an ambulance or the fire brigade, and 110 for the police. Both are free from any phone, and you may ask for an English-speaking attendant on 112. If you need medical help urgently but it is not life-threatening, and the doctor's practices are closed, dial 116117, the medical on-call service. It runs around the clock, 7 days a week, and it can point you to the nearest on-call practice, or send a doctor where needed. The phone service works in German only, so have your address and your symptoms written down before you call. If you need the United States Embassy, call +49-30-8305-0 in Berlin, at Clayallee 170, 14191 Berlin. The consulates in Frankfurt, Munich, Hamburg, Dusseldorf, and Leipzig are listed on the State Department page if your trip takes you there. Germany has no reciprocal healthcare agreement with the United States, so there is no card that covers you. A visitor pays for medical care out of pocket, and most care providers abroad only accept cash payments. Check your travel insurance before the trip, so the call is not the first time you read it, and keep every receipt from any clinic or pharmacy visit.

If you develop symptoms of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous complication where the blood turns acidic when insulin runs too low, get urgent help right away. Vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation, and that is a hospital visit, not a wait-and-see day. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the ketone checks and the plan for that day. Carry glucagon in your day bag, and make sure your travel companion knows where it is and how to use it. Our glucagon emergency kit guide covers what is in the kit and how to keep it travel-ready. If the trip goes wrong in a bigger way, a bag lost or stolen, our lost or stolen supplies playbook covers the exact call order, and the travel insurance emergency line is the call that arranges care, including a local doctor visit. If the trip involves driving, your United States licence works for up to 6 months in Germany, with no German licence needed, and each rule is set nationally, so check the current limit before you drive. The blood alcohol limit is 0.05 percent, and in snowy or icy conditions your vehicle must have snow or all-season tires with an M+S marking. A first-aid kit, a reflective vest, and a reflective triangle must always be in the vehicle. Remember that Germany drives on the right, the same side as at home, and carry your licence and your passport with you. Our driving abroad guide covers licenses, rental cars, and the rules of the road.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I buy insulin in Germany without a prescription?

A: No. Insulin is prescription-only in Germany, and a pharmacist cannot sell it to you without a prescription from a German doctor. A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in Germany, so if you run out of insulin, see a doctor in Germany, at a family doctor practice or through the 116117 on-call service, show your prescription and your doctor's letter so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the German prescription to a pharmacy. As a visitor you are a private patient, so you pay the full amount for the visit and for the medicine, and you claim it back from your travel insurance. The brand names are the same as at home: NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid in Germany, and both are insulin aspart. Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine.

Q: How much medicine can I bring into Germany?

A: German customs allows travellers to bring in medicinal products in quantities that meet their usual personal needs, and usual personal need means a maximum of a three-month supply of the recommended dose of each medicinal product. Carry your insulin in its original boxes with the pharmacy labels, carry a copy of your prescription and a letter from your prescriber that lists your medicines by their generic names, and keep the syringes with the insulin they accompany. Medicines that Germany classifies as narcotics have their own rule: only a 30-day supply, with a multilingual certificate from your prescribing doctor that states the individual and daily doses, the active ingredient, and the length of the trip, authenticated by the competent health authority in your home country. You cannot receive prescription medicines by mail in Germany.

Q: Can a German pharmacy fill my prescription from home?

A: No. German pharmacies generally do not accept prescriptions from foreign countries, and a prescription from the United States cannot be filled in Germany. If you run out of insulin, see a doctor in Germany, show your prescription and your doctor's letter so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the German prescription, a private prescription, to a pharmacy. Only a pharmacy, an Apotheke, sells medicine in Germany. A drugstore such as DM or Rossmann sells no medicine. Pharmacies are often closed in the evenings, on Sundays, and on public holidays, so check the emergency pharmacy rota if you need one outside normal hours.

Q: What about Adderall and other controlled medicines?

A: Adderall, mixed amphetamine salts, is not approved for sale in Germany, so a German pharmacy cannot sell it to you, and there is no local replacement under that brand name. Methylphenidate is sold in Germany as Ritalin, Concerta, and Medikinet, and lisdexamfetamine is sold as Vyvanse. Stimulant medicines are narcotics under German law, so if you take one, carry a multilingual certificate from your prescribing doctor that states the individual and daily doses, the active ingredient, and the length of the trip, authenticated by the competent health authority in your home country, and carry no more than a 30-day supply. Talk to your prescriber well before the trip about what happens if you run out, because the answer cannot be found at the border. Do not bring cannabis, edibles, extracts, or CBD products across the border in either direction.

Q: Will my Dexcom G7 app work in Germany?

A: Yes, if it was already installed and set up at home. The Dexcom G7 app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when it is first installed, so install and update it before you leave and do not delete it while you are abroad: a deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country. Bring the Dexcom receiver as backup. It has no app store and no install check, and it pairs with your sensor anywhere. The G7 has been sold in Germany since October 2022, and the Omnipod 5 launched in Germany in 2023 and works with the G7 there. Germany uses Type C and Type F plugs at 230 volts, so bring a plug adapter: United States plugs do not fit German outlets.

Q: What are the emergency numbers in Germany?

A: Dial 112 for an ambulance or the fire brigade, and 110 for the police. Both are free from any phone, and you may ask for an English-speaking attendant on 112. If you need medical help urgently but it is not life-threatening, and the doctor's practices are closed, dial 116117, the medical on-call service. It runs around the clock, 7 days a week, and it can point you to the nearest on-call practice. The phone service works in German only, so have your address and your symptoms written down before you call. If you need the United States Embassy, call +49-30-8305-0 in Berlin. United States Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for medical care outside the United States, so a visitor pays for medical care out of pocket, and travel insurance is the cover that matters.
