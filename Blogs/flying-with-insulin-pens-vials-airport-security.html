Flying with Insulin Pens and Vials: Airport Security and In-Flight Guide (2026)

Pump travelers get most of the airport advice, but plenty of people with diabetes fly with pens and vials instead. The good news: the rules for you are simpler, and almost everything you need fits in your carry-on.

Updated October 2026  ·  8 min read

I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and have flown with an insulin pump for most of them, so the pump guides on this site come from lived experience. Pens and vials are a different story: the airport rules are simpler, there is no device to keep out of the scanners, and the questions travelers ask are different. This guide answers the ones that come up most, using the American Diabetes Association's published air travel guidance and manufacturer travel guidance where it exists.

What TSA actually allows

The American Diabetes Association publishes a list of diabetes items permitted through airport security, and it covers everything a pen or vial traveler carries:

  • Insulin and insulin-loaded dispensing products. Vials, boxes of individual vials, preloaded syringes, and pen devices.
  • An unlimited number of unused syringes and pen needles when accompanied by insulin or other injectable medication.
  • Used syringes, when carried in a sharps disposal container or similar hard-surface container.
  • Liquid medications, which are exempt from the 3-1-1 liquids rule. Declare them to the officer at the checkpoint.
  • Meters, test strips, lancets, alcohol swabs, and all other diabetes-related medication, equipment, and supplies.

TSA does not require you to carry a prescription, though the ADA notes that having one may speed up screening if you are pulled aside. You also do not need to notify the airline in advance to carry insulin and needles. A travel letter from your doctor is optional, and most travelers are never asked for one, but it costs nothing to carry.

Pens and vials through the X-ray

This is the big difference from pump travel. Insulin pumps carry manufacturer warnings against X-ray and full-body scanners, but pens and vials do not. They go through the carry-on bag X-ray with the rest of your bag.

Tandem Diabetes Care's published travel guidance states it directly: unlike a pump, insulin vials can safely pass through the X-ray luggage scanner with your carry-on luggage. The American Diabetes Association's guidance says the same in plainer terms: under normal conditions, insulin can safely pass through X-ray machines at airport terminals. If you would rather your insulin not go through the machine, you can ask for a hand inspection instead.

Practical tip from frequent travelers: keep all of your diabetes supplies in one clear pouch inside your carry-on. If an officer wants a closer look at anything, you hand over one pouch instead of unpacking your whole bag.

Needles, syringes, and the spare pen

Pen needles and syringes are the items new travelers worry about most, and they are the least of your problems at the checkpoint. Unused needles are explicitly permitted when you are carrying insulin. Pack more than you need: pen needles are light, and a bent or lost needle on day three of a trip is an avoidable problem.

Pack a spare pen. Pens can jam, crack, or get left in a hotel room, and a backup pen weighs almost nothing. If you use a reusable pen, pack spare cartridges too. For used needles, bring a small hard-sided container. Cap each needle after use and store it there until you can dispose of it properly at your destination. Do not hand loose sharps to the cabin crew.

Injecting on the plane

There is no rule against injecting insulin on the plane. Where you do it is personal preference: at your seat or in the lavatory both work, and nobody is required to announce anything.

One piece of timing advice comes up constantly in traveler discussions, and it is worth repeating: wait until the meal is actually served before injecting mealtime insulin. Meals can be delayed, swapped, or smaller than expected, and injecting for food that has not arrived yet is how in-flight lows happen. Keep your fast-acting glucose at your seat, not in the overhead bin, so it is reachable with the seatbelt sign on.

Eat like you do at home. Airline meals are unpredictable, so many travelers keep it simple: a protein-forward meal, easy on the unknowns. It is the same approach that works on the ground.

Temperature: the cold problem on fall and winter trips

Heat gets all the attention, but holiday-season flights bring the opposite risk. Insulin freezes at 32°F (0°C), and frozen insulin must be discarded. A pen riding in an outer coat pocket on a freezing jet bridge, or a bag left in a cold car at the airport, can get there.

Keep insulin close to your body in cold weather, not in outer pockets or a bag sitting in the cold. And never put insulin in checked luggage in any season: the cargo hold can freeze, and a lost bag means lost insulin. Most opened pens and vials stay good at room temperature for about 28 days, but check your insulin's label for the exact number. For the full heat and cold breakdown, see how to keep insulin cool while traveling.

Crossing time zones with long-acting insulin

If you take a long-acting (basal) insulin once or twice a day plus mealtime (bolus) insulin, crossing time zones shifts your schedule with the clock. The size of the shift depends on how many hours you gain or lose and in which direction.

Pen and vial travel checklist

Here is the full list, in this order:

Flying with Insulin Pens and Vials: Pre-Flight Checklist

  • Keep every pen, vial, needle, and syringe in your carry-on, never in checked luggage
  • Pack a spare pen, plus spare cartridges if you use a reusable pen
  • Bring more pen needles than you need, and a small hard-sided container for used ones
  • Put all diabetes supplies in one clear pouch for faster screening
  • Declare liquid medications to the officer; they are exempt from the 3-1-1 rule
  • Carry a copy of your prescription and consider a travel letter
  • Keep fast-acting glucose at your seat, not in the overhead bin
  • Wait until the meal is served before injecting mealtime insulin
  • In cold weather, keep insulin close to your body, never in outer pockets or a cold car
  • Run your route through the time zone planner if you are crossing time zones
  • Make sure your travel insurance covers your diabetes before you fly

Frequently Asked Questions

Can insulin pens go through airport X-ray machines?

Yes. Unlike insulin pumps, pens and vials have no manufacturer restriction against carry-on X-ray machines. Tandem Diabetes Care's published travel guidance states that insulin vials can safely pass through the X-ray luggage scanner with your carry-on, and the American Diabetes Association notes that under normal conditions insulin can safely pass through X-ray machines at airport terminals. If you prefer, you can ask for a hand inspection instead.

Can I bring needles and syringes on a plane?

Yes. The American Diabetes Association's air travel guidance lists insulin and insulin-loaded dispensing products, including vials and preloaded syringes, as permitted through security, along with an unlimited number of unused syringes when accompanied by insulin. Used syringes must be carried in a sharps disposal container or similar hard-surface container. TSA does not require a prescription, but carrying one may speed up screening.

Can I inject insulin on the plane?

Yes, there is no rule against it. Many travelers wait until the meal is actually served before injecting mealtime insulin, in case the meal is delayed or different from expected. Inject at your seat or in the lavatory, whichever you prefer, and store each used needle in a small hard-sided container until you can dispose of it properly at your destination.

Should insulin go in checked luggage?

No. Insulin can freeze in the cargo hold, and frozen insulin must be discarded. Keep every pen, vial, and needle in your carry-on. Most opened pens and vials stay good at room temperature for about 28 days, but check your insulin's label for the exact number.

How do I handle long-acting insulin across time zones?

Your long-acting insulin schedule shifts with the clock when you cross time zones. The timing shift depends on how many hours you gain or lose, and our free time zone planner computes it for you. It adjusts timing only, never dose amounts, and you should confirm any plan with your care team.
