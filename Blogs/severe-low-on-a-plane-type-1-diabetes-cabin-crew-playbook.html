A Severe Low on a Plane: What Cabin Crew Can (and Cannot) Do for Type 1 Diabetes (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Thanksgiving is 18 days away, November is Diabetes Awareness Month, and the holiday flights are the long ones: five hours across the country, or ten hours across an ocean, with nowhere to land quickly and nobody to call but the people in the cabin. That is the flight where the anxiety moment lives. You go low, the low gets worse, and you cannot treat it yourself. What happens next is not a mystery, and it is not luck. It is a chain of people and rules: the flight attendant you told at boarding, the training the crew has and the training they do not have, the kit in the cabin and what is actually in it, the doctor in row 14 who volunteers, and the captain who decides whether the plane lands early. This guide walks that chain in order, so you can set it up before wheels-up instead of discovering it mid-flight.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I fly with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets. I have never had a severe low on a plane, and this guide does not invent one. Every rule below comes from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) regulations and guidance, the text of the Aviation Medical Assistance Act, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), or the glucagon label, and every place where the rule runs out is marked as such. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team, and section 9 lists exactly what to ask them before the trip.

Talk to your care team before flying about your own severe-low plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. Airline policies and FAA rules change over time, so confirm the current details for your flight before you travel, and ask your care team whether flying is safe for you, especially if you have had a severe low, lost awareness of lows, or have another condition that affects your safety in the air.

This guide is the in-flight companion to the rest of the site, and it does not repeat them. The glucagon guide covers the three glucagon products, their storage limits, and the full companion briefing. The solo travel playbook covers the remote-companion system for flying alone: CGM sharing, the follower plan, and the hotel-room rules. The sick-day playbook covers getting ill on a trip, including the section on getting sick on the plane. The TSA rules guide covers the checkpoint, and the packing list covers what goes in the bag. Read the glucagon guide before this one if you have not packed glucagon yet, and keep this guide for the flight itself.

1. What a severe low means at 35,000 feet

A severe low is not a number. It is a state, and the American Diabetes Association (ADA) defines it by that state. In the ADA Standards of Care 2026, level 1 hypoglycemia is glucose below 70 mg/dL and at or above 54 mg/dL, and level 2 is below 54 mg/dL. Level 3, the severe low, is defined differently: it is "a severe event characterized by altered mental and/or physical functioning that requires assistance from another person for recovery, irrespective of glucose level." The same section adds that level 3 hypoglycemia "can progress to loss of consciousness, seizure, coma, or death." The number does not matter at level 3. What matters is that you cannot recover on your own.

That definition is the whole plan in one sentence, because it names the one thing a severe low takes away: your ability to act for yourself. On the ground, the other person is whoever is in the room. On a plane, the other person has to be found, told, and equipped in advance, because the cabin is a small space with a locked door, no pharmacy, and no hospital down the street. The everyday low still belongs to you: the CGM number at 60 to 70 gets one sugar packet, below 60 gets two, then you check again. The severe low belongs to the people around you, and the rest of this guide is about making sure those people know what to do.

Some scale helps here, from the CDC. Medical emergencies occur on approximately 1 of every 604 commercial flights, and the most common are fainting, breathing symptoms, and nausea and vomiting. For 90 percent of these emergencies, the aircraft continues to its destination. For the remaining 10 percent, the aircraft diverts to another landing site. A severe low is one kind of emergency in that mix, and the 90 percent figure is worth holding on to: most in-flight medical emergencies end at the airport you booked, with help waiting there, not at a surprise runway halfway across the country.

2. Tell the lead flight attendant at boarding

The single most useful thing you can do on a flight happens before takeoff, takes 30 seconds, and costs nothing. Tell the lead flight attendant, the one who greets you at the door or runs the cabin, that you have type 1 diabetes and what to do if you cannot help yourself. You are generally not required to give the airline advance notice of a disability: the US Department of Transportation (DOT) says that under the Air Carrier Access Act (ACAA), the law that bars discrimination against passengers with disabilities, passengers are generally not required to provide advance notice for disability-related accommodations. So this is not a legal step. It is a practical one. The crew cannot help with a problem they do not know exists, and the boarding door is the last calm moment before the cabin fills up.

  • What to say, in one sentence. "I have type 1 diabetes. If I seem confused or I will not wake up, my glucagon is in my seat pocket, and my companion knows how to use it." That sentence gives the crew four facts: the condition, the sign to watch for, the location of the treatment, and the person who can give it. If you are flying alone, change the ending: "please call for medical help and ask if a doctor is on board." Do not hand over a medical history. The crew needs the action, not the backstory.
  • Carry the same sentence on a card. A paper card, or a note on your phone lock screen, that says your name, type 1 diabetes, where the glucagon is, and an emergency contact. If you cannot speak, the card speaks for you, and it gives a seatmate or a crew member the same four facts without a conversation. Keep the card in the seat pocket with the glucagon, not in the overhead bin.
  • Keep your supplies with you, not over your head. DOT's travel tips for passengers with disabilities say to carry medicine or other assistive devices like syringes as a carry-on item, because passengers can get separated unexpectedly from checked baggage, and that if you carry medication or assistive devices on board, the items cannot be counted toward your carry-on baggage limit. On the plane, that means the personal item under the seat in front of you, where your hand can reach it in the dark. The TSA rules guide covers the checkpoint side of the same rule: everything diabetes-related rides in the cabin, never in checked luggage.
  • Point at the seat pocket, not the overhead bin. The glucagon guide says the same thing for the same reason: a kit in the overhead bin helps nobody when the person who needs it cannot stand up. Show your companion, and the crew member you told, exactly where it is. The seat pocket is the spot because it is the one place on the plane that is always within arm's reach of your seat.

3. What cabin crew can do, and what they cannot do

Cabin crew are the first people on the scene in any in-flight medical emergency, and the FAA trains them for that job. What the training covers, and where it stops, is written down in the regulations, and knowing both sides keeps your expectations honest.

What they are trained to do. Under 14 CFR 121.805, the FAA rule on crewmember training for in-flight medical events, every Part 121 crewmember gets instruction in emergency medical event procedures, including coordination among crewmembers, in the location, function, and intended operation of emergency medical equipment, and in the contents of the emergency medical kit (EMK). For each flight attendant, the rule adds instruction, with performance drills, in the proper use of an automated external defibrillator (AED) and in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), plus recurrent training, with performance drills, in both, at least once every 24 months. The companion rule, 14 CFR 121.417 on crewmember emergency training, adds instruction in emergency assignments and procedures, in the location, function, and operation of emergency equipment, and in the handling of emergency situations, including the evacuation of persons who may need the assistance of another person to move to an exit. The CDC puts the practical version in one line: flight attendants are certified in CPR and in the use of an AED, and a medical volunteer should use them as assistants.

What they are not trained to do. The same rule that requires the training also sets its ceiling. Section 121.805 says the crewmember instruction, performance drills, and recurrent training "are not required to be equivalent to the expert level of proficiency attained by professional emergency medical personnel." Crew are first responders, not medics, and the FAA's own guidance says so in plain words. Advisory Circular 121-33B, the FAA guidance on emergency medical equipment, says: "It is unrealistic to expect flight attendants to achieve the same level of proficiency as emergency medical personnel who perform medical procedures on a routine basis. Flight attendants should not be expected to administer medications or to start IVs."

  • They will do first aid, CPR, and the AED. Those are the trained skills, and they are the ones that matter most in the first minutes of any emergency. They will also keep you comfortable, bring the kit, and talk to the cockpit, which is how a diversion gets decided.
  • They should not be expected to give injections, including glucagon. Giving a drug is administering medication, and the FAA guidance above says crew should not be expected to do it. Some airlines publish the rule in exactly those terms. ANA says: "Cabin attendants are not permitted to perform any medical treatment, including administering injections, giving medication, and operating medical equipment. Necessary treatment must be performed by the customer or their escort." Gulf Air says: "The Gulf Air cabin staff are trained in First Aid only, and are not permitted to administer any injection." Those are two airlines that publish the rule. This guide does not claim every airline publishes it the same way, because no US airline page stating the rule was found. The safe plan does not depend on the airline's wording: it depends on the FAA guidance, which covers every US Part 121 carrier, and on naming a companion who can give the glucagon instead.
  • They will ask for a medical volunteer before they open the kit. The FAA guidance says flight attendants should grant access to the equipment only to trained crewmembers or to other persons qualified and trained in the use of emergency medical equipment, and that it would be preferable for flight attendants to check the credentials of passengers holding themselves out as medical specialists. That is why the crew makes an announcement asking if a doctor is on board, and why the next section covers what happens when one answers.

4. What is actually in the plane's emergency medical kit

Every US airline flight you take carries a medical kit, and the contents are set by federal regulation, not by the airline's choice. Section 121.803 of the FAA rules says no person may operate a passenger-carrying airplane under Part 121 unless it is equipped with the emergency medical equipment listed in that section. For treatment of injuries, medical events, or minor accidents that might occur during flight time, each airplane must have approved first-aid kits, and in airplanes for which a flight attendant is required, an approved emergency medical kit. In airplanes for which a flight attendant is required and with a maximum payload capacity of more than 7,500 pounds, an approved AED as well. The kit must be readily accessible to the crew, stored securely, and kept free from dust, moisture, and damaging temperatures.

The full list lives in Appendix A to Part 121, in the version in force since April 12, 2004. Here is what the emergency medical kit, the EMK, must contain, in the quantities the regulation prints:

  • Monitoring and airway gear: one sphygmomanometer (a blood pressure cuff), one stethoscope, three oropharyngeal airways (one pediatric, one small adult, one large adult), one self-inflating manual resuscitation device with three masks, and three CPR masks in the same sizes.
  • Intravenous (IV) gear: one IV administration set with tubing and two Y connectors, two alcohol sponges, one roll of adhesive tape, one pair of tape scissors, one tourniquet, 500 cc of saline solution, one pair of protective gloves, six needles, and four syringes.
  • Drugs: four non-narcotic analgesic tablets (325 mg), four antihistamine tablets (25 mg), two single-dose ampules of injectable antihistamine (50 mg), two single-dose ampules of atropine (0.5 mg), four aspirin tablets (325 mg), one inhaled bronchodilator, one single-dose ampule of dextrose 50 percent, 50 cc, injectable, two single-dose ampules of epinephrine 1:1000, two of epinephrine 1:10,000, two of lidocaine (5 cc, 20 mg per ml), ten nitroglycerin tablets (0.4 mg), and one copy of the basic instructions for use of the drugs in the kit.

The diabetes item in that list is one ampule of 50 percent dextrose, injectable, for IV use. That is the whole diabetes story of the plane's kit, and it has three limits worth understanding before you need it. First, it is an injectable drug that goes into a vein, with the needles, syringes, IV set, and tourniquet listed alongside it. It is not sugar packets, not juice, and not something the crew can hand you to swallow. Second, the kit holds no glucagon at all. Glucagon is your emergency treatment, and it is not in the regulation's list. Third, the kit is for use by a person qualified and trained in the use of emergency medical equipment, per the FAA guidance in section 3. The crew opens it for a medical volunteer, not for a passenger to use on themselves.

The first-aid kit is even simpler, and it holds no sugar either. The CDC lists its contents as adhesive tape, antiseptic swabs, ammonia inhalants, bandage compresses, bandage scissors, a roller bandage, and arm and leg splints. Between the two kits, the plane carries a hospital-style drug for a qualified volunteer and nothing you can eat or drink for a low. That is why your own sugar packets and your own glucagon are the diabetes plan on board, and the kit is the backup for the volunteer, not a substitute for either.

One more limit: the kit above is the US rule. The CDC notes that EMK contents may vary among international carriers, despite guidance from the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), the United Nations body that sets international aviation standards. On a foreign carrier, do not assume the same list, the same drugs, or the same quantities. Your own kit is the part of the plan that does not change with the flag on the tail.

5. If a doctor on board helps

When the crew announces that they need medical help, a doctor, nurse, paramedic, or emergency medical technician (EMT) in the cabin may volunteer. The CDC checklist for medical personnel responding to in-flight emergencies describes how that works in practice: ask the ill person for verbal consent if they are alert and oriented, obtain a medical history, check their pulse and blood pressure, and perform a physical examination appropriate to the problem. Ask the flight attendants for needed items from the first aid kit, the EMK, and the AED. Ask for ground-based medical consultation. Ask about what medical equipment the ill person has in their carry-on, especially for items that are not available in the EMK, such as a glucose meter or medication. Communicate with the pilot through the flight attendants about the ill person's condition.

The law that protects the volunteer. The reason a doctor can step forward without worrying about a lawsuit is a federal law, the Aviation Medical Assistance Act of 1998. Section 5 of the act, titled Limitations on Liability, has two parts. The first protects the airline: an air carrier shall not be liable for damages in any action brought in a Federal or State court arising out of the performance of the air carrier in obtaining or attempting to obtain the assistance of a passenger in an in-flight medical emergency, or out of the acts or omissions of the passenger rendering the assistance, if the passenger is not an employee or agent of the carrier and the carrier in good faith believes that the passenger is a medically qualified individual. The second protects the volunteer directly: "An individual shall not be liable for damages in any action brought in a Federal or State court arising out of the acts or omissions of the individual in providing or attempting to provide assistance in the case of an in-flight medical emergency unless the individual, while rendering such assistance, is guilty of gross negligence or willful misconduct." The act defines a medically qualified individual as any person who is licensed, certified, or otherwise qualified to provide medical care in a State, including a physician, nurse, physician assistant, paramedic, and emergency medical technician.

  • The protection has a limit. Gross negligence or willful misconduct removes it. The CDC states the same limit in plain words: the 1998 act protects medical personnel from damages in federal or state court for providing good-faith medical care in the event of a medical emergency, and it does not cover gross negligence or willful misconduct.
  • The crew will check credentials. The FAA guidance says it would be preferable for flight attendants to check the credentials of passengers holding themselves out as medical specialists, and the CDC notes that a volunteer may be asked to provide documentation of their level of training and licensure. A doctor who volunteers should expect to show a license or ID, and the crew may ask which specialty they practice.
  • On international flights, the protection is less certain. The CDC is direct about this: when responding to a medical emergency on an international flight, the Aviation Medical Assistance Act might not apply, and it is unclear what entity has jurisdiction over liability for care rendered. The country where the aircraft is registered might have jurisdiction, or jurisdiction could be based on the aircraft's geographic location at the time. Most airlines and countries offer protection for Good Samaritans, but a volunteer on a foreign carrier is working under that country's law, not the US act. For you as the passenger, the practical point is the same either way: a volunteer who steps forward is covered by some protection in nearly every case, and the crew will still ask them to help.
  • The volunteer does not work alone. The CDC notes that most commercial aircraft use ground-based medical services to assist with clinical assessment and make decisions regarding severity and flight diversion. The volunteer in the cabin, the crew, and a doctor on the ground form one team, and the next section covers the decision that team feeds into.

6. Sugar, juice, and glucagon: what treats the low, and who gives it

Three treatments exist for a low on a plane, and they belong to three different people. Mixing up who gives which one is the mistake this section exists to prevent.

  • While you can still swallow and act for yourself: your sugar packets. The fastest sugar on the plane is the sugar already in your pocket, because no one has to fetch it and no one has to approve it. One packet at 60 to 70, two packets below 60, then check again. That is the routine from home, and it does not change at altitude. If your own sugar is gone and you can still swallow, ask the crew for juice. The crew serve drinks from the galley, and juice is the fast-acting sugar the glucagon label itself names for the moment after a person responds.
  • When you cannot help yourself: glucagon, given by someone else. That is the entire point of glucagon, and the label says so: severe hypoglycemia requires help of others to recover, so the patient should inform those around them about the product and its instructions for use. The full product comparison, storage limits, and companion briefing live in the glucagon guide, and this guide does not repeat them. The procedure facts that matter on the plane are these, from the Baqsimi label: administer as soon as possible when severe hypoglycemia is recognized, the dose does not need to be inhaled, call for emergency assistance immediately after administering the dose, and if there has been no response after 15 minutes, an additional dose may be administered while waiting for emergency assistance. If the person is unconscious, turn them on their side. When they can safely swallow, give a fast-acting source of sugar such as juice.
  • Never put food or drink in the mouth of someone who cannot swallow. The label's condition is the rule: sugar by mouth comes only when the person can safely swallow. An unconscious person who is given juice or sugar by mouth can choke on it. Turn them on their side, give the glucagon, and call for help. The sugar waits until they are awake enough to swallow it safely.
  • The plane's dextrose is not your plan. The single ampule of 50 percent dextrose in the EMK needs a vein, a needle, and a qualified person to give it. It is the treatment a medical volunteer reaches for when your glucagon is not there or has not worked, not the first treatment for a low. If you are counting on the kit instead of your own glucagon, you are counting on a volunteer being on board, the crew opening the kit for them, and an IV being started in a shaking cabin. Pack your own glucagon and brief your companion, and the kit stays what it is: a backup.

7. How the captain decides on a diversion

A diversion is the most expensive and disruptive decision on a flight, and it is also the one with the clearest chain of responsibility. The FAA guidance puts the decision with the carrier and its agents: the decision to offer treatment or take other action, including safe diversion of the aircraft, is discretionary with the air carrier and its agents, and the FAA does not require any actions by the air carrier and its agents or other passengers other than having certain emergency medical equipment on board. The FAA guidance also says that if a critical in-flight medical event occurs and a passenger medical specialist is not available, the sick passenger should be made as comfortable as possible and the pilot in command should determine whether to attempt safe diversion of the aircraft.

The CDC describes the same decision from the cabin side: most commercial aircraft use ground-based medical services to assist with clinical assessment and make decisions regarding severity and flight diversion, and ultimately, the pilot and the ground-based emergency medical services contracted through the carrier have the responsibility to make a decision about diverting the flight. The pilot flies the plane and owns the final call. The ground doctor, usually reached by satellite phone or radio through a service the airline contracts, owns the medical recommendation. The volunteer in the cabin owns the examination. No one of the three decides alone.

What the decision weighs. The facts in the cabin are the inputs: whether you responded to glucagon, whether you are awake and stable or still slipping, how far the flight is from a suitable airport, what medical care that airport can offer, and how much time the flight has left. The CDC numbers from section 1 frame the odds: about 90 percent of in-flight medical emergencies end at the planned destination, and about 10 percent divert, most frequently for cardiac arrest, cardiac symptoms, obstetric or gynecologic issues, or a possible stroke. A severe low that responds to glucagon and leaves you awake and stable may stay on course with monitoring. One that does not respond, or that leaves you unconscious, is the kind of case where a diversion is weighed seriously. There is no rule that says a severe low always diverts, and no rule that says it never does. Prepare for either outcome: the flight may continue with you watched closely, or it may land early at an airport you did not plan to visit, with the ground service planning for your arrival.

8. Alone versus with a companion

The plan changes with one fact: who is sitting next to you. The severe low is the same either way, but the person who acts is different, and the setup has to match.

With a companion. The companion is the plan. Brief them before wheels-up, the way the glucagon guide describes: show them the actual device, walk through the printed instructions, and agree on where it will live, always the same spot, in the seat pocket. The briefing covers the what, the where, and the when: the device, the seat pocket, and the call for emergency help right after giving it. The companion also needs to know the sign to watch for, which is the same sentence you gave the crew: confusion, or not waking up. A companion who has rehearsed the steps once at home will do them under pressure on a plane. One who first sees the device during the emergency will not.

Alone. Flying alone does not mean facing a severe low alone, but it means the safety net has to be built from the cabin and from home instead of from the next seat. Tell the crew at boarding, using the alone version of the sentence from section 2. Tell the seatmate the one-line version too, and show them the seat pocket: a stranger who knows where the glucagon is can give it, and the nasal form needs no needle, which lowers the barrier for someone who has never given an injection. Set up the remote system from the solo travel playbook before departure: CGM sharing with a home contact who can see your numbers and call for help if you stop answering. The solo playbook covers that system in full, including the follower plan, the hotel-room rules, and the phone-as-lifeline setup. The time zone guide covers the other solo-flight variable, the insulin timing shift on long-haul routes, which is timing only, never dose amounts.

If you fly alone often, make the boarding sentence a habit. The same 30 seconds, the same sentence, every flight. Crew change, seatmates change, and routes change, but the sentence is the same, and it is the cheapest insurance on the plane. If a severe low ever comes, the people around you will already know the condition, the sign, the location, and the action. That is the whole plan, and it fits in one breath.

9. The pre-flight setup that makes all of this work

Everything above depends on five things being true before the plane leaves the ground. Check them the night before, not at the gate.

  • CGM low alarm loud, and the warning alarm on. Your driving floor is 90 mg/dL at home, and the flight floor is the same habit: keep the low alert loud so it wakes you and the people around you. The Dexcom G7 gives a 20-minute warning when your glucose is heading toward 55 mg/dL, through its Urgent Low Soon alert, and its Urgent Low alert at 55 mg/dL cannot be turned off. Use those alerts as the warning system, not as the plan: the plan is the sugar in your pocket, the glucagon in the seat pocket, and the people who know both.
  • Glucagon in the seat pocket, not the overhead bin. The glucagon guide puts it there for the same reason this guide does: the overhead bin is unreachable when it matters. A second device, if you carry one, rides in the same pocket or in the personal item under the seat, never in checked luggage.
  • Sugar packets in your pocket, not just in the bag. Pockets, a waist pack, the jacket you are wearing. The bag can be stowed, zipped, or out of reach. The sugar in your pocket cannot.
  • Medical ID, and the card. A medical ID or a lock-screen note that says type 1 diabetes, the glucagon location, and an emergency contact. The card from section 2 goes in the seat pocket with the glucagon.
  • Supplies with the 20 percent buffer, split between bags. Pack 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says, and split them so one lost bag never ends the trip. The packing list is the starting point, and the driving-abroad guide covers the same buffer rule for the rental car at the other end of the flight.
  • Questions for your care team: What is my severe-low plan on a plane, and who should give the glucagon if I am alone? Which glucagon product should I carry, and how should my companion practice with it? What CGM alert settings should I use for travel days? What should I do after a severe low mid-flight, and when should I seek hospital care after landing? Do my plans change if the flight is long-haul, overnight, or international?

If the setup feels like too much, fly with a companion on the long flights. The setup above is the solo version of the plan, and it works. But the simplest version of all is a companion who knows the device, the spot, and the sentence. For a holiday long-haul, that is worth more than any kit on the plane, because the kit belongs to a volunteer who may not be on board, and the companion is sitting next to you.

Flight booked? Pack the emergency kit first. The severe-low plan starts with glucagon, not with the plane. The glucagon guide covers the three products and the companion briefing, and the solo travel playbook covers the remote-companion system for flying alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can cabin crew give me glucagon if I have a severe low on a plane?

A: Do not plan on it. The FAA's guidance to airlines says flight attendants should not be expected to administer medications or to start IVs, and some airlines publish the rule plainly: ANA says cabin attendants are not permitted to perform any medical treatment, including administering injections, and Gulf Air says its cabin staff are trained in first aid only and are not permitted to administer any injection. Crew are trained in first aid, CPR, and the AED, and they will keep you comfortable, call for a medical volunteer, and talk to the cockpit. Your glucagon plan should name a companion or a seatmate who can give it, not the crew.

Q: What is in the plane's emergency medical kit for low blood sugar?

A: One item: a single-dose ampule of 50 percent dextrose, injectable, for IV use, with the needles and syringes to give it. The kit is required on US Part 121 airplanes for which a flight attendant is required, and it also holds a blood pressure cuff, a stethoscope, airways, a manual resuscitator, epinephrine, nitroglycerin, and other drugs. It holds no glucagon, no sugar packets, and no juice. The dextrose is a drug for a qualified medical volunteer to use, not a treatment the crew can hand you, so your own glucagon is still the diabetes emergency treatment on board.

Q: Will the plane divert if I have a severe low?

A: Not always, and no rule says it must. The pilot decides, with advice from the ground-based medical service the airline contracts and from any medical volunteer on board. The CDC reports that about 90 percent of in-flight medical emergencies end at the planned destination and about 10 percent divert, most often for cardiac arrest, cardiac symptoms, obstetric or gynecologic issues, or a possible stroke. A severe low that responds to glucagon and leaves you awake and stable may stay on course with monitoring; one that does not respond is the kind of case where a diversion is weighed. Prepare for either outcome.

Q: What should I tell the flight attendant when I board?

A: One short sentence, to the lead flight attendant: 'I have type 1 diabetes. If I seem confused or I will not wake up, my glucagon is in my seat pocket, and my companion knows how to use it.' If you are flying alone, end it with 'please call for medical help and ask if a doctor is on board.' You are generally not required to give the airline advance notice of a disability under the Air Carrier Access Act, so this is a practical heads-up, not a legal step. Keep a matching card in your seat pocket with your name, type 1 diabetes, the glucagon location, and an emergency contact.

Q: Can a doctor on board help me, and are they protected if they do?

A: Yes, and on US flights the law protects them. The Aviation Medical Assistance Act of 1998 says an individual shall not be liable for damages in a Federal or State court for providing or attempting to provide assistance in an in-flight medical emergency, unless they are guilty of gross negligence or willful misconduct. A medically qualified individual includes a physician, nurse, physician assistant, paramedic, or emergency medical technician. The crew will usually check the volunteer's credentials before handing over the medical kit. On international flights the US law might not apply, and the CDC notes that jurisdiction is not always clear, so the protection is strongest on US flights.

Q: What should I pack in my seat pocket for a low on a plane?

A: Sugar packets, your glucagon device, and your one-line card. Sugar packets are the fastest treatment for a low you can still treat yourself: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, then check again. Glucagon rides in the seat pocket, not the overhead bin, so a companion or seatmate can reach it when you cannot. The card says type 1 diabetes, where the glucagon is, and an emergency contact. Keep your phone charged with the CGM low alarm loud, and keep the rest of your supplies in your carry-on, never in checked luggage.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your severe-low plan, your glucose targets, and your treatment decisions are conversations with your care team, ideally at an appointment four to six weeks before your next trip. Airline policies, FAA rules, and kit contents change over time, so confirm the current details for your flight before you travel. If you have a severe low on a plane, tell the crew, get help from the people around you, and seek medical care after landing.
