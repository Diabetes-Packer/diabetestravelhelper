Theme Parks with Type 1 Diabetes: The Disney and Universal Playbook (2026)

Updated October 2026 · 9 min read

Orlando in December is the Super Bowl of family travel, and a theme park day is the trip type where every diabetes variable fires at once: Florida sun on your insulin, miles of walking, roller coasters tugging at your pump and sensor, and carb-heavy park food on a schedule set by ride queues instead of you. This playbook covers the four things that actually go wrong at theme parks, and the parks' real rules for each one.

Everything park-specific below is verified against the parks' published policies and device manufacturers' guidance, not traveler lore. The flight there is a separate battle, and it is covered in TSA Rules for Flying with Diabetes and your pat-down rights at the checkpoint.

The theme park day in five moves. 1. Ride with devices protected: unclip a tubed pump for coaster rides, keep pods and sensors on, check them after. 2. Beat the heat: shade the day's insulin, stash backups in First Aid's fridge. 3. Carry your lows kit in the bag: sugar packets, 1 at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. 4. Bring your own food: both parks allow it within their published rules. 5. Plan the walking: a park day is hours on your feet, so watch the CGM (continuous glucose monitor) and talk to your care team about an activity-day plan before the trip.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your care team owns the medical plan; this page owns the park plan. Park policies change, so confirm the current rules before you go.

Rides: what happens to your pump and CGM on a coaster

This is the number one question, and the honest answer is calmer than the worry. A 2007 study in Diabetes Care put people with type 1 diabetes on high-speed roller coasters and found that the severe, short-lived stress of the rides barely affected glucose control, even though heart rate and stress hormones spiked. The ride itself is not the enemy.

The device question is narrower and has a real answer. A peer-reviewed review of diabetes at amusement parks gives the one hard rule: disconnect an insulin pump for roller coasters that use electromagnets, because the magnetic fields can affect insulin delivery. Everything else is protection, not prohibition:

  • Tubed pump: unclip it for the ride and stow it in a zippered pocket or bag, then reconnect after. A two-minute ride is well within what your care team would plan for a brief disconnect, and it removes both the magnet question and the risk of a yanked infusion set.
  • Tubeless pod (Omnipod): you cannot take it off for a ride, and you do not need to. It stays on. The pod is rated IP28, waterproof to 25 feet for 60 minutes, so water rides are covered too. Check the pod's adhesive after big rides.
  • CGM: Dexcom says the G7 sensor is waterproof to 8 feet (2.4 meters) and can be submerged for up to 24 hours without failure when properly installed, so splash rides are fine. Two caveats: Bluetooth readings may pause while you are in or near water and resume when you get out, and the G7 receiver is only splash-resistant, not waterproof, so keep it in a dry pocket on water rides.
  • After the ride: restraints pressing on an arm-worn sensor can briefly skew readings. Glance at the sensor after a big coaster, and confirm a fingerstick before treating anything that looks off.

Heat: the variable that quietly changes your insulin

Central Florida sun is the park variable that actually moves blood sugar, and it works in two directions. Heat speeds up how fast injected insulin is absorbed, by up to 60 percent at 35C compared with 20C, which raises the risk of lows. At the same time, dehydration from hours in the sun makes glucose control worse. Add that heat can make low symptoms, like sweating and dizziness, harder to tell apart from plain heat exhaustion, and you have a day that demands checking instead of guessing.

The practical moves: keep the day's insulin shaded and off hot surfaces, never in a parked car or baking in direct sun. In-use insulin is designed to be carried at room temperature, so the day's supply does not need ice; the backups are what need the fridge. Every Walt Disney World theme park has a nurse-staffed First Aid station, and they will store prescriptions that require refrigeration for you, free, during park hours. They also provide sharps containers for used needles. Keep sugar packets in a zip pocket, 1 at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, and ask any quick-service counter for a free cup of ice water. The heat playbook in full lives in the all-inclusive resort guide, which covers the same Florida-level sun.

The walking: a park day is an endurance event

A theme park day means hours of walking with no real rest, and that matters because muscle contractions pull glucose into cells independently of insulin. The American Diabetes Association points to that mechanism as one reason physical activity improves glucose control, and recommends at least 150 minutes a week of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity for adults with diabetes. A full park day can blow past that in one afternoon.

That is mostly good news with one catch: the lows arrive quietly, mid-afternoon, right when you are deep in a queue and far from food. Watch the CGM trend through the day, keep fast carbs in the bag rather than the locker, and build real breaks into the plan: a sit-down lunch, an air-conditioned show, the parade. Before the trip, ask your care team about an activity-day plan, since insulin needs shift on heavy-walking days and that decision belongs to them, not to a blog post.

The food: what you can bring, and how to eat there

Both parks let you bring your own food, and the published rules are generous. Disney's policy: guests may bring outside food and nonalcoholic beverages into the park for self-consumption, as long as they are not in glass containers, do not require heating, reheating, processing, refrigeration or temperature control, and have no pungent odors. Tell a Security Cast Member about your food items when you enter. Universal Orlando's published policy allows bottled water, small snacks that do not require heating, any food necessary for medical purposes, any food needed for dietary needs, and baby food, in insulated bags no larger than 8.5 by 6 by 6 inches.

For park food itself, published carb counts are inconsistent across stands and seasonal items, so estimate and let the CGM guide the rest of the day rather than chasing perfect numbers at the register. Pre-bolus for meals the way you normally would; if you use a phone timer as a nudge, it is an optional crutch, not a required step. Where the park app lets you order ahead, use it to land meals on your schedule instead of a queue's.

The gate: security, DAS, and the line question

Park bag screening is routine for medical supplies. Disney uses electronic scanning machines at the gates; most medical devices are not a cause for additional screening, though it is possible, and medication that is clearly marked as prescribed with your name is generally permitted as long as it is not in a glass container. Mention medical supplies to the security team as you go through.

On the line question, know the current rule before you plan around it. Since May 2024, Disney's Disability Access Service (DAS) is intended only for guests who, due to a developmental disability like autism or similar, are unable to wait in a conventional queue for an extended period. Diabetes alone does not qualify for DAS. If you need to step out of a line for a low, tell a Cast Member; the current accommodation for guests who must leave a queue is a return-to-line option. Shorter practical wins: rope-drop the headliners, use single-rider lines where they exist, and schedule the longest waits around your meals.

The theme park checklist

Here is the full park-day routine, in this order:

  1. Pack 20% more supplies than the trip math says, and split them so one lost bag is never all of your supplies.
  2. Put sugar packets in a zip pocket you will actually reach: 1 at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. Add a backup stash in the park bag.
  3. At the gate, mention medical supplies to the security team and flag any outside food per the park's policy.
  4. Morning: drop backup insulin at First Aid for refrigerated storage; keep the day's supply shaded at room temperature.
  5. Before coaster rides, unclip a tubed pump into a zippered pocket; keep pods and sensors on and check them after.
  6. On water rides, keep the CGM receiver in a dry pocket; readings may pause in water and resume after.
  7. Through the day, watch the CGM trend as the walking adds up; hydrate with free ice water from quick-service counters.
  8. Estimate park-food carbs and pre-bolus as usual; confirm a fingerstick before treating any reading that looks off after a ride.
  9. If you must leave a line, tell a Cast Member and ask about the return-to-line option.
  10. After the park, check every device: adhesive, infusion set, sensor, and the day's supply levels for tomorrow.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I wear my insulin pump on roller coasters?

A: The peer-reviewed guidance is narrow: disconnect a pump for roller coasters that use electromagnets, because the magnetic fields can affect insulin delivery. For a tubed pump, unclip it into a zippered pocket for the ride and reconnect after. A tubeless pod stays on; check it after the ride. A 2007 Diabetes Care study found the stress of high-speed coasters barely affected glucose control in people with type 1 diabetes.

Q: Can I wear my CGM on water rides?

A: Yes. The Dexcom G7 sensor is rated waterproof to 8 feet (2.4 meters) and can be submerged for up to 24 hours without failure when properly installed. Bluetooth readings may pause while you are in the water and resume when you get out. The G7 receiver is only splash-resistant, so keep it in a dry pocket on splash rides. Omnipod pods are IP28 rated to 25 feet for 60 minutes.

Q: Will Disney store my insulin at the park?

A: Yes. Every Walt Disney World theme park has a nurse-staffed First Aid station, and they store prescriptions that require refrigeration. They also provide sharps containers for used needles. First Aid is open during regular park hours.

Q: Does diabetes qualify for Disney's Disability Access Service?

A: No, not on its own. Since May 2024, Disney's DAS is intended only for guests who, due to a developmental disability like autism or similar, are unable to wait in a conventional queue for an extended period. If you need to step out of a line, tell a Cast Member; the current accommodation is a return-to-line option.

Q: Can I bring my own food and low-blood-sugar supplies into Disney and Universal?

A: Yes. Disney allows outside food and nonalcoholic beverages for self-consumption, as long as they are not in glass containers, do not require heating, reheating, processing, refrigeration or temperature control, and have no pungent odors; tell a Security Cast Member about food items at entry. Universal Orlando allows bottled water, small snacks that do not require heating, and any food necessary for medical purposes or dietary needs.
