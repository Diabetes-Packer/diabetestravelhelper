Traveling with Diabetes in Mexico: The Pharmacy, the Food, and the Trip (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Winter is the season this guide is written for: beach trips to Cancun, Puerto Vallarta, and Los Cabos, and city trips to Mexico City and Oaxaca. It is also the second country on this site to get its own destination guide, after Japan, and that is not an accident. Mexico is one of the few places where the diabetes rules are genuinely different from the ones you already know: a pharmacy system that sells insulin over the counter, a food label system that warns you instead of informing you, four time zones where the clocks stopped moving in 2022, and heat that never really leaves the coast. None of it is hard. All of it is specific. This guide covers the import rules, the pharmacy, and the trip itself, in that order, so you can sort the paperwork at home and enjoy the trip once you land.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. This guide is not a trip report, and it does not pretend to be one. It is built from the published guidance of the United States Embassy in Mexico, the United States Department of State, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Mexico's Federal Commission for Protection against Health Risks (COFEPRIS), the agency that regulates medicine in Mexico, and Mexico's food labeling standard, NOM-051. Where a rule comes from one of those sources, this guide says so. Where something is my own travel habit, it is labeled as mine, and it is kept separate from the official rules. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own travel plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not going, tell your care team.

1. The import rule in one paragraph, then the details

Mexico does not run a certificate system like Japan's. There is no import form to file before you fly, and no office that stamps your insulin. The paperwork is a letter, and the rule is that the letter must match the bag. The United States Embassy in Mexico publishes the traveler rules in a short page called Bringing Items into Mexico, and the rules turn on the same two questions as everywhere else: what the medicine is, and how much of it you carry. For medicine for your own personal use, the answer is a prescription or a letter from your doctor, translated into Spanish, in your hand luggage, in the original containers. Our customs and border rules guide covers the general version of this system for any country, and our international travel guide covers the wide version of traveling abroad with diabetes. Mexico is where the general version gets specific.

The letter has a fixed shape. The Embassy says it should state the medicine, the amount you take each day, and the total amount you need for the trip, and it must carry your doctor's name, signature, contact information, and professional registration number. Get it translated into Spanish before you leave. The quantity you carry must match the length of the trip as described in the letter. There is no fixed cap in the Embassy's wording, so the letter is what sets the amount. Carry the prescription too, and keep every medicine in its original pharmacy container with the label on it, so an officer can match the medicine to the paper without a conversation. The United States Department of State makes the same point on its Mexico page: check the COFEPRIS list before you pack, because some medicines sold over the counter in some U.S. states are controlled substances in Mexico. Our travel letter guide covers what the letter should say, and our pre-trip appointment guide covers the full appointment, including asking for generic-name prescriptions before you leave. A prescription that lists the generic name is the one that travels. A brand name that only exists in your home country does not.

At the point of entry, report to customs and present the prescription, as the Embassy's page requires. If you take a controlled medicine, declare it, and check its status against the COFEPRIS list before you pack. Keep the letter and the prescription with the medicines in your hand luggage, not at the bottom of a checked bag, so you can produce them the moment you are asked. If the trip runs longer than the supply you can carry, our long-stay abroad playbook covers the planning for a trip that outlasts the supply in your bag.

2. Insulin at the border: pens, syringes, and what to carry

Insulin travels the same way it travels everywhere: in the bag that stays with you, in its original box with the pharmacy label on it. Syringes have one packing rule of their own. Keep the syringes with the injectable medicine they accompany, and make sure the doctor's letter mentions them. Do not plan to bring a loose syringe on its own. Pack the syringes with the insulin they belong to, and list them in the letter, so the letter and the bag agree with each other.

Two habits from my own travel routine, kept separate from the border rules. Everything diabetes-related rides in my carry-on, never in checked luggage, and I pack 20 percent extra of everything: pods, sensors, insulin, test strips, lancets, ketone strips, and the low kit. Our packing list does the counting for the trip length. The 20 percent buffer covers the pod that fails on a beach day and the sensor that peels off in the humidity. Bring backup insulin for injections in case a pod fails mid-trip, and keep your insulin plan written down somewhere that is not the pump. The flight there follows the same airport rules as any trip, covered in our TSA rules guide for the United States departure and our airport security abroad guide for the arrival side.

3. What is banned, and what needs a second look

Some medicines cannot come to Mexico at all, and no letter fixes that. Mexico bans the importation of products containing pseudoephedrine and codeine. That covers common cold and allergy products such as Sudafed, Actifed, and Vicks inhalers. A valid prescription from home does not change it. Check every medicine by its active ingredient, not its brand name, before you pack. The active ingredient is what the law sees. The brand name is not.

A second group is not banned, but it is not sold in Mexico either. Adderall, the mixed amphetamine salts used for ADHD, is not registered for sale in Mexico and cannot be legally imported, even with a United States prescription. ADHD in Mexico is treated with methylphenidate, which requires a special prescription and strict controls. If you take Adderall, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about what to do instead, because the answer cannot be found at the border. Cannabis is a separate problem. All forms of cannabis remain illegal to bring into Mexico, including products sold as medical marijuana or CBD, and there is no exception for travelers carrying products that are legal in their home state or country. Leave them at home.

Two more points for the medicine bag. If a medicine you take is not approved for sale in Mexico, you may need prior authorization from COFEPRIS, the Federal Commission for Protection against Health Risks, the agency that regulates medicine in Mexico. Start that process months before the trip, not weeks. And the controlled-substance rules keep tightening: tramadol was reclassified as a controlled medicine by COFEPRIS in July 2026, and it now requires a written prescription and official recordkeeping at the dispensing pharmacy. If you take a controlled medicine, check its status against the COFEPRIS list before you pack, and carry the prescription that proves it is yours.

4. The pharmacy: insulin over the counter, and the prescription that is not yours

Mexico's pharmacy system is regulated by COFEPRIS, the Federal Commission for Protection against Health Risks, the agency that does the job the Food and Drug Administration does in the United States. The pharmacy on the corner is called a farmacia, and the big ones are chains you will see in every city: Farmacias del Ahorro, Farmacias Guadalajara, Farmacias Similares, Farmacias Benavides, and Farmacia San Pablo. Pharmacy counters inside Walmart and Costco are reliable too. The system works well once you understand it, and the first thing to understand is that it is the opposite of Japan's in the way that matters most: insulin is sold over the counter, without a prescription.

That does not mean every insulin is on every shelf. The older human insulins are the ones most widely stocked: Humulin R and Novolin R are sold in Mexico under the same names, often called insulina regular. The analog insulins are sold in Mexico too. Lantus, which is insulin glargine, is listed by Mexican pharmacies, but stock varies by pharmacy and by city, so do not plan a trip around buying your exact analog at a given farmacia. Tell the pharmacist the generic name of your insulin, not just the brand: insulin aspart, insulin lispro, insulin glargine, insulin degludec. The pharmacist works from the generic name, and the generic name is what survives the brand change at the border. NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid almost everywhere else, and both are insulin aspart. Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine. Our finding insulin abroad playbook covers the name game in full, including the concentration check that matters more than the brand name.

Mexican insulin pens use the same U-100 standard, 100 units in each milliliter, so a pen dial counts the same way it does at home. If you buy a vial instead of a pen, check the concentration printed on the box before you use it, because a syringe is calibrated for one concentration only.

Two warnings before you walk in. First, the United States Department of State warns travelers to exercise caution when purchasing medication in Mexico: counterfeit pills are often advertised on social media and sold at small pharmacies near the border and in tourist areas, and United States citizens have become seriously ill or died after using synthetic drugs or contaminated prescription pills. Buy at the major chains, not at a small shop offering controlled medicines without questions. A pharmacy offering a controlled medicine without a prescription is operating outside the rules, and that is a sign to walk away. Second, a prescription from home cannot be relied on to be filled in Mexico. Mexican pharmacies require prescriptions written by Mexican-licensed physicians. The practical route is the walk-in clinic attached to most major pharmacy chains, where a doctor can see you and write a Mexican prescription, called a receta, on the spot. Bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product. Keep every receipt for your travel insurance claim, and check your policy before the trip so the clinic visit is not the first time you read it. Our travel insurance guide covers what to declare and what to look for.

One more rule that catches travelers by surprise, and it matters on a sick day. Antibiotics in Mexico require a prescription from a licensed Mexican doctor, and the pharmacy keeps the prescription. The rule dates to 2010, and enforcement at the major chains has grown considerably stricter in 2025 and 2026. A sick-day antibiotic is not a walk-in purchase. If you fall ill, the walk-in clinic attached to the pharmacy is the route to the prescription, and our sick-day travel playbook covers the ketone checks and the plan for that day.

5. Your devices in Mexico: the app, the sensors, and the heat

Start with the phone, because it holds the one trap that ruins more international trips than any other. The Dexcom G7 app is region-locked at install: Dexcom's own user guide states the app must be installed in your country of residence, and the app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when you first install it. The check happens at install time. An app that is already installed and working keeps working abroad, and travelers report normal operation on international trips as long as nobody deletes the app mid-trip. So install the G7 app and update it to the latest version before you leave, turn off auto-updates before the trip so nothing about your diabetes setup changes mid-trip without your say-so, and do not delete the app while you are in Mexico. A deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country, and that is a long trip without CGM data. Our G7 app region-lock guide covers the full prep list, and nothing in it is specific to Mexico: the same rule applies everywhere you land.

The receiver is the backup that makes the app problem a non-issue. Dexcom's own international travel guidance says that if you have a receiver, it makes a great backup to your phone app. The receiver has no app store, no region check, and no install step. It pairs with your sensor and shows readings anywhere on earth. Pack it with its USB cable and charger. Our devices charging guide covers plugs and power on the road. If you do not already own a receiver, ask Dexcom about getting one before a big international trip. Bring the fingerstick meter too, as the final fallback, and keep it in your day bag rather than buried in your hotel room.

Do not plan to buy sensors in Mexico. Pack more sensors than the trip math says you need, with the spares in your carry-on, and do not plan to shop. The same applies to pump supplies. Carry spare Pods and a backup injection plan with syringes and a vial, and keep your basal rates written down somewhere that is not the pump. Our pod failure backup plan covers the sequence when a Pod fails away from home.

Then there is the heat, which is the one place where your devices meet a real limit on a Mexico trip. Dexcom publishes exact temperature numbers for the G7. The sensor's operating range is 50F to 107.6F, and its storage range is 36F to 86F. A worn sensor sits against warm skin, so a normal day does not push it out of range, but a long day in direct sun and a hot car can push a spare past its storage ceiling. Keep sun exposure short where you can, and check the readings against your fingerstick meter after a long hot day. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. Heat loosens adhesive too. Press the sensor and pod edges down firmly after swimming, and pack extra overpatches. Our G7 heat and cold guide has the full temperature table, and our adhesion playbook covers keeping a sensor and a pump stuck through holiday trips.

6. Eating in Mexico: the label that warns you, and the plate that does not

Mexico is a country where the food label warns you and the plate does not, so the split is between packaged food and restaurant food, and the supermarket sits on the easier side. Under Mexico's food labeling standard, NOM-051, packaged food and non-alcoholic drinks carry two things. On the front, black octagonal seals warn when a product is over the limit for calories, sugars, saturated fat, trans fat, or sodium. Each seal reads EXCESO, followed by the nutrient. On the back or side, the nutrition table, headed información nutrimental, lists the nutrients per 100 grams or 100 milliliters and per serving, in Spanish. The carbohydrate line reads carbohidratos, or hidratos de carbono, and the number is in grams. The front seals will tell you a product is high in sugar. They will not give you a carb number. For that, find the table, find the carbohydrate line, and use the number on the package in front of you. This guide gives no carb counts for specific foods, because the number on the package in front of you is the one that counts, and it is printed right there.

Restaurant food has no label, so it works the way eating out works everywhere. Ask what is in the dish, estimate the carbohydrate from the rice, the beans, or the tortillas, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. I pre-bolus in my usual way before meals. That habit travels with me, and Mexico does not change it. A taco plate is mostly the tortillas and the rice, a plate with rice and beans is mostly the rice and the beans, and a soup with tortillas on the side is mostly the tortillas. Our eating out playbook covers the ordering and estimating routine in full, and our pre-bolus guide covers the timing habit. For lows, keep sugar packets in your day bag. I treat lows with sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, and that is the low kit that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day in any country. Do not let the low kit be something you buy after you need it. Pack it before you leave the hotel.

Water is the other half of eating safely in Mexico, and the CDC is blunt about it. The CDC's Yellow Book says tap water in Mexico is not safe to consume. Drink bottled or purified water only, and use it for brushing your teeth too. Ice is safe only if it was made from purified water, so ask at small stalls and street vendors. The CDC also says to avoid unpasteurized dairy products, particularly soft or fresh cheeses, raw or undercooked meat or fish, leafy greens, and raw fruit or vegetables, and to avoid swallowing water when swimming. Traveler's diarrhea frequently affects visitors to Mexico, and it typically develops after ingesting contaminated food or water. A stomach bug is a diabetes problem, not just a comfort problem, because vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the plan for that day.

Vaccines are a question for the care-team appointment, not for the airport. The CDC's Yellow Book says hepatitis A is endemic to Mexico, and visitors should receive at least one dose of the vaccine series before travel. Typhoid fever is endemic too, and vaccination is recommended, especially for those visiting smaller cities or rural areas. All travelers should be up to date on routine immunizations, and influenza circulates in Mexico from October to May, so the current season's flu vaccine belongs on the list for a winter trip. No vaccine is required to enter Mexico. Our travel vaccines playbook covers the appointment in full.

7. Time zones: four zones, and the clock that stopped moving

Mexico runs on four time zones, and since 2022 the clocks in most of the country have stopped moving. Mexico abolished daylight saving time on October 30, 2022, under a law called the Ley de los Husos Horarios, and most of Mexico now stays on standard time all year. The four zones are Centro, UTC-6, which covers Mexico City, Guadalajara, Puerto Vallarta, Oaxaca, and most of the country; Sureste, UTC-5, which covers Quintana Roo, including Cancun, Tulum, and Playa del Carmen; Pacífico, UTC-7, which covers Baja California Sur, Sinaloa, Sonora, and Nayarit; and Noroeste, UTC-8, which covers Baja California, including Tijuana. The exception is a set of municipalities right on the United States border, mostly in Baja California, Coahuila, Nuevo León, and Tamaulipas, which still change their clocks on the same dates as the United States. If your trip stays in Cancun, Mexico City, or Los Cabos, the clock will not move while you are there. The only thing that changes is the gap between Mexico and home, and that changes because home moves its clocks, not because Mexico does.

The gaps are small, and that is the good news. From the United States West Coast, Mexico City is 2 hours ahead in winter and 1 hour ahead in summer. Cancun matches New York in winter and sits 1 hour behind it in summer. Either way, the shift is small enough that the plan is simple, and it still needs to be made before the flight, not after landing.

The plan follows the rule in our time zone insulin guide. If you use a pump, change the pump clock to local time when you land, so your basal rates, the background insulin the pump drips all day, run on local time. Most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, so this is one manual step at the gate or the hotel. If you take multiple daily injections, keep your long-acting insulin on home time on the travel day, then switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone when you arrive, for example bedtime. Meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch: take it when you eat, wherever you are. Run your route through our time zone planner before you fly, and confirm the plan with your care team. The planner adjusts timing only, never dose amounts. Dose changes are a conversation with your care team, not something a tool or a blog post should decide.

8. Heat in Mexico: the coast, the capital, and the hotel fridge

Heat is the season this guide is written for, and it is the thing to plan around, because coastal Mexico is hot all year. In Cancun, daytime highs average 83F to 84F even in the coolest months, and reach 93F to 94F in the summer, on Mexico's own climate normals. That means in-use insulin, which must stay below 77F, will pass its limit in a beach bag in direct sun by mid-morning on an ordinary day. The fix is the same as it is everywhere: a FRIO cooling wallet or an insulated case with a gel pack in the day bag for the in-use insulin, and the unopened supply in the fridge, never in a hot car. Unopened insulin belongs in the fridge at 36 to 46F, and insulin freezes at 32F, and frozen insulin must be discarded. Hotel minibar fridges can freeze items at the back, so keep unopened insulin away from the cooling element and check that it has not frozen. Our insulin cooling guide covers the methods, and our hotel insulin storage guide covers the minibar trap in detail.

Mexico City is the exception that proves the rule. The capital sits at 2,250 meters, or 7,382 feet, above sea level, and it runs cooler than the coast all year. The heat plan still applies to the bag, because a warm hotel room and a day in the sun will still push in-use insulin past 77F, but the city itself will not do the work of a beach day. The weak links on any Mexico trip are the spares and the phone. Spare sensors must be stored between 36F and 86F, so do not leave them in a hot car or in direct sun. Keep them insulated in your carry-on, and let a hot spare cool indoors before you put it on. Phones give up in extreme heat long before the sensor does, so keep your phone in the shade and bring a portable charger, since heat drains batteries faster. The one heat habit that matters most is the simplest: keep the supplies on your body or in the bag that stays with you, not in a hot car, a hot room, or a checked bag.

9. If something goes wrong: numbers, clinics, and the insurance call

Save two numbers in your phone before you land. Dial 911 for police, ambulance, and fire. It is the single national emergency number, it works nationwide, and it is free from any phone. If you are driving, save 078 too. That is the number for the Angeles Verdes, the Green Angels, the tourism ministry's free roadside patrol on federal highways. They help with breakdowns, flat tires, fuel, and first aid, and they can point you to help in English. They are not paramedics and they are not a substitute for 911 in a life-threatening emergency, so call 911 first when the emergency is medical. Our driving abroad guide covers licenses, rental cars, and the rules of the road, and it is the right companion to this section if the trip involves driving.

If you develop symptoms of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous complication where the blood turns acidic when insulin runs too low, get urgent help right away. Vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation, and that is a hospital visit, not a wait-and-see day. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the ketone checks and the plan for that day. Carry glucagon in your day bag, and make sure your travel companion knows where it is and how to use it. Our glucagon emergency kit guide covers what is in the kit and how to keep it travel-ready. If the trip goes wrong in a bigger way, a bag lost or stolen, our lost or stolen supplies playbook covers the exact call order, and the travel insurance emergency line is the call that arranges care, including a local doctor visit. Check your policy before the trip, so the call is not the first time you read it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do I need a prescription or a doctor's letter to bring insulin into Mexico?

A: Yes, carry both. The United States Embassy in Mexico says travelers should carry a medical prescription or a letter from their doctor stating the medicine, the amount taken each day, and the total amount needed for the trip, and the prescription or letter must be translated into Spanish. Keep every medicine in its original container with the pharmacy label, and carry it in your hand luggage. The quantity you carry must match the length of the trip as described in the letter. Keep the syringes with the insulin they accompany, and list them in the letter.

Q: Can I buy insulin at a pharmacy in Mexico without a prescription?

A: Yes. Insulin is sold over the counter at Mexican pharmacies, without a prescription. The older human insulins, Humulin R and Novolin R, are sold under the same names. Analog insulins such as Lantus, which is insulin glargine, are sold in Mexico too, but stock varies by pharmacy and by city, so do not plan a trip around buying your exact analog at a given farmacia. Mexican insulin pens use the U-100 standard, 100 units in each milliliter, so check the concentration printed on the box before you use a vial.

Q: Can a Mexican pharmacy fill my prescription from home?

A: Do not count on it. Mexican pharmacies require prescriptions written by Mexican-licensed physicians. If you run out of insulin in Mexico, see a doctor at the walk-in clinic attached to most major pharmacy chains. Bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the Mexican prescription, called a receta, to the pharmacy.

Q: What medicines are banned in Mexico?

A: Mexico bans the importation of products containing pseudoephedrine and codeine, including common cold and allergy products such as Sudafed, Actifed, and Vicks inhalers, and a valid prescription from home does not change that. Adderall is not registered for sale in Mexico and cannot be legally imported, even with a United States prescription. All forms of cannabis, including products sold as medical marijuana or CBD, are illegal to bring into Mexico. Check every medicine by its active ingredient, not its brand name, before you pack.

Q: Will my Dexcom G7 app work in Mexico?

A: Yes, if it was already installed and set up at home. The Dexcom G7 app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when it is first installed, so install and update it before you leave and do not delete it while you are abroad: a deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country. Bring the Dexcom receiver as backup. It has no app store and no install check, and it pairs with your sensor anywhere.

Q: What are the emergency numbers in Mexico?

A: Dial 911 for police, ambulance, and fire. It is the single national emergency number, it works nationwide, and it is free from any phone. If you are driving, dial 078 for the Angeles Verdes, the Green Angels, the tourism ministry's free roadside patrol on federal highways. They help with breakdowns and first aid, but they are not a substitute for 911 in a life-threatening emergency.
