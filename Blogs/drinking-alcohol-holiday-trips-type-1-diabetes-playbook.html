Drinking on Holiday Trips with Type 1 Diabetes: The Alcohol Travel Playbook (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 10 min read

Thanksgiving is 23 days away. After it comes Christmas, then New Year's Eve, and November is Diabetes Awareness Month, which makes this the right week to settle one question before the drinks season starts: what is your alcohol plan on a trip? This guide is not a generic drinking guide. It is the travel version, for the nights when you are away from home, in a hotel room, surrounded by people who may not know your low signs, and facing a low that may not arrive until hours after the last drink, while you are asleep.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes (T1D) for 20 years and I fly with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets. This guide does not invent a drinking story for me, and it does not tell you what dose to take or what setting to change. Those decisions belong to you and your care team, and section 8 lists exactly what to ask them before the trip.

Talk to your care team before the trip about your own alcohol plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any insulin dose, any pump setting, or any treatment decision. If you are unsure whether drinking fits your current control, or what your plan should be on a drinking night, ask your care team before you travel, not after the first drink. If a low after drinking is severe, get urgent help right away, using the steps in section 6.

Alcohol already has a place in several guides on this site, in small sections: the restaurant playbook covers drinks at the table, the Christmas markets playbook covers mulled wine, the Halloween playbook covers party nights, the resort playbook covers the swim-up bar, and the Thanksgiving playbook covers the family table. This guide is the deep version those sections point to. Read it once before the season, then use the short checklists in sections 4 and 5 on the night itself.

1. Why alcohol is a travel problem, not just a drinking problem

At home, drinking happens inside a routine you know: your own glasses, your usual food, your own bed, and people who have seen your lows before. A trip removes all four at once, and that is what makes alcohol a travel problem specifically.

  • Unfamiliar drinks and hidden carbs. A new bar, a host pouring freely, a cocktail you have never ordered, a sweet wine at a market stall: you do not always know what is in the glass. Section 3 sorts drinks into two groups so you can ask the right question before you drink, not after.
  • The low arrives late. Alcohol can cause lows for up to 12 to 24 hours after drinking, usually overnight and the following morning. You can feel fine at midnight and still go low at 3 in the morning in a hotel room, far from your usual safety net.
  • Alcohol masks low symptoms. The signs of being drunk look like the signs of a low: slurred speech, confusion, drowsiness, and unsteadiness. You may miss your own low, and the people around you may read it as just too much to drink. That delay is the danger, because a low caught early is a snack, and a low caught late is an emergency.
  • Judgment about treating a low gets worse exactly when it matters. After drinking, you are less likely to check, less likely to act on a falling reading, and more likely to decide you will deal with it in the morning. The plan in this guide is built for that version of you: decisions made before the evening, sugar already by the bed, and another person already briefed.
  • Glucagon may work less well after drinking. Glucagon is the emergency hormone that tells the liver to release stored glucose. While the liver is busy processing alcohol, it releases less glucose, so glucagon has less to work with. Section 6 explains what that means in practice: prevention first, and emergency services early, not as a last resort.
  • The room, not the bar, is where the risk lands. The dangerous hours are the sleeping hours. A hotel room has no family member down the hall who knows your routine, unless you put one there on purpose. Section 5 is the overnight plan that closes that gap.

2. What alcohol does to blood sugar, in plain words

Your liver is your backup battery. Between meals and overnight, it releases stored glucose into your blood to keep your sugar steady. The stored form is called glycogen. Think of glycogen as glucose packed away on a shelf, ready to be released when levels drop.

When you drink, the liver stops that job and does a different one first: breaking down the alcohol. While it is processing alcohol, it cannot make and release glucose the way it normally would. That is the whole mechanism, and it explains every rule that follows.

Two more pieces stack on top of it on a trip. First, insulin on board: insulin from an earlier dose that is still working in your body. Insulin lowers blood sugar, and it keeps working whether the liver is releasing glucose or not. So the danger window is insulin still working plus a liver that is busy with alcohol and not releasing glucose. Second, activity: dancing, walking home, a long day on your feet. Activity also lowers blood sugar, and a hospital diabetes guide for type 1 diabetes names dancing and walking home as activities that increase hypo risk after drinking. A hypo is a low blood sugar episode, and in the United States a low is a reading below 70 mg/dL.

Put together, the shape of the night is this. A drink that contains carbohydrate can push glucose up at first. Then, hours later, the alcohol effect takes over and glucose can fall, sometimes while you are asleep. A drink with little or no carbohydrate does not give that first rise, but it still carries the later fall. Either way, the risk does not end when the evening ends. It runs overnight and into the next morning, which is why the checks in this guide run that long too.

The American Diabetes Association (ADA) Standards of Care in Diabetes 2026 says the same thing in guideline form. Recommendation 5.18 says adults with diabetes who drink should not exceed the recommended daily limits, and people who do not drink should not start drinking, even in moderation. Recommendation 5.19 says people with diabetes should be counseled about delayed hypoglycemia, the medical word for a delayed low, and about the importance of monitoring glucose after drinking alcohol, especially when using insulin. The ADA alcohol text names hypoglycemia and delayed hypoglycemia as risks, particularly for people using insulin, and says people with diabetes should be encouraged to monitor glucose frequently before and after drinking. It also quotes the World Health Organization position that there is no safe amount of alcohol intake, and says the long-term effects of alcohol for people with diabetes are unknown. This guide takes no position beyond that: if you drink on a trip, drink with a plan, and if you do not drink, nothing here is a reason to start.

3. Know the drink: carbs, limits, and pace

Start with the glass, because the glass decides the first half of the night. A hospital diabetes guide for type 1 diabetes sorts drinks into two groups, and that sorting is the most useful thing in this section.

  • Drinks that contain carbohydrate and can raise glucose at first: lagers, ales, and stouts; ciders and fruit ciders; alcopops; non-diet mixers, including fruit juice; Irish Cream; and sweet wines. In large amounts, these also increase the risk of delayed lows later.
  • Drinks with little or no carbohydrate and no first rise: spirits; red and white wine; and diet (sugar free) mixers. These still carry the later low risk, and in large amounts they also increase the risk of delayed lows.

Two practical consequences follow. First, if you mix spirits, choose a sugar-free mixer, because a sugary mixer turns a no-carb drink into a carb drink. Second, never trust a drink you did not see made: a busy bartender can hand you a regular mixer when you asked for a diet one, and a host can top up a glass without you noticing. Ask, check, and count the glass as a drink even if you did not finish it in one sitting.

Pour size matters as much as drink choice, and it varies. A hospital guide for type 1 diabetes notes that a typical pint of beer or lager contains around one to two alcohol units, and a glass of wine can be between one and a half to three units depending on the strength and the size of the glass. A UK alcohol unit is 10 ml or 8 g of pure alcohol. On a trip, a generous host pour or a large restaurant glass can be a bigger drink than the one you counted at home, so count glasses, not assumptions.

On limits, use the guideline for the country you are in, and ask your care team which one applies to you. In the United States, the ADA 2026 text follows the Dietary Guidelines for Americans: limit intake to no more than 2 drinks a day for men and no more than 1 drink a day for women, where one drink equals a 12-ounce beer, a 5-ounce glass of wine, or 1.5 ounces of distilled spirits. In the United Kingdom, the hospital guidance is to keep health risks low by drinking no more than 14 units per week, spread over three days or more, with several alcohol-free days each week. Both are ceilings, not targets, and neither is a personal prescription. Your care team may advise less, or none, for your own situation.

Pace is the last part of knowing the drink. Alternate alcoholic drinks with water or a non-alcoholic drink, eat while you drink, and decide your limit before the evening starts. The ADA says to monitor glucose frequently before and after drinking, and a hospital guide says to monitor regularly while drinking and for the next 24 hours. Checking is not a chore added to the night. It is the only way to see the late fall coming, because the fall often arrives without the warning you expect.

4. The drinking-night rules

Run this list before the first drink, not after the third. Every item is a decision made early, so the later, tireder version of you does not have to make it.

  1. Eat food with drinks, and never drink on an empty stomach. The CDC says eating when you drink alcohol can help avoid a nighttime low, and a hospital diabetes leaflet says the same in blunter words: never drink on an empty stomach, and eat a meal before drinking. On a trip, that means a real meal, not a handful of bar snacks, and it means eating even when the evening runs late and the kitchen is closing.
  2. Check before, during, and after. Check before the first drink, check during the evening, and check before bed. The ADA says to monitor glucose frequently before and after drinking. If your CGM is your check, keep the phone charged and nearby, because a dead phone ends the monitoring.
  3. Keep fast sugar on your person, not only in the bag. My own low treatment is sugar packets: one packet when I am at 60 to 70, two packets below 60, then I check again. Keep packets in a pocket for the walk home, the taxi queue, and the hotel corridor, because the bag may be in the room, in the car, or with someone else when the low hits. The low snacks ranking compares the travel options.
  4. Brief one companion, by name, before the evening. That person should know you have type 1 diabetes, what your low looks like, where your fast sugar is, where your glucagon is, and that a low can look like drunkenness. A hospital guide says to always go out with someone who knows you have diabetes, and to carry diabetes identification, just in case your low is mistaken for too much alcohol. Carry the identification too: a card or a medical ID on your phone.
  5. Know where the glucagon is, and make sure someone else knows too. Glucagon may work less well after drinking, which makes the companion and the emergency call more important, not less. The glucagon emergency kit guide covers what to pack and how to brief a companion before the trip.
  6. Do not drive after drinking, and do not swim after drinking. A hospital diabetes leaflet states the driving rule without qualification: never drink and drive. Plan the ride home before the evening starts, and leave swimming for a sober, steady time, not for the walk back from the bar. Both are judgment calls that alcohol makes worse, and neither can be fixed after the fact.
  7. Do not drink alone on a trip. If you are traveling solo, the companion rule still applies: tell someone where you are going, share your CGM if you use sharing, and make sure hotel staff or a friend knows how to reach you. The solo safety playbook covers the wider version of this plan.
  8. Decide the end of the evening in advance. A fixed end time, a ride already booked, and a bedtime check already planned beat willpower at midnight. The holiday travel playbook covers the travel days around these evenings.

5. The overnight plan: the hotel room hours

The evening ends, but the risk does not. This is the section to set up before you go out, because you will not set it up well when you get back tired.

  1. Put fast sugar by the bed before you leave. A hospital guide says to always have hypo treatment with you, and to check that you also have hypo treatment by the bed before you go to sleep. My packets go on the nightstand, not in the suitcase, not in the bathroom bag.
  2. Keep CGM alarms loud overnight, and set the low alert to catch a fall early. A hospital guide says to turn on CGM alarms and ask a friend to follow, and says you can set the low alert at a higher glucose level to alert earlier if glucose is dropping. The CDC says the same about the night: you may wake up with a low, but you should not rely on that, and a CGM can alert you while you sleep. Alcohol can make you sleep very deeply, and a hospital guide warns you may not wake up if you have a low, so loud alarms are not optional on a drinking night.
  3. Ask someone to check on you overnight. A hospital guide says to get someone to check on you while you are asleep, at least once in the middle of the night and in the morning if you are sleeping in late. If you are traveling with a companion, agree this before the evening. If you are alone, use CGM sharing with someone who will actually respond to an alarm, and tell them that a drinking night is a night to watch.
  4. Have a snack before bed, on your own care-team plan. The CDC says that if you think you are at risk of a low overnight, a snack before bed can help, and a hospital leaflet says to have a snack when you get home. What that snack is, and whether it fits your insulin plan, is a care-team question to settle before the trip, not a guess at midnight. Section 8 lists it as a question to ask.
  5. Keep the room ready for a low. Phone charged and within reach, fast sugar on the nightstand, glucagon where your companion can find it in the dark, and the hotel room number known to the person checking on you. The hotel storage guide covers the room setup for insulin, and the same room discipline applies here.

6. A low after drinking: what to do, and when to get urgent help

If you can swallow safely, treat the low with fast sugar right away, using your own care-team plan, then check again. My own treatment is sugar packets: one packet at 60 to 70, two packets below 60. Do not wait to see if it passes, and do not decide the low is just tiredness or just the drink. If the first treatment does not bring you back up, treat again on your plan and keep checking. If you are unsure what your plan says, call your care team or local emergency services rather than guessing.

A low after drinking is harder to recover from than the same low sober, because the liver is busy with alcohol and glucagon works less well. That is why the steps below are more urgent than the usual low routine, not less.

Get urgent help right away if any of these are true:

  • You cannot swallow safely, you are unconscious, or you are not responding normally.
  • You have had a seizure, or you cannot stay awake.
  • You are vomiting, especially if you cannot keep fluids down.
  • You have ketones, or signs that point that way: fruity smelling breath, trouble breathing, or deep and rapid breathing.
  • You are ill as well as low, or the low keeps coming back after treatment.

For a person who cannot swallow or is unconscious, do not put food or drink in the mouth, because they could choke. Put the person in the recovery position, on their side, and give glucagon straight away if one is available and you know how to use it. If the person starts to recover within 10 minutes of glucagon and can swallow safely, give food or drink that will raise blood sugar. Call emergency services (911 in the United States, 999 in the United Kingdom, 112 in much of Europe) if a glucagon injection is not available or you do not know how to use it, if you have given glucagon and the person has not recovered within 10 minutes, or if the person has been drinking alcohol. That last point comes straight from NHS guidance: a severe low plus drinking alcohol is itself a reason to call, even before you judge how bad the low looks.

Ketones deserve their own note, because they change the picture. Ketones are acids that build up in the blood when the body does not have enough insulin, and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is the dangerous condition that follows when ketones build up and blood sugar runs very high. A hospital sick-day guide lists vomiting and fruity smelling breath among the symptoms of DKA, and says that if you have those symptoms, test your ketones and call your doctor or go to the nearest hospital emergency center, because this can quickly become serious. On a trip, vomiting after drinking is easy to dismiss as just the drink. Do not dismiss it: check ketones if you have a way to check, and get help. The sick-day travel playbook covers the wider sick-on-a-trip plan.

7. The next morning: the plan most people skip

The morning after is still part of the drinking night. The risk runs up to 12 to 24 hours after drinking, and a hospital leaflet warns that the risk continues well into the next morning. So the morning has its own short list:

  1. Check as soon as you wake, before breakfast decisions. Do not assume a good bedtime reading means a safe morning.
  2. Do not skip breakfast. A hospital leaflet says to try not to miss breakfast the next day and to keep snacks handy. Eat, even if you feel rough, and keep fast sugar with you for the rest of the morning.
  3. Keep checking through the morning. A hospital guide says to monitor regularly while drinking and for the next 24 hours. The morning checks are the tail end of that window, not extra caution.
  4. Do not drive until you are sober, steady, and sure your glucose is safe. Morning-after impairment and a morning low are a bad combination for a rental car or a long drive home. If in doubt, delay the drive.
  5. Tell your companion how the night went. If you went low overnight, that changes the next evening's plan, and it is worth telling your care team too. One bad night is information, not a failure, and it should change the plan before the next drink, not after.

8. Before the trip: the care-team conversation and the packing list

Book this conversation before the trip, not during it. The pre-trip appointment guide covers the full appointment, and these are the alcohol questions to add to it:

  • Does drinking fit my current control, and is there a limit you want me to stay under on this trip?
  • What should my bedtime check and bedtime snack plan be on drinking nights, and how does it fit my insulin plan?
  • Should any pump or insulin setting change on drinking nights, and who decides that change? Do not change a setting on the basis of this guide.
  • What should my companion do for a severe low, and what should they do differently because I have been drinking?
  • What is my ketone checking plan if I am vomiting or ill after drinking, and when do I go to emergency care rather than waiting?
  • Is there anything about this destination that changes the plan: different drink measures, different emergency numbers, or a hotel without someone nearby overnight?

Packing for drinking nights is small, and it rides with the rest of the diabetes kit, counted with the 20% extra buffer this site uses on every trip:

  • Sugar packets in a pocket and by the bed, plus spares in the bag that stays with you.
  • Glucagon, with a companion who knows where it is and how to use it.
  • A blood glucose meter and strips, for the moments when CGM readings do not match how you feel.
  • Diabetes identification: a card or a medical ID on your phone, with emergency contact details.
  • Ketone checking supplies, if your care team has you check ketones.
  • A charged phone and a charger, so CGM alarms and CGM sharing keep working overnight.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Why is alcohol riskier on a trip than at home?

A: A trip changes every variable at once: unfamiliar drinks and pour sizes, food at odd hours, more walking and dancing, a strange bed, and a night when nobody around you knows your low signs. Alcohol also hides its main risk until later. The low it causes can arrive hours after drinking, often overnight in a hotel room, when you are asleep and least able to catch it.

Q: How long after drinking can a low arrive?

A: Alcohol of all types can cause lows for up to 12 to 24 hours after drinking, usually overnight and the following morning. That is why the plan in this guide does not end when the evening ends: check before bed, keep fast sugar by the bed, keep the CGM alarms loud overnight, and keep checking the next morning.

Q: Does glucagon still work after drinking?

A: It may work less well. Glucagon raises blood sugar by telling the liver to release its stored glucose, but while the liver is busy processing alcohol it releases less glucose, so glucagon has less to work with. That is one more reason the prevention steps matter: eat with drinks, do not drink alone on a trip, and make sure someone with you knows the low plan and where the glucagon is.

Q: What should I do for a low after drinking?

A: If you can swallow safely, treat the low with fast sugar right away, using your own care-team plan, then check again. My own low treatment is sugar packets. If you cannot swallow, are unconscious, or are not responding normally, do not put food or drink in the mouth. Put the person in the recovery position, give glucagon straight away if one is available and you know how to use it, and call emergency services. If the person has been drinking alcohol, that alone is a reason to call, even before you judge how bad the low looks.

Q: What do I ask my care team about alcohol before a trip?

A: Ask about your own drinking plan for the trip: whether drinking fits your current control, what your bedtime check and bedtime snack plan should be on drinking nights, what pump or insulin settings if any should change and who decides that, and what your companion should do for a severe low. Bring the trip dates and the kind of evenings you expect, because a quiet dinner and a late party night are different plans.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your alcohol plan, your insulin plan, your pump settings, and your treatment decisions are conversations with your care team, ideally at an appointment four to six weeks before your next trip. Guidelines, limits, and emergency numbers differ by country, so confirm the current details for your destination before you travel. If a low after drinking is severe, or you are vomiting, ill, or have ketones, get urgent help right away.
