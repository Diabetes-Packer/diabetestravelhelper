Travel Anxiety with Type 1 Diabetes: The First-Flight Playbook (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Here is the fear nobody puts on a packing list: the dread in the weeks before a first flight with type 1 diabetes, or the first flight in years. It is not a fear of flying. It is a fear of going low at 35,000 feet with nowhere to go, of a security officer who does not understand the pump on your hip, of a bag that never arrives in a city where you cannot replace what is in it. That fear is normal, it is common, and it has a fix. The fix is not willpower. It is preparation, done in the right order, until every fear has a written answer. This guide is that order. Thanksgiving falls on Thursday, November 26, and if your first flight in years is the Thanksgiving flight home, you are not the only one boarding with this fear.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. This guide does not describe my own first flight, and it does not claim I have felt this fear. The fear in it comes from other travelers who have said it out loud, and they are named wherever their words appear. Every number below comes from a published study, the American Diabetes Association (ADA), the Transportation Security Administration (TSA), or the travelers themselves, and every place where the evidence runs out is marked as such. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next flight about your own plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If anxiety is changing how you manage your diabetes, or stopping you from traveling at all, tell your care team. The ADA recommends screening for fear of hypoglycemia and referring people who have it to mental health providers, and that conversation is part of diabetes care, not separate from it.

The post that started this guide was a short thread in r/Type1Diabetes at the end of August 2026. A traveler in Germany was planning a first vacation in five or six years, a flight to the Canary Islands, and was anxious about flying with an Omnipod 5 and a Dexcom G6. That is the whole story: no crisis, no bad flight, just the fear before the first one in years. If that is your trip, this guide is written for you. The holiday playbook covers the trip itself, and the Thanksgiving day-of playbook covers the meal at the end of it. Read this guide for the fear before the flight, and keep the others for the rest of the trip.

1. What travel anxiety with type 1 diabetes actually is

Start with the name the research gives it. Fear of hypoglycemia is a specific and extreme fear of going low, or of the risk of going low. It is not a vague worry. It has two parts: the worry itself, and the behavior that follows from it, like keeping glucose high on purpose, snacking more than needed, or avoiding the situations where a low might happen. A flight stacks all three triggers at once: an unfamiliar place, a long stretch where you cannot leave your seat, and a low that would be hard to treat in front of strangers.

How common is it? A 2023 study in BMJ Open Diabetes Research and Care screened 553 adults with type 1 diabetes at three T1D Exchange clinics in California, Colorado, and New York, using a nine-item fear of hypoglycemia screener. Thirty percent reported a high fear of hypoglycemia score. The people with high fear also had higher A1C, the three-month average blood sugar measure, more severe low episodes in the past year, and more impaired awareness of lows, meaning they were less able to feel a low coming. High fear was also tied to higher scores on standard anxiety and depression measures. The study authors are plain about what follows from that: fear of hypoglycemia is common in type 1 diabetes, and it affects quality of life, emotional well-being, and diabetes management.

That last line is the one to sit with. The fear does not just feel bad. It changes what people do. And the ADA's psychosocial care guidance answers it directly: screen for fear of hypoglycemia with standardized and validated tools, and refer adults who have it to mental health providers. That is not a footnote. It means the fear is a recognized part of diabetes care, with a named screening step and a named referral, the same as any other complication. If your fear is constant, if it is changing how you manage your diabetes, or if it is the reason you have not booked the flight, that is the conversation to have with your care team, and it belongs in the appointment, not after it.

One more distinction before the playbook. The fear in this guide is the fear of a low, a checkpoint, and a lost bag. Those are practical fears with practical answers, and sections 2 through 5 give them. If your fear is bigger than that, if it is panic at the thought of boarding, or dread that does not lift after the prep is done, the playbook still helps, but it is not the whole answer. The whole answer includes your care team, and section 6 says when to bring them in.

2. The three fears, named

Anxiety shrinks when it gets a name and a fact. Almost every version of travel anxiety with type 1 diabetes is one of three fears, and each one has a written answer.

Fear one: going low with nowhere to go. This is the fear underneath the others. A low on a plane feels different from a low at home because you cannot walk to the kitchen, you cannot pull over, and the people around you are strangers. Here is the fact that answers it. A low on a plane is treated the same way as a low anywhere else, from the kit at your seat, and the crew is part of the plan. Tell the flight attendant you have diabetes, especially if you are traveling alone. That is the advice Beyond Type 1's Tiana Cooks gives, and it is the simplest version of the rule: the crew cannot help with what they do not know. Keep the low kit in your pocket or in the bag under the seat in front of you, never in the overhead bin. And if the low is severe and you cannot treat yourself, the crew has an emergency medical kit and a plan for it. Our severe-low playbook covers what cabin crew can and cannot do, what is in the plane's medical kit, and how the captain decides on a diversion, so the worst case has a written answer too.

Fear two: the checkpoint. The airport security line is where the fear gets loudest, because it is the one part of the trip you cannot control. You do not know which scanner they will use, whether the officer will understand the pump, or whether your bag will be opened in front of a line of strangers. Here is the fact that answers it. The rules are written down, and they are on your side. The ADA's flying guidance says the Transportation Security Administration (TSA) allows all diabetes supplies through the checkpoint after screening or a pat-down, and that airlines must allow diabetes supplies to stay with you on the plane under the Air Carrier Access Act. You can ask for a pat-down instead of the body scanner, and you can ask for a private screening with a witness of your choice. You can ask for a visual inspection of your supplies instead of the x-ray, if you ask before they go on the belt. The officers see travelers with diabetes every day. Ali Runs on Insulin, writing about her first flight after diagnosis, puts it plainly: airport security sees travelers with diabetes every day, and staying calm and communicating clearly makes the process much easier. Our TSA rules guide, our pat-down rights guide, and our scanner guide cover the whole checkpoint in detail, and our first-hand airport security guide covers what it actually feels like.

Fear three: something breaking or running out far from home. A pod fails, a sensor peels off, a bag goes missing, and you are in a city where you do not know a pharmacy. Here is the fact that answers it. Insulin is available almost everywhere, though brand names differ by country, and the fix for a failure is a spare, not a prayer. Breakthrough T1D UK, the charity formerly known as JDRF in the UK, says to pack at least twice as much as you expect to use, to divide supplies between bags in case one is stolen or lost, and to check where the nearest pharmacy or hospital is at your destination before you leave. Our finding insulin abroad playbook covers the brand-name equivalents and the pharmacy rules by region, and our lost supplies playbook covers the exact call order when the bag is gone. The fear is real. The answer is a spare and an address.

3. The prep that shrinks the fear

Preparation is the treatment for this kind of anxiety, because every step you finish before the trip is one less unknown on the day. Do it in this order, starting weeks out.

Four to six weeks before: the appointment. Book the pre-trip appointment with your care team and bring the itinerary. That visit is where the travel letter, the generic-name prescriptions, the time zone plan, the sick-day plan, and the backup plan for a failed pod all come from. Our pre-trip appointment guide lists the questions to ask and what to leave with. If fear of hypoglycemia is part of why you are anxious, say so at this visit. That is the screening conversation the ADA recommends, and it is easier to have it six weeks out than at the gate.

Packing: count it, then add more. This site's rule is to pack 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says. Experienced travelers pack more than that, and their numbers are worth hearing because they come from the failures. Ali Runs on Insulin packs at least twice the supplies she expects to use, because pump sites fail, sensors peel off, flights get canceled, and vacations get extended. Tiana Cooks at Beyond Type 1 packs three times what she needs: a seven-day trip gets 21 days of supplies. Breakthrough T1D UK says at least twice as much. The spread tells you the rule: the floor is 20 percent extra, and the ceiling is whatever lets you stop worrying about running out. Our packing list and our how much insulin to pack guide do the counting for you, and the packing calculator on this site builds the list for your exact trip length. Divide the supplies between bags, as Breakthrough T1D UK advises, so one lost bag cannot take everything. And pack the bag the night before, not the morning of. The morning of a flight is the worst time to discover you are short a sensor.

Know the rules before you meet them. Read the TSA rules guide before the trip, not at the checkpoint. Carry a travel letter from your care team listing your treatments and devices, plus a copy of your latest prescription, in case you need emergency supplies while away. That is Breakthrough T1D UK's advice, and the travel letter guide covers what the letter should say. Wear a medical ID, which Beyond Type 1, T1D Exchange, and Ali Runs on Insulin all name as the one item that speaks for you if you cannot speak for yourself. If you want a person waiting for you at the checkpoint, call TSA Cares at 1-855-787-2227 within 72 hours of your flight. The ADA's flying guidance gives the hours: Monday to Friday, 8:00 a.m. to 11:00 p.m. Eastern Time, and weekends and holidays, 9:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. Eastern Time. More than 2,600 officers serve as passenger support specialists, and TSA tries to have one available during all checkpoint operating hours. You can also carry the TSA Disability Notification Card, which discreetly tells the officer you have a medical condition or device. It does not exempt you from screening, but it starts the conversation without you having to explain yourself out loud.

Set up the safety net at home. Before you fly, set up glucose sharing, so someone at home can see your numbers while you travel. That is the remote companion for a solo trip: a parent, a partner, or a friend who gets the alert if you go low and cannot answer. Our CGM sharing playbook covers the setup, the airplane mode trap that pauses sharing, and the rule that both phones have to be online. If you are flying alone, our solo travel playbook covers the rest of the plan that works when nobody is sitting beside you.

Do a rehearsal. This is the step the community swears by and the one most packing lists skip. Before the big flight, do a short practice trip with the full kit: a day trip, an overnight stay, a drive to the next town. Pack the bag exactly as you will pack it for the flight, go through the routine, treat a low from the pocket kit, and come home. Joanne Milo, writing in T1D Exchange, says she feels the anxiety before leaving home even for a two-hour road trip, and she leaves prepared for even a day trip, with a bag and a mini go-bag that hold her insulin, pump supplies, meter, and medical ID. The rehearsal does two things at once. It tests the kit, and it proves to you that the kit works. The fear of the first flight is mostly the fear of the unknown. A practice trip makes the unknown known.

4. The airport and the flight, step by step

The travel day has its own rhythm, and knowing it in advance is half the cure. Here it is, in order.

Arrive early, and then earlier. The ADA's flying guidance says to arrive two to three hours before your flight. Beyond Type 1's Tiana Cooks adds 15 to 20 minutes on top of that for the checkpoint, because navigating security with diabetes is never the same twice: the process can be different at every airport, and the extra minutes are the buffer that keeps a slow screening from becoming a missed flight. Rushing is the fastest way to turn a manageable checkpoint into a stressful one. The extra time is not wasted. It is the anxiety treatment.

At the checkpoint, say it first. Before screening begins, tell the officer you have diabetes and that you are wearing an insulin pump or a continuous glucose monitor, and separate your diabetes supplies from your other belongings in a clear, sealable bag. That is the ADA's guidance, and it is also what every traveler quoted in this guide does. If you prefer a pat-down to the body scanner, say so. The ADA's guidance is clear: passengers who use insulin pumps and continuous glucose monitors have the right to decide to be screened by body scanner or by pat-down, and officers should never tell you to take off your devices. If a pat-down is required, you have the right to be screened in private with a witness of your choice, and under most circumstances the officer will ask you to pat down your own devices and then test your hands for traces of explosives. Medical liquids, including insulin and juice for a low, are exempt from the 3.4 ounce limit. Take them out of your bag and tell TSA, and do not put them in the quart-sized bag with your other liquids. If you go low during screening, tell the officers immediately. If you want help at the airport, ask any officer for a passenger support specialist, or request preboarding at the gate so your carry-on with the medical supplies is not gate-checked when the overhead bins fill. Our airport assistance guide covers preboarding, terminal help, and the sunflower lanyard for hidden disabilities.

On the plane, keep the kit at your seat. Everything you might need goes in the bag under the seat in front of you: insulin, pump supplies, sensor spares, the fingerstick meter, the glucagon device, chargers, and the low kit. Nothing diabetes-related goes in the overhead bin, and nothing goes in checked baggage. Tell the flight attendant you have diabetes, especially if you are traveling alone. Tiana Cooks says it plainly: tell the flight attendant if you are really feeling anxious about traveling on the airplane. Then eat. Cooks says the same thing in her own words: travel days run on adrenaline, and you do not want a severe low in the middle of a flight, so eat, and keep your own snacks with you, because there are times when the crew will tell you to take your seat and service stops. Check your glucose more often than usual on a travel day. Ali Runs on Insulin says she does not expect perfect blood sugars on travel days, and instead focuses on staying flexible, checking her CGM more often, and making small adjustments as needed. When the meal tray lands, wait until it is in front of you before taking insulin for the meal. Our airline meals guide covers the meal timing and what to order.

One warning about stress and blood sugar. Travel days often run high, not low, and that surprises people. Breakthrough T1D UK says insulin requirements may vary with changes in activity and stress levels and unfamiliar foods, and T1D Exchange's Scott Johnson says he usually sees a day or two of higher blood sugars after a trip, because the travel and the disruption to his routine stress his body. Do not read a high number on a travel day as a failure. It is the normal cost of a disrupted routine, and it settles when the routine does.

5. When things go wrong anyway

Preparation does not promise a perfect trip. It promises a next step for every failure, and the next step is what kills the fear. Here are the four failures travelers worry about most, and the next step for each.

The flight is delayed. A delay is a diabetes logistics problem, not a ruined trip. Keep the low kit and the insulin with you in the terminal, eat on a schedule even when the departure time keeps moving, and charge the CGM phone while you wait. Our long layovers playbook covers meal timing when food is uncertain, lows away from your bag, and your delay rights in the US and the EU.

The bag is lost. This is the fear that keeps people up the night before a flight, and the answer is the packing rule from section 3: the carry-on holds everything you need, the checked bag holds nothing you cannot replace, and the supplies are divided between bags. If the bag with the spares is gone, the recovery playbook gives the exact call order for replacing insulin, pump supplies, and sensors fast. Our lost supplies playbook has it, and the finding insulin abroad playbook covers the pharmacy route when you are in another country.

A device fails. A pod fails, a sensor peels off, the CGM reads wrong. Pack the backup the way the T1D Exchange checklist says: backup pens and syringes if you use a pump, a fingerstick meter and strips even if you use a CGM, and ketone strips in the just-in-case supplies. A meter is the backup that never needs a signal, and it is the one item every traveler in this guide packs. If the sensor fails, the meter is the plan. If the pump fails, the pens are the plan. Write the plan down before the trip, so you are following a list, not improvising.

You run out. It happens, usually because the trip got longer than planned. The fix is the same as the fix for a lost bag: the pharmacy route, the travel insurance route, and the care team back home, who can send a prescription to a pharmacy near you. Carry cash, as Tiana Cooks advises, because a vending machine at 4:00 a.m. in a hotel takes cash when nothing else is open, and she has been the person walking to it. The point of this section is not that nothing will go wrong. Things go wrong on trips with diabetes all the time. The point is that every one of them has a next step, and the next step is written down before you leave.

6. The first flight after a long gap, and the second one

There is a difference between a first flight and a first flight after years away, and it is worth naming. A first flight is a fear of the unknown. A first flight after years away is a fear of the unknown plus a habit of staying home, and the habit is the harder half. The traveler in the r/Type1Diabetes thread had not taken a vacation in five or six years. Five or six years of staying home teaches the brain that home is safe and everywhere else is not, and the longer the gap, the louder that lesson gets. The cure is the same, but the dose is smaller: a short trip first, then the long one.

That is what the experienced travelers say, in their own words. Ali Runs on Insulin, writing about her first flight after diagnosis, says it gets easier, and then she gives the timeline: the second will feel easier, and by the tenth, you will be helping someone else who is taking their first trip. Julie Kiefer, a frequent traveler living with type 1 diabetes quoted by Breakthrough T1D UK, says each time we face our fears we get strength, courage and confidence, and the results are in the doing. Tiana Cooks says the same thing from the other side: you keep yourself alive every single day, and going on a trip is doable, because you already know what to do. None of them says the fear disappears. They say the fear gets smaller with each flight, and that the doing is what shrinks it.

So take the small version first. A short practice trip, a companion on the first flight, a call to your care team to walk through the plan. Bring someone if you can. If you cannot, the solo travel playbook covers the plan that works alone, and the glucose sharing setup from section 3 means someone at home is watching your numbers. And if the fear is still the thing stopping you after the prep is done, that is the moment to bring your care team in, not the moment to cancel the trip quietly. The ADA recommends screening for fear of hypoglycemia and referring people who have it to mental health providers. That referral is not a verdict. It is the next step, the same as the pharmacy route or the backup pen.

Thanksgiving week is the right moment for this guide because it is the week when the most first flights in years happen. If yours is one of them, pack the bag the night before, arrive early, tell the officer, tell the flight attendant, keep the kit at your seat, and eat. Then get on the plane. The second flight will feel easier.

What to do on your next flight

Here is the full list, in this order:

Travel Anxiety with Type 1 Diabetes: The First-Flight Checklist

  • Four to six weeks before: book the pre-trip appointment with your care team, bring the itinerary, and leave with a travel letter, generic-name prescriptions, a time zone plan, a sick-day plan, and a backup plan for a failed pod; say it out loud if fear of hypoglycemia is part of why you are anxious
  • Pack 20% extra supplies, more if it helps you stop worrying; all insulin and supplies in the carry-on at your seat, never in checked baggage; divide supplies between bags
  • Low kit at your seat: sugar packets (1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60), fingerstick meter, glucagon device; backup pens and syringes if you use a pump; chargers and a power bank; cash; medical ID
  • Within 72 hours of the flight: call TSA Cares at 1-855-787-2227 if you want help at the checkpoint; carry the travel letter and the TSA Disability Notification Card
  • Night before: pack the bag, charge every device, set up glucose sharing so someone at home can see your numbers, and check that your CGM alarms are on
  • At the airport: arrive 2 to 3 hours early, plus 15 to 20 extra minutes for the checkpoint; tell the officer you have diabetes before screening begins; ask for a pat-down or a private screening if you prefer it; ask for preboarding at the gate so your medical bag is not gate-checked
  • On the plane: tell the flight attendant you have diabetes, especially if you are traveling alone; keep the low kit at your seat, not in the overhead bin; eat; check your glucose more often; wait until the meal tray lands before taking insulin for the meal
  • If things go wrong: a delay is a logistics problem, use the layover plan; a lost bag means the recovery call order; a failed device means the meter and the backup pens; running out means the pharmacy route and the care team back home
  • After landing: check your glucose, reset your pump clock to local time with the time zone guide, restock before the next trip, and read the trip in your CGM data with the post-trip reset playbook

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is it normal to feel anxious about flying with type 1 diabetes?

A: Yes. The fear has a name in the research: fear of hypoglycemia, the fear of going low, and it is common. In a 2023 study of 553 adults with type 1 diabetes, 30 percent reported a high fear of hypoglycemia. The fear is not a sign that you cannot travel. It is a sign that your brain is doing its job, flagging the real risks of a low far from home, and the fix is preparation, not willpower.

Q: What is fear of hypoglycemia, and how common is it?

A: Fear of hypoglycemia is a specific and extreme fear of going low, or of the risk of going low. A 2023 study in BMJ Open Diabetes Research and Care screened 553 adults with type 1 diabetes and found 30 percent had a high fear of hypoglycemia score. People with high fear also had higher A1C, more severe low episodes, and more impaired awareness of lows. The American Diabetes Association recommends screening for fear of hypoglycemia in clinical practice and referring people who have it to mental health providers.

Q: What should I tell TSA at the airport?

A: Tell the officer you have diabetes and that you are wearing an insulin pump or a continuous glucose monitor before screening begins, and separate your diabetes supplies from your other belongings. You can ask for a pat-down instead of the body scanner, and you can ask for a private screening with a witness of your choice. Medical liquids, including insulin and juice for a low, are exempt from the 3.4 ounce limit: take them out of your bag and tell TSA. If you want help in advance, call TSA Cares at 1-855-787-2227 within 72 hours of your flight.

Q: How much extra diabetes supplies should I pack?

A: Pack more than the trip math says. This site's rule is 20 percent extra, and experienced travelers pack more: Ali Runs on Insulin packs at least twice what she expects to use, and Beyond Type 1's Tiana Cooks packs three times what she needs. All insulin and supplies ride in your carry-on at your seat, never in checked baggage. If you want the exact count for your trip, the packing calculator on this site builds the list for you.

Q: What do I do if I go low on the plane?

A: Treat it with your usual plan, from the low kit at your seat, not from the overhead bin. Keep sugar packets in your pocket: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. Tell the person next to you and the flight attendant. If the low is severe and you cannot treat yourself, the crew has an emergency medical kit and a plan for it, which our severe-low playbook covers in full. After a low, check your glucose again before your next meal.

Q: What if my anxiety is so bad I keep putting off the trip?

A: First, know that the fear is common and that other travelers have felt it before their first flight and flown anyway. Second, shrink the trip before you take it: a short practice trip, a companion on the first flight, and a call to your care team to walk through the plan. Third, if anxiety is changing how you manage your diabetes, or stopping you from traveling at all, tell your care team. The American Diabetes Association recommends screening for fear of hypoglycemia and referring people who have it to mental health providers, and that conversation is part of diabetes care, not separate from it.
