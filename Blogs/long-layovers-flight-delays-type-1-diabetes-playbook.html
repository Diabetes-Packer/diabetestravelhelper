Long Layovers and Flight Delays with Type 1 Diabetes: The Airport Playbook (2026)

Updated October 2026 · 9 min read

Picture the departure board: your 45 minute connection is now 4 hours, then 8, then tomorrow morning. A short layover is a bathroom break. A long one is a second travel day, unplanned, inside a building that was never designed for it. For type 1 diabetes (T1D), the delay does not create one problem. It creates five at once: a meal that may or may not arrive, a low that finds you away from your bag, a phone battery draining toward the CGM (continuous glucose monitor) going dark, insulin sitting through a hot jet bridge or a cold overnight terminal, and a rebooking that can separate you from your supplies.

This guide is for the delay itself, not the flight. It covers meal timing when food is uncertain, lows away from your bag, power for the phone your CGM lives on, insulin temperature in the terminal, rebooking without losing supplies, and the delay rights that actually exist in the US and in Europe. Every rule and number below was verified against the current TSA (Transportation Security Administration), DOT (Department of Transportation), FAA (Federal Aviation Administration), EU, or manufacturer source in October 2026. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I fly with an insulin pump, so the lived-experience lines in here are mine and the regulatory lines are sourced.

The rule that organizes everything else. A delay is a logistics problem, not a medical experiment. Dosing decisions, a pump backup plan, and any change to your usual routine belong in a conversation with your care team before the trip, not at a gate at midnight. This guide is general information, not medical advice, and nothing here replaces your endocrinologist or diabetes educator.

The five things that break, in order

Delays break diabetes plans in a predictable order, and knowing the order is the plan.

  • First, the meal plan. The meal you planned to eat at your destination slides, and the meal you planned on the plane may never arrive.
  • Second, the low plan. You are walking terminals, standing in rebooking lines, and sitting far from your bag when a low hits.
  • Third, the power plan. Your phone is doing boarding passes, rebooking, maps, and CGM readings at once, and the battery drains faster than on any normal day.
  • Fourth, the temperature plan. Insulin spends hours in a hot jet bridge, a cold gate area, or an overnight terminal.
  • Fifth, the supply plan. A rebooking moves you to a new flight, and a checked bag does not always follow.

The fix for all five is the same shape: keep the essentials on your person, in the bag under the seat, not in the bag in the bin or the bag in the belly of the plane.

Meals: never bolus for food that has not arrived

This is the rule that matters most on a delay day, and it is simple. Do not bolus for a meal until the food is actually in front of you. A delayed tray, a swapped special meal, or a cancelled flight after an early bolus is how lows are made on travel days. The airline meals guide makes the same point for the cabin: meal in hand, then your normal pre-bolus.

On a long layover, eat a real meal when you can, before the next flight, not during the boarding scramble. Protein-forward food travels best through a delay: it is the same principle as the low-carb, high-protein breakfast at home, and it holds steadier than pastry-case sugar across hours of waiting. I pre-bolus without a timer and it works fine; a phone reminder is an optional crutch if you are someone who wants one, never the plan. Run the routine that already works at home. A delay day is not the day to experiment with new timing.

Pack backup food you control, in the personal item, not the overhead bin. Solid food clears TSA (Transportation Security Administration) screening with no quantity limit, and medically necessary liquids, including juice you carry for lows, may exceed the 3.4 ounce limit if you declare them to the officer at the checkpoint. The backup meal is what turns a cancelled dinner service from an emergency into an inconvenience.

Lows away from your bag: sugar on your person

A delay puts distance between you and your bag. Rebooking lines, gate changes, and terminal walks all happen with the bag somewhere else. The fix is a pocket rule: sugar packets live on your person, not in the pack. Sugar packets are my low treatment everywhere, one packet at 60 to 70, two below 60. They are flat, they weigh nothing, and they survive every version of a delay.

Keep the same rule for the meter. A fingerstick meter in a reachable pocket beats a perfect CGM setup in a bag you cannot reach during a low. If your sensor reading does not match how you feel during a chaotic delay day, check with the meter and trust the number that matches your symptoms.

Power: your phone is a medical device now

If your CGM readings live on your phone, a dead phone means no readings, no alerts, and no trend arrows. A delay day burns phone battery harder than any normal day: boarding passes, rebooking apps, maps, messages home, and CGM readings, all at once. Treat the phone charger as a supply, not an accessory.

The FAA (Federal Aviation Administration) rules for the power bank itself are strict and simple. The FAA counts power banks as spare lithium batteries: up to 100 watt-hours (Wh, the unit for stored energy) needs no airline approval; 101 to 160 Wh needs airline approval, with a limit of two spare batteries; anything over 160 Wh is not allowed on passenger aircraft. Power banks must ride in carry-on only, never in checked luggage, and many airlines set stricter limits than the FAA floor, so check your carrier. The full power routine, including which cable each device needs, is in the devices charging guide.

Two delay-day power moves matter more than the rest. First, charge at the gate, not on the plane: seat power is inconsistent, and the gate area is your reliable charge stop. Second, if your carry-on gets gate-checked because the flight is full, take the power bank out first and put it in your personal item. The moment the bag leaves your hands for the hold, a power bank inside it is a violation, and your CGM phone charger just flew without you.

The Dexcom G7 receiver is the insurance policy here. It shows readings without a phone, it is rechargeable, and it keeps working when the phone is dead or out of charge. And remember the sharing rule from the CGM sharing playbook: sharing pauses while your phone is in flight mode, and your followers only see your numbers again once your phone has internet. A quick "landed, back online" text after a long delay keeps everyone calm.

Insulin temperature in the terminal

A terminal is not climate-controlled the way a hotel room is. A sun-facing jet bridge in July is hot. A gate area during an overnight delay in January is cold. Insulin rides through both in your bag, so the temperature rules are worth knowing cold.

The NovoLog label states the standard: unopened insulin stays refrigerated between 36F and 46F until expiry; do not freeze it, and do not use it if it has been frozen; in-use insulin keeps at room temperature below 86F (30C) for up to 28 days, away from direct heat and light. Frozen insulin does not recover when it thaws, so freezing is a discard, not a scare. The full storage rules, labels included, are in the hotel insulin storage guide.

On a delay, the practical rule is placement. Keep insulin in your personal item, close to your body, not in an overhead bin, not in a bag left at a gate, and never in a checked bag. A pump adds one more line from Insulet's own safety information: pressure changes during takeoff can cause unintended insulin delivery from tiny air bubbles expanding inside the Pod, so check glucose frequently when flying to avoid a prolonged low. A delay means more takeoffs, more pressure changes, and more reason to watch the trend arrows.

Dexcom's G7 numbers set the other temperature boundary. The sensor operates between 50F and 107.6F, the receiver down to 32F, and spare sensors store between 36F and 86F, never in the freezer. A sensor worn under a layer stays inside its range in any terminal. Spare sensors in a bag left in a hot jet bridge or a freezing gate area do not.

Rebooking without losing your supplies

A missed connection is where supplies get lost, and the loss is almost always preventable. The rule is absolute: diabetes supplies never go in checked luggage. The DOT (Department of Transportation) itself advises keeping critical items, including medicine, in your carry-on, and notes that even a bag that is not lost may be delayed for a day or two. Pack everything you need for the first 24 hours in the carry-on, as a matter of routine, not as a delay-day exception.

Book connections on one ticket when you can. On a single booking, the airline owns getting you to your destination, and your bags are tagged through. On separate tickets, the second airline owes you nothing for the first airline's delay, and a rebooking can strand both you and a checked bag. This is a booking choice you make at home, not a negotiation you win at a gate.

If a checked bag is delayed anyway, file the airline's lost-baggage report before you leave the airport and keep the reference number. The DOT advises exactly this: report problems before leaving the airport, insist on a report record and its number, and get an agent name and phone number for follow-up. If the bag is truly gone, the recovery call order, pharmacy transfer, care-team on-call line, telehealth, manufacturer emergency shipping, is in the lost-or-stolen supplies playbook.

And pack the buffer for this exact scenario. The how-much-insulin guide sets the standing rule: calculate what the trip needs, add 20%, and pack that. A delay that stretches a 7 day trip into 9 or 10 days is paid for out of that buffer, not out of a pharmacy run in a strange city. A spare pod in the day pack, not just in the luggage, is the same idea: the pod-failure backup plan covers the drill.

Your delay rights: US and EU, side by side

Rights do not fix blood sugar, but they decide who pays for the hotel, the meal, and the rebooking. The US and EU rules are very different, and confusing them is the expensive mistake.

In the US: the tarmac rule is strong, the terminal rule is not

The one hard US rule is the tarmac delay rule. The DOT states that at a US airport, an airline may not keep passengers aboard an aircraft for more than 3 hours on a domestic flight, or 4 hours on an international flight, without an opportunity to deplane. Food and water must be offered at the 2 hour point, lavatories must stay operational, and medical care must be provided if needed. Exceptions exist for safety, security, and air traffic control.

In the terminal, the picture flips. The DOT is blunt: there are no Federal requirements for terminal amenities during a delay. Each airline has its own policies about meals, phone calls, and hotels, and some offer nothing when weather or something beyond their control caused the delay. Ask the staff what they will cover, and check the airline's published commitments on the DOT customer service dashboard before you argue. If the flight is cancelled and you choose not to travel, you are entitled to a refund for the unused transportation, even on a non-refundable ticket, plus any bag fee you paid.

In Europe: EU261 pays for care, and sometimes cash

EU261, the EU's air passenger rights regulation, covers flights departing an EU airport on any airline, and flights arriving in the EU on an EU airline. It gives two different things, and they are easy to mix up.

  • Care, which applies no matter the cause. During a long delay, the airline owes meals and refreshments reasonable for the wait, two phone calls or emails, and hotel accommodation plus transport between the airport and the hotel when an overnight stay becomes necessary. The delay thresholds for care depend on flight distance: 2 hours for short flights, 3 hours for medium flights, 4 hours for long flights. Care applies even for weather delays.
  • Cash compensation, which depends on fault. When you arrive 3 or more hours late and the airline cannot show extraordinary circumstances, compensation is 250 euros for flights of 1,500 km or less, 400 euros for intra-EU flights over 1,500 km and other flights between 1,500 and 3,500 km, and 600 euros for longer flights. A delay of 5 hours or more also gives you the choice of a refund or re-routing.

The diabetes angle on EU261 is practical, not legal. An overnight delay means an overnight stay, and an overnight stay means your insulin, your spares, your charger, and your sugar packets need to be in the bag you carry into the hotel, not in the bag the airline is still looking for. Claim the hotel, take the room, and keep the kit with you.

One more rights note for the trip as a whole: travel insurance with trip-delay coverage can fill the gap the US rules leave open, paying for meals and a hotel after a set delay, often 6 or 12 hours depending on the plan. The travel insurance guide covers what to check before you buy, including the pre-existing condition waiver window.

The long-layover checklist

Here is the full kit, in delay order:

  1. Never bolus for a meal until the food is in front of you; eat a real meal before a long layover when you can, and pre-bolus the way you do at home.
  2. Sugar packets on your person, not in the pack: one packet at 60 to 70, two below 60, plus a fingerstick meter in a reachable pocket.
  3. Phone charger and a power bank at or under 100 Wh in your carry-on; charge at the gate, not on the plane; take the power bank out before any gate-checked bag.
  4. The Dexcom G7 receiver, charged, as the phone-independent backup; tell your followers sharing pauses in flight mode.
  5. Insulin in your personal item, close to your body: in-use below 86F for up to 28 days, never frozen, unopened spares refrigerated 36F to 46F.
  6. All diabetes supplies in the carry-on, never checked; book connections on one ticket when you can.
  7. 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says, with a spare pod in the day pack: the how-much-insulin guide is the starting point, and the lost-or-stolen recovery playbook covers the worst case.
  8. If a checked bag is delayed, file the lost-baggage report before leaving the airport and keep the reference number.
  9. Know the rule for where you are: the US tarmac rule (deplane at 3 hours domestic, 4 hours international; food and water at 2 hours), and EU261 care plus the 250, 400, and 600 euro compensation bands in Europe.
  10. Travel insurance checked before departure, including trip-delay coverage and the pre-existing condition waiver: the travel insurance guide covers what to check.

Flying into the holidays? The airline meals guide covers the tray side of the trip, and the devices charging guide covers the power side. A delay is survivable when the kit stays on you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Should I bolus for a meal during a long layover if I am not sure when I will eat?

A: No. Wait until the food is actually in front of you before you bolus for it. A delayed, swapped, or cancelled meal after an early bolus is how lows happen on travel days. Eat a real meal before a long layover when you can, pack backup food you control, and pre-bolus the way you normally do at home once the meal is in hand. If travel-day timing feels different from your normal routine, that conversation belongs with your care team before the trip.

Q: What are my rights during a tarmac delay in the US?

A: Under the US Department of Transportation tarmac delay rule, an airline at a US airport may not keep passengers aboard for more than 3 hours on a domestic flight (4 hours on an international flight) without an opportunity to deplane. Food and water must be offered at the 2 hour point, lavatories must stay operational, and medical care must be provided if needed. There are exceptions for safety, security, and air traffic control.

Q: Can I take a power bank on a plane for my CGM phone?

A: Yes, but only in your carry-on, never in checked luggage. The FAA counts power banks as spare lithium batteries: up to 100 watt-hours needs no airline approval, 101 to 160 watt-hours needs airline approval with a limit of two spare batteries, and anything over 160 watt-hours is not allowed on passenger aircraft. If your carry-on gets gate-checked, take the power bank out first and keep it with you.

Q: What does EU261 give me during a long delay in Europe?

A: EU261 covers flights departing an EU airport on any airline, and flights arriving in the EU on an EU airline. During a long delay the airline owes care: meals and refreshments reasonable for the wait, two phone calls or emails, and hotel accommodation plus transport to and from it when an overnight stay becomes necessary. Cash compensation of 250, 400, or 600 euros by distance applies when you arrive 3 or more hours late and the airline cannot show extraordinary circumstances. A delay of 5 hours or more also gives you the choice of a refund or re-routing.

Q: How do I keep insulin safe during a long hot or cold layover?

A: Keep insulin in your personal item, close to you, not in an overhead bin or a checked bag. In-use insulin stays good at room temperature below 86F (30C) for up to 28 days on the NovoLog label, and frozen insulin must be discarded because it does not recover when it thaws. Unopened insulin stays refrigerated between 36F and 46F until expiry. A hot jet bridge in summer and a cold overnight terminal in winter are both reasons to keep insulin on your body, not in a bag left somewhere.

Q: My connection is missed and my checked bag is gone with it. What do I do about my diabetes supplies?

A: This is exactly why diabetes supplies never go in checked luggage: your carry-on is the supply that cannot be separated from you by a missed connection. If a checked bag is delayed, file the airline's lost-baggage report before you leave the airport and keep the reference number. The US Department of Transportation notes a bag may be delayed for a day or two, and advises keeping everything you need for the first 24 hours in your carry-on. The recovery call order for a truly lost bag is in the lost-or-stolen supplies playbook.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your insulin plan on a delay day, your pump backup strategy, CGM alert settings, and any change to your usual meal timing are conversations with your care team, ideally before you book anything nonrefundable. Manufacturer specifications and passenger-rights rules change, so confirm the current numbers for your devices and your airline before you travel. If you have a severe low or suspected DKA (diabetic ketoacidosis) on a trip, get medical help and tell your care team afterward.
