TSA PreCheck with Type 1 Diabetes: What Actually Changes at the Checkpoint (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

The hardest minute of holiday travel with type 1 diabetes is the minute at the checkpoint when the officer waves you toward the body scanner and you are wearing an insulin pump and a continuous glucose monitor (CGM), a small sensor that reads your glucose every few minutes. Three programs promise to change that minute: TSA PreCheck, Global Entry, and CLEAR Plus. They do not do the same thing, they do not cost the same, and only one of them changes what happens to your devices. This guide walks through each one with the 2026 fees and the device rules as they are published, so you can pick the right one and enroll now, in time to use it this season. Thanksgiving falls on Thursday, November 26, and the Christmas flights come right after it.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. This guide does not describe a personal enrollment story. It is built from the published rules of the Transportation Security Administration (TSA), U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP), and CLEAR, plus the device guidance from the makers, and every fee and rule below is dated to the page it came from. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own checkpoint plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not flying, tell your care team.

1. What PreCheck actually changes, and what it does not

TSA PreCheck is the Transportation Security Administration's (TSA) own expedited screening program. Once you are approved, you get a Known Traveler Number (KTN). Add it to your airline profile and to each booking, and the airline prints a TSA PreCheck indicator on your boarding pass. At the airport you use a dedicated lane, and in that lane you keep your shoes, belt, and light jacket on, and your laptop and 3-1-1 liquids stay in your bag. TSA says about 99 percent of PreCheck passengers wait less than 10 minutes in the lane.

What PreCheck does not change matters just as much. TSA is plain about it: all travelers will be screened, and no individual is guaranteed expedited screening. You can still be picked for additional screening on any given trip, and PreCheck does not exempt you from screening at all. The lane is faster and the routine is lighter, but it is still screening, and the device rules in the next section still apply. Our TSA rules guide covers the whole checkpoint for travelers without PreCheck, so you can compare the two lanes side by side.

2. The device rules in a PreCheck lane

This is the part most guides get wrong, so here is the rule as our pat-down guide states it, from TSA's own published screening rules. In a PreCheck lane, additional screening of your insulin pump or CGM is only required if the device alarms in the walk-through metal detector or the body scanner. No alarm means the lane moves like it does for everyone else. If it does alarm, the officer directs you to pat down the device yourself, over your clothing, and then tests your hands for traces of explosives. It takes under a minute, in the open lane, and it is the same routine for tubed pumps, tubeless pods, and CGMs.

For an Omnipod 5, the maker's guidance is specific. Insulet says the Pod can go through a walk-through metal detector, and it recommends requesting alternative screening, a hand-held wand, a walk-through metal detector, or a pat-down, instead of the body scanner, because the system has not been fully tested in all body scanner environments. The Controller goes in the tray with your other electronics, and Insulet says not to take the Omnipod 5 System through X-ray baggage scanners, so ask the officer for a visual inspection of the Controller and any bags holding system parts instead. Our Omnipod 5 TSA guide covers the Pod and Controller in full.

For a Dexcom CGM, the two models split. Dexcom says the G7 sensor can go through walk-through metal detectors and AIT body scanners, with a pat-down or hand-wanding as the alternative, while the G6 must not go through AIT scanners. AIT stands for advanced imaging technology, the booth where you raise your arms. If you wear a G6, the pat-down track is the right one. Our Dexcom G7 airport guide covers what the scanner does and does not do to a sensor, and our pat-down rights guide covers the full screening choice, including the exact sentences to say.

The opt-out still works in a PreCheck lane. If you choose not to be screened by the body scanner, the officer will conduct a pat-down instead. You do not need a reason, a doctor's note, or anyone's permission, and you will not be asked to take the device off. You can ask for a private screening with a witness of your choice at any point. The three sentences from our pat-down guide work here too: say you wear an insulin pump and a CGM, say you would like a pat-down instead of the body scanner, and say please do not remove the device or put it on the X-ray belt.

3. How to enroll in TSA PreCheck

Enrollment has two steps. First, the online form, which TSA says takes as little as 5 minutes. Second, an in-person visit of about 10 minutes, where the provider checks your documents, takes your fingerprints and a photo, and collects payment. TSA works with three authorized enrollment providers, CLEAR, IDEMIA, and Telos, with more than 1,300 enrollment locations between them. PreCheck is open to U.S. citizens, U.S. nationals, and lawful permanent residents, and every applicant goes through a background check.

The fee depends on which provider you pick. As listed on tsa.gov in November 2026, a new five-year enrollment costs $79.75 through IDEMIA, $84.95 through CLEAR, and $85.00 through Telos. The membership lasts five years, and the lane is the same whichever provider you choose, so pick the one with a location and appointment time that suit you. Fees change, so check the current price on tsa.gov before you pay.

Most applicants receive approval notification in 3 to 5 days, though some applications can take up to 60 days. That is the reason to enroll now rather than the week before a holiday flight: a 3 to 5 day approval still leaves room for a slow case, and a 60 day case does not. Once you are approved, add your Known Traveler Number to your airline profile and to every booking, and check that the PreCheck indicator shows on your boarding pass before you leave for the airport.

Renewal is cheaper than a new enrollment and is done online with most providers. As listed on tsa.gov in November 2026, online renewal costs $58.75 through IDEMIA, $69.95 through Telos, and $72.95 through CLEAR, with in-person renewal at $66.75, $58.75, and $84.95 in that same order. TSA encourages members to renew at least 60 days before expiry, because an expired Known Traveler Number will not put the PreCheck indicator on your boarding pass.

4. Global Entry: the same lane, plus the trip home

Global Entry is run by U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP), not by TSA, and it works at the other end of the trip. It is a Trusted Traveler Program for pre-approved, low-risk travelers arriving in the United States, and it gives expedited clearance through customs when you land back home. The application fee is $120, and membership lasts for five years. The benefit that matters for this guide is on CBP's own list: Global Entry includes TSA PreCheck access. One application and one fee buy both the faster lane out and the faster customs line home. Our international travel guide covers the rest of the return trip, from insulin at the border to the time zone reset on landing.

The process is heavier than PreCheck alone. You apply through the Trusted Traveler Programs website, pay the $120 fee, and wait for CBP to complete a background check. CBP says most applications are reviewed within 2 weeks, while some can take up to 12 months or longer. Then you schedule and attend an in-person interview, which is required for first-time enrollment. Minors are free with a parent who is already a member or applying at the same time. If you fly abroad even once a year, Global Entry is the better buy: $120 against $85 or less for PreCheck alone, for the same five years. If you never leave the country, PreCheck alone is the cheaper path.

5. CLEAR Plus: faster to the line, not through it

CLEAR Plus is the odd one out, because it is a private company, not a government program. It uses biometrics, your face or your eyes, to verify your identity at the checkpoint, and it walks you past the document-check queue to the front of the screening line. The price is $219 a year, as listed on clearme.com, and it runs at more than 150 CLEAR+ Lanes across 63 airports. CLEAR says it can also be used with TSA PreCheck for the fastest way to your gate.

Here is what it does not do. CLEAR Plus speeds the document check, and the screening itself still happens exactly as it would have. Without PreCheck alongside it, you still take off your shoes and take out the laptop and liquids once you reach the belt. With a pump or CGM, nothing about the device screening changes: the alarm rule, the self pat-down, and the opt-out all work the same way, because CLEAR verifies who you are and TSA still does the screening. CLEAR Plus is worth it only if you fly often through airports that have CLEAR lanes and the document-check line is the part that costs you time. For most travelers with diabetes, PreCheck is the purchase that changes the checkpoint, and CLEAR Plus is an add-on for the line before it.

6. Kids and family: the age rules that trip people up

Children 17 and under can join an enrolled adult in the TSA PreCheck lane for free, but the details depend on the child's age. Children 12 and under can accompany an enrolled parent or guardian in the PreCheck lane without restriction: no separate membership, no fee, and the indicator does not need to appear on the child's own boarding pass. Children 13 to 17 can also use the lane, but only if the PreCheck indicator appears on the teenager's own boarding pass, which happens when the teen is on the same airline reservation as an enrolled adult whose boarding pass shows the indicator. A teen booked on a separate reservation will not get the indicator without their own enrollment, so check the booking before the trip, not at the airport.

Anyone 18 or older needs their own enrollment and their own Known Traveler Number. For Global Entry, the family math is kinder: minors are free when a parent is already a member or applying at the same time, though every applicant still goes through a background check. 7. The holiday plan: enroll now, use it this season

Thanksgiving falls on Thursday, November 26, and the Christmas flights come right after it. If you enroll this week, the usual 3 to 5 day approval lands well before the holiday lines form, and even a slow case has room to clear. If you are already enrolled, check that your Known Traveler Number is on every holiday booking and that the indicator shows on each boarding pass, because a booking made before you enrolled will not have it.

If approval runs long, the backup plan is the standard lane with the same device rules: tell the officer about the pump or CGM before screening begins, use the opt-out script if you prefer the pat-down, and call TSA Cares at 1-855-787-2227 within 72 hours of your flight if you want a Passenger Support Specialist to meet you at the checkpoint. Pack 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says, keep sugar packets in your pocket through security and at your seat, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, and keep the low kit at your seat, not in the overhead bin. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Does TSA PreCheck change how my insulin pump or CGM is screened?

A: It changes the routine, not the rights. In a PreCheck lane you keep your shoes, belt, and light jacket on, and your laptop and 3-1-1 liquids stay in your bag. For the devices, the rule is specific: additional screening of your insulin pump or CGM is only required if the device alarms in the walk-through metal detector or the body scanner. No alarm means the lane moves like it does for everyone else. If it does alarm, the officer directs you to pat down the device yourself and tests your hands for traces of explosives, the same as in standard lanes. The opt-out still works too: you can ask for a pat-down instead of the body scanner, and you will not be asked to take the device off.

Q: How much does TSA PreCheck cost, and how long does approval take?

A: A new five-year membership costs $79.75 through IDEMIA, $84.95 through CLEAR, or $85.00 through Telos, as listed on tsa.gov in November 2026. The lane is the same whichever provider you choose. You apply online in about 5 minutes, then finish at an enrollment location in about 10 minutes, where the provider checks your documents and takes your fingerprints and a photo. Most applicants receive approval notification in 3 to 5 days, though some applications can take up to 60 days, so enroll well before a holiday flight. Membership lasts five years, and online renewal costs $58.75 through IDEMIA, $69.95 through Telos, or $72.95 through CLEAR.

Q: Does Global Entry include TSA PreCheck?

A: Yes. Global Entry is run by U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP), costs $120 for five years, and lists TSA PreCheck access as a membership benefit. It also gives expedited clearance through customs when you arrive back in the United States, and it can be used at land borders. The process is heavier than PreCheck alone: you apply through the Trusted Traveler Programs website, pass a background check, and attend an in-person interview, which is required for first-time enrollment. CBP says most applications are reviewed within 2 weeks, while some can take up to 12 months or longer. Minors are free with a parent who is already a member or applying at the same time.

Q: Does CLEAR Plus help with a pump or CGM at security?

A: No. CLEAR Plus is a private biometric identity service that speeds the document check at the front of the checkpoint: you verify your identity with your face at a CLEAR pod and walk past the ID line. It costs $219 a year and runs at more than 150 lanes across 63 airports. It does not change the physical screening, so a pump or CGM gets the same screening route with or without it, and the alarm rule, the self pat-down, and the body scanner opt-out all work the same way. CLEAR Plus is useful only alongside TSA PreCheck, where it shortens the line before the faster lane.

Q: Can my kids use TSA PreCheck with me?

A: Children 17 and under can join an enrolled adult in the TSA PreCheck lane for free, with one age split to know. Children 12 and under can accompany an enrolled parent or guardian without restriction, and the PreCheck indicator does not need to appear on the child's own boarding pass. Children 13 to 17 can use the lane only if the PreCheck indicator appears on their own boarding pass, which requires traveling on the same airline reservation as an enrolled adult. A teen on a separate reservation will not get the indicator without their own enrollment. Anyone 18 or older needs their own membership and Known Traveler Number.

Q: Can I opt out of the body scanner in a PreCheck lane?

A: Yes. If you choose not to be screened by the body scanner, the officer will conduct a pat-down instead, and that right does not change in a PreCheck lane. You do not need a reason, a doctor's note, or anyone's permission, and you will not be asked to remove a device attached to your body. You can ask for a private screening with a witness of your choice at any point. The practical script is the same as in a standard lane: say you wear an insulin pump and a CGM, say you would like a pat-down instead of the body scanner, and say please do not remove the device or put it on the X-ray belt.
