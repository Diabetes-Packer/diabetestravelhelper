Pod Failed on Vacation? Your Omnipod Travel Backup Plan (2026)

A pod failure while traveling is basically inevitable. After 18 years on Omnipod, here is my backup plan: the 20% packing rule, why supplies never go in checked luggage, what to actually do when a pod fails, and the adhesion fix that changed everything for me.

The 20% Rule: Always Pack More Than the Math Says

This is my oldest travel rule and my most important one: I always pack 20% more supplies than I calculate I need. You never know what will happen on a trip, and having an extra pod turns a stressful, annoying situation into a slightly better one.

The math for a 5-day trip says 2 pods (each lasts up to 3 days). I bring 3 minimum, and most of the time I bring 4. I keep them all in their own dedicated pocket in my bag so I never misplace anything. That pocket is sacred: pods, and only pods.

Why 20% works: it covers exactly one pod failure with margin left over. One failed pod on a 5-day trip with only 2 packed means you are suddenly doing the rest of the trip on injections. With 4 packed, a failure is a shrug and a pod change.

Never in Checked Luggage

All of those carefully packed extras are worthless if they are in the belly of the plane. On a 12-hour flight once, I realized I had left all my supplies in my checked bag. I spent the flight freaking out, wondering what would happen if my pod failed at 35,000 feet with no spare anywhere on the plane. Luckily, it did not fail. But that flight was a friendly reminder I never needed twice: diabetes supplies always go in the carry-on, by default, every trip, no exceptions.

Checked bags get lost. They get delayed. They sit on hot tarmacs. Your carry-on stays with you, and that is the entire point. Pods, insulin, backup supplies, all of it rides in the cabin.

When a Pod Actually Fails: My Scariest Travel Moment

I was 15, on a school trip, walking the Roman Forum in Italy, when my pod failed. This was before CGMs, so I was pricking my finger constantly, I did not feel good, and I had to work up the nerve to tell one of our parent supervisors, then figure out how to get a taxi, in Italian, back to the hotel. I changed the pod, everything ended up okay, but I missed the adventures the rest of my class had that afternoon.

That day taught me the whole backup plan, and it comes down to one habit: a spare pod rides in my day pack, not just in my luggage back at the hotel. In Rome, my supplies were at the hotel, which is why a failed pod cost me the whole afternoon. Now, whenever I head out for the day, a spare pod and fast-acting glucose go in the day pack with me. A pod failure with a spare on you is a 10-minute fix on a bench. A pod failure with your spares back at the hotel is a taxi ride in a foreign language.

The backup insulin plan matters too: always travel with a way to dose by injection, so a pod problem never means no insulin at all. But day to day, the day pack spare is the move that actually saves your plans.

The Problem Nobody Warns You About: Pods Rubbing Off

Not every pod problem is a failure alarm. For years, my hardest activities were the ones where I had to lie on my stomach, like surfing. The pod would just rub off against the board, no alarm, no warning, just gone. I avoided or dreaded those activities for a long time because of it.

It was not until recently that I discovered adhesive overpatches, patches you place over your pod or CGM to give it extra adhesion. Since I started using them, I have been able to enjoy those activities without worrying about my pod falling off, for the first time ever. In 18 years on Omnipod, it is the single biggest adhesion upgrade I have found.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many extra Omnipod pods should I pack for travel?

Pack 20 percent more than the math says you need. Each pod lasts up to 3 days, so a 5-day trip technically needs 2 pods. I bring 3 minimum and usually 4. Pod failures are more common while traveling because of heat, water, and physical activity, and the extra pod turns a stressful failure into a minor inconvenience.

What should I do if my Omnipod pod fails while traveling?

Keep a spare pod in your day pack, not just in your luggage back at the hotel. A pod failure with a spare on you is a 10-minute fix; with your spares back at the hotel, it is a ruined afternoon. If you have no pods left, switch to your backup insulin plan for injections and get to a pharmacy. Paper copies of your prescriptions make pharmacy visits abroad much easier.

Can I put Omnipod pods in checked luggage?

Do not put your diabetes supplies in checked luggage. Bags get lost and delayed, and a pod failure with no supplies is exactly the scenario you are trying to avoid. Keep all pods, insulin, and backup supplies in your carry-on, in a dedicated pocket or pouch so nothing gets misplaced.

How do I keep my Omnipod pod from falling off while swimming or surfing?

Use adhesive overpatches designed for pumps and CGMs. They go over the pod and add serious staying power for water, sweat, and friction from activities like surfing where the pod rubs against your body or a board. They are the single biggest adhesion upgrade I have found in 18 years on Omnipod.

What is a good backup plan if I run out of pods on a trip?

Carry a backup insulin plan for injections so you are never without a way to dose. Bring paper copies of your prescriptions so a local pharmacy can help, and contact Insulet support if you need replacement pods. But the real backup plan is prevention: pack 20 percent more pods than you need and keep them all in your carry-on.
