The Diabetes Road Trip Guide: Driving Safely with Type 1 Diabetes (2026)

Flying gets all the diabetes travel advice, but plenty of trips happen at 70 miles per hour. The one scenario every diabetic driver fears is a low behind the wheel, and it is the one with the clearest playbook.

Updated October 2026  ·  8 min read

I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years, and most of the travel guides on this site are about airports because that is where the complicated rules live. Road trips are simpler in almost every way except one: you are the one operating the vehicle. That makes the low-blood-sugar plan the most important part of the trip, not an afterthought. This guide covers the driving-specific rules, built on the American Diabetes Association's published driving guidance and Northwestern Medicine's driving safety guidance, plus the car-specific packing and temperature lessons from the road.

The pre-drive check

Make this a habit as automatic as the seatbelt. The American Diabetes Association recommends checking your blood glucose before getting behind the wheel, and at regular intervals on drives of an hour or longer. Their guidance sets the starting bar above 90 mg/dL.

The detail people miss is the low-normal start. The ADA specifically warns against beginning an extended drive with low-normal glucose, roughly 70 to 90 mg/dL, without eating some carbs first. A number in the low 80s feels fine sitting in the driveway and can be a 65 two hours into highway driving. If you are starting a long leg in the 70s or 80s, eat something before you merge, not after the first rest stop.

If you wear a continuous glucose monitor (CGM), keep your low alarm on and loud while you drive, and keep your phone on a car charger. A dead phone on hour four is a silent CGM.

Fast-acting carbs within arm's reach

Both the ADA and Northwestern Medicine say the same thing: always carry fast-acting carbs in the vehicle, within easy reach of the driver. Not in the trunk. Not in a bag in the back seat. In the center console or the door pocket, where your hand finds them without looking.

My low treatment is sugar packets: one packet in the 60s or low 70s, two below 60. I keep a fistful in the console. Whatever your version is, glucose tablets, juice boxes, hard candy, it needs to be reachable with the seatbelt on and the car moving, because the moment you need it is not the moment to be digging. That everyday routine is separate from the driving rule below: the rule of 15 means a full 15 grams of fast carbs, about three sugar packets.

If you feel a low behind the wheel

This is the playbook, and it is worth rehearsing mentally before you need it:

  1. Pull over safely as soon as you can. The ADA's guidance is unambiguous: stop the vehicle at the first symptoms of low blood glucose. Do not try to make the next exit.
  2. Follow the rule of 15. Eat 15 grams of fast-acting carbs, wait 15 minutes, and recheck. If you are still below 70 mg/dL, repeat.
  3. Do not rush back onto the road. Northwestern Medicine's driving guidance says not to drive again until you are above 100 mg/dL. The ADA adds the reason people ignore: clear thinking recovers more slowly than the number, usually lagging by 30 to 60 minutes. Feeling better is not the same as being safe to drive.
The seat rule. Your fast-acting carbs live where you sit, not where your luggage sits. Console, door pocket, or jacket pocket. If a low hits in stop-and-go traffic with nowhere to pull over immediately, the treatment is already in your hand.

The emergency kit for the car

A road trip emergency kit is a smaller version of the travel kit, with two additions that matter specifically because you are driving:

  • Glucagon, and a copilot who knows about it. If your care team has prescribed glucagon, bring it. Nasal glucagon (such as Baqsimi) is the travel-friendly option: no injection, and the manufacturer's instructions say it stores at room temperature up to 86°F. The critical step is not packing it, it is showing your copilot exactly where it is and how it works before you leave. That is straight from the manufacturer's instructions: they need to know before an emergency, not during one.
  • A medical ID. If you are ever in an accident and cannot speak for yourself, a bracelet or necklace that says you have diabetes tells first responders to check your glucose first. That is the difference between the right treatment and a guess.

Northwestern Medicine's driving checklist adds three more: keep an emergency contact marked ICE in your phone, carry a list of your current medications, and keep your meter with you. The medications list also solves the pharmacy problem below.

Insulin and the car: the temperature rules

This is where road trips punish the careless. A parked car in summer sun can top 130°F inside, Opened insulin is generally fine below 86°F for up to 28 days, but sustained heat far above that range degrades it. The cruel part is that heat-damaged insulin usually looks completely normal. You inject it, expect it to work, and it does not.

  • Never leave insulin in a parked car, even for a quick stop on a warm day.
  • Keep your insulin case in the passenger cabin, not the trunk, where airflow is limited.
  • Bring insulin inside overnight. Do not leave it in the car at the motel.
  • Cold weather cuts the other way. Insulin freezes at 32°F, and frozen insulin must be discarded, it does not recover on thawing. On fall and winter drives, keep insulin close to your body, not in an outer bag in a cold car.

For long drives, an insulated case with ice packs or a FRIO evaporative cooling wallet does the same job it does on flights. The full breakdown is in how to keep insulin cool while traveling.

Eating on the road

Gas station food is a blood sugar obstacle course, so keep the same approach that works at home. Mine is a low-carb, high-protein breakfast to steady the day, then protein-focused choices after that. It is not exciting, but it keeps the driving hours predictable, and predictable is the whole goal.

Plan your stops around meals when you can. Bolusing for food you have not received yet is how lows happen, in restaurants and at drive-throughs alike. Order, see the food, then dose.

Multi-day drives: time zones and pharmacies

Crossing time zones on a multi-day drive shifts your long-acting insulin schedule with the clock, the same as flying. Run your route through our free time zone planner before you leave. It adjusts timing only, never dose amounts, and confirm the plan with your care team.

If you lose or damage insulin on the road, keep a copy of your prescriptions in your phone. Most US pharmacy chains can transfer a prescription to a store near you with a call from your doctor. And keep your travel insurance details somewhere you can find them without digging through email at a pharmacy counter.

Diabetes road trip checklist

Here is the full list, in this order:

Diabetes Road Trip: Pre-Drive Checklist

  • Check your glucose before driving; start above 90 mg/dL, and eat carbs first if you are in the 70s or 80s
  • Put fast-acting carbs in the console or door pocket, within arm's reach with the seatbelt on
  • Keep your CGM low alarm loud and your phone on a car charger
  • Recheck at every rest stop on drives over an hour
  • If you feel low: pull over, rule of 15, and do not drive again until above 100 mg/dL and feeling fully normal
  • Pack glucagon and show your copilot where it is before you leave
  • Wear a medical ID; keep an ICE contact and medication list in your phone
  • Keep insulin in the passenger cabin, never the trunk, and never leave it in a parked car
  • Bring insulin inside overnight; in cold weather keep it close to your body
  • Pack 20% extra supplies, and run multi-day routes through the time zone planner
  • Keep copies of your prescriptions in your phone in case you need a pharmacy transfer

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drive with type 1 diabetes?

Yes. Having diabetes does not disqualify you from driving. The American Diabetes Association's driving guidance is built around preparation: check your glucose before you drive and at regular intervals on longer drives, always carry fast-acting carbs in the car, and stop at the first sign of a low.

What should my blood sugar be before I start driving?

The American Diabetes Association recommends starting above 90 mg/dL. If you are about to start a long drive and you are sitting in the 70s or 80s, eat some carbs first rather than hoping the number holds for the next two hours.

What should I do if I feel low while driving?

Pull over safely as soon as you can. Follow the rule of 15: eat 15 grams of fast-acting carbs, wait 15 minutes, and recheck. Repeat if you are still below 70 mg/dL. Wait until you are above 100 mg/dL and feeling fully normal before driving again. The ADA notes that clear thinking can lag behind the number by 30 to 60 minutes, so do not rush back onto the road.

Where should I keep insulin in the car?

In the passenger cabin with you, never in the trunk, and never leave it in a parked car. Parked cars can top 130 degrees in summer sun, and heat-damaged insulin often looks normal while losing effectiveness. In cold weather, keep insulin close to your body: it freezes at 32 degrees Fahrenheit, and frozen insulin must be discarded.

Should I bring glucagon on a road trip?

If your care team has prescribed it, yes. Nasal glucagon (such as Baqsimi) is the travel-friendly option: no injection, stored at room temperature up to 86 degrees Fahrenheit. Show your copilot exactly where it is and how it works before you leave, per the manufacturer's instructions.
