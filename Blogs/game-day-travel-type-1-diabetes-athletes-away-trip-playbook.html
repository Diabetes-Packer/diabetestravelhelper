Game-Day Travel with Type 1 Diabetes: The Athlete's Away-Trip Playbook (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 9 min read

An away game is the hardest trip in youth and college sports, and the diabetes half of it is the part nobody puts on the team travel sheet. The flight, the hotel, the unfamiliar field, the game itself, and then the bus or the flight home, all with a pump and a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that read glucose every few minutes, insulin that has to stay cool, and a low that can show up mid-game in front of a crowd. This guide is the playbook for that trip: the kit list from the airport to the locker room, the glucose checks before, during, and after the game, treating a low from the stadium bag, getting the bag through the venue gate, and what heat and cold do to the tech on the field. Thanksgiving falls on Thursday, November 26, and if your team is traveling for a holiday tournament that week, pack this playbook with the uniform.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. This guide does not describe my own away-game travel, and it does not claim I have played competitive sports on the road. The athlete stories in it come from Dexcom's published survey and the athletes it names, and they are named wherever their words appear. Every number below comes from a published survey, the American Diabetes Association (ADA), the National Football League (NFL) bag policy, or the device makers, and every place where the evidence runs out is marked as such. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next away trip about your own game-day plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. Game-day insulin and food decisions belong to the athlete, the care team, and the coach, made before the trip, not on the sideline. If a low keeps happening during games, or fear of a low is the reason you are sitting out, tell your care team.

The news that started this guide arrived on October 6, 2026, when Dexcom launched season 5 of Dexcom U, its one-of-a-kind NIL (Name, Image and Likeness) program exclusively for college athletes with diabetes. The roster is the largest yet: 27 athletes with type 1 diabetes (T1D) across 13 sports, 12 new and 15 returning. The point of the program is simple: young athletes with diabetes need to see people like them playing at the highest level, and they need mentors who have done it. Dexcom pairs the college athletes with pro mentors, including Mason Miller of the San Diego Padres, Mark Andrews of the Baltimore Ravens, Adonai Mitchell of the New York Jets, Josh Gesky of the Green Bay Packers, and Jordan Morris of the Seattle Sounders. The rest of this guide is the practical half of that promise: what it actually takes to get to the game and play it.

1. The news: more athletes are staying in sports, and the hard part is still game day

Dexcom surveyed 1,004 U.S. adults with type 1 diabetes, or parents of children under 18 with type 1 diabetes, from July 8 to 14, 2026, through the dQ&A patient panel. Every number in this section comes from that survey, published as Dexcom, data on file, 2026. It is a company-commissioned survey, not independent science, and it should be read that way. But the direction it shows is worth knowing, because it is the first good news in this space in years.

The share of young adults who quit sports after diagnosis fell from 24 percent to 16 percent, while overall sports participation rose from 81 percent to 85 percent. Frequent loneliness fell from 28 percent to 15 percent, and feeling embarrassed because of type 1 diabetes fell from 20 percent to 12 percent. Seventy-seven percent of young adults say awareness of athletes with type 1 diabetes would help newly diagnosed people, 82 percent feel more represented seeing people with diabetes in sports and media, and among those surveyed who have a diabetes mentor, 92 percent report a positive experience. That is what Dexcom U is built on: the mentor matters as much as the device.

But the survey also names the wall that is still there. Among the young adults who did quit sports, 62 percent feared low glucose, and 54 percent found managing diabetes during activity too complicated, up from 42 percent. The fear is shrinking, but the complexity is growing, which makes sense: the tech got better and the management got more detailed at the same time. The survey has an answer to that too, and it is the strongest number in the whole release. People who started using a CGM within a year of diagnosis reported far lower rates of glucose-control difficulty (11 percent versus 28 percent), fear of lows (22 percent versus 54 percent), and mental burden (18 percent versus 34 percent) than people who started later. Early CGM use does not make the game easy. It makes the game knowable, and knowable is what this playbook is about.

Austin Howard, a Vanderbilt University football player and a first-year Dexcom U athlete, says it in his own words. He was diagnosed with type 1 diabetes when he was 15: "Football has been a part of my life for as long as I can remember, so when I was diagnosed with Type 1 diabetes when I was 15, it forced me to reimagine how I train and fuel my body. With the support of my coaches, I was able to come back stronger than ever." The coach is in that sentence for a reason. No athlete manages game day alone, and the coach who knows the plan is part of the plan.

2. The kit: from the airport to the locker room

An away trip has two bags, and they have different jobs. The travel bag gets you to the city. The game-day bag gets you through the game. Pack them separately, and pack the game-day bag first, because it is the one you will reach for under pressure.

The travel bag. Everything diabetes-related rides in your carry-on at your seat, never in checked baggage. That rule does not change because the trip is short. Insulin, pump supplies or pens, spare sensors, a fingerstick meter and strips, the glucagon device, sugar packets for lows, chargers and a power bank, a medical ID, and the travel letter from your care team. This site's rule is to pack 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says. A tournament trip earns the top end of that rule, because a tournament can run long: extra games, a delayed flight home, a pod that fails on day two. Our packing list does the counting, and the packing calculator on this site builds the list for your exact trip length.

At the airport. The checkpoint rules are the same for an athlete as for any traveler with diabetes, and they are written down. The Transportation Security Administration (TSA) allows diabetes supplies, equipment, medications, and food to treat a low through the checkpoint after screening or a pat-down. Tell the officer you have diabetes and that you are wearing a pump or a CGM before screening begins, and separate your diabetes supplies from your other belongings. You can ask for a pat-down instead of the body scanner, and you can ask for a private screening with a witness of your choice. Medical liquids, including insulin and juice for a low, are exempt from the 3.4 ounce limit: take them out of your bag and tell TSA. Never put insulin in checked baggage. A separate medical bag flies free and does not count toward your luggage limit. Our TSA rules guide covers the whole checkpoint, our pat-down rights guide covers the screening choice, and our Dexcom G7 airport guide covers what the scanner does and does not do to a sensor. If you want help at the airport, call TSA Cares at 1-855-787-2227 within 72 hours of your flight.

The game-day bag. This is the small bag that stays at the bench or on the sideline: sugar packets, the fingerstick meter, the glucagon device, a spare sensor and a spare pod if you use them, and a phone charger. Nothing in it is a spare for later. Everything in it is for the next two hours. Keep it where the trainer can reach it without asking you, and keep it out of the locker room during the game, because the locker room is where the bag is when you need it on the field. One more rule from the road: if a pod or sensor change would fall right before the flight or mid-game, do it the night before instead. A fresh pod or sensor can have issues right after insertion, and you would rather find that out at the hotel than at the gate or on the bench.

Set up the safety net before you leave. Turn on CGM sharing before the trip, so a parent, a coach, or a teammate back home can see your numbers while you play. Our CGM sharing playbook covers the setup and the airplane mode trap that pauses sharing. And tell the people who need to know: the coach, the trainer, and at least one teammate. They do not need your full medical history. They need three facts: where the low kit is, what a low looks like in you, and where the glucagon device is. That conversation takes two minutes and it is the most important prep on this list.

3. Game-day glucose: before, during, and after

Exercise changes glucose in both directions, and the direction depends on the sport, the timing, and the insulin already on board. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) position statement on exercise and diabetes is the reference for this section, and it starts with a warning that is worth hearing plainly: glucose responses to activity in type 1 diabetes are highly variable, and there is no single formula that works for every athlete. What follows is the routine the guidance describes, not a prescription. Your numbers and your plan come from your care team.

Before the game. Check your glucose before exercise. The ADA position statement says glucose concentrations should always be checked before exercise by people with type 1 diabetes, and gives 90 to 250 mg/dL as the ideal range before exercise. Ask your care team what range they want you to aim for, because the range for one athlete may differ. Check early enough that a number outside the range leaves time to act, not at the whistle. Eat before the game in your usual way, and take insulin for the meal in your usual way. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and that routine does not change because the meal is in a hotel or a team bus.

During the game. The ADA notes that additional carbohydrate intake or insulin changes are typically needed to keep glucose steady during and after activity, and that frequent glucose checks are required to make those calls. That is the whole game-day routine in one sentence: check often, and have fast-acting carbohydrate in reach. A CGM helps here, because it shows the trend, not just the number, and the ADA notes that CGM during activity can detect hypoglycemia when used as an addition to fingerstick tests, not as a replacement for them. If the CGM says one thing and your body says another, check with the meter. Team sports have one advantage the ADA names: mixed activities, such as interval training or team and field sports, are associated with better glucose stability than activities that are mostly steady aerobic exercise. The stop and start of a game is easier on glucose than a long steady run, which is a small piece of good news for field and court athletes.

After the game. The game does not end at the final whistle for glucose. The ADA position statement notes that glucose uptake can stay elevated after exercise, by insulin-dependent mechanisms for up to 48 hours if the exercise is prolonged, which is why a low can arrive hours after the game, including overnight. Check after the game, check before bed on game night, and keep sugar packets on the nightstand. That overnight check is the one athletes skip most, and it is the one that matters most after a tournament day with two games. If the trip crosses time zones, reset your pump clock to local time on landing with the time zone guide, because a pump running on home time will dose on the wrong schedule all weekend.

One rule about devices and competition. The ADA notes that in certain sports, such as basketball or contact sports, wearing pumps and other devices may be prohibited during competition. Check with the coach and the league before the trip, not at the venue. If a device has to come off for the game, that is a care-team conversation before the trip, with a written plan for the hours it is off, not an improvisation on the sideline.

4. Treating a low from the stadium bag

A low during a game is not a failure. It is the most predictable event in this guide, and the plan for it should be the most rehearsed. Here is the plan, in order.

Step one: treat it from the bag at the bench. Keep sugar packets in the game-day bag and in your pocket: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. Do not go back to the locker room for them, and do not wait for halftime. A low treated early is a short break. A low treated late is the rest of the game.

Step two: tell someone. Tell the coach or the trainer the moment you feel low, and tell a teammate too. The Dexcom survey says 62 percent of the young adults who quit sports feared low glucose. The fear gets smaller when the low is not a secret. A teammate who knows what a low looks like in you is a safety net that no device can replace.

Step three: know where the glucagon device is, and who can use it. The glucagon device is for a severe low, when you cannot treat yourself. Someone on the team has to know where it is and how to use it, and that someone cannot be you. Our glucagon guide covers the device, the storage, and the conversation to have with the coach before the trip. If a severe low happens, tell the people around you, treat it with the plan your care team set, and seek medical care after.

Step four: check again before the next meal. After a low, check your glucose again before your next meal, and do not assume the low is finished because you feel better. The fast-acting carbohydrate works quickly, and the game keeps going, so the check after the low is part of the treatment, not an extra.

5. Getting through the gate: venue bag policies

The airport is not the only gate on an away trip. The stadium has one too, and it has its own bag policy. This is the part of the playbook where the only honest rule is to check before the trip, because policies vary by venue, by league, and by event.

The NFL policy, as a worked example. The NFL clear-bag policy is the best-documented venue policy in American sports, and it shows how a medical exception works. Fans may carry a bag that is clear plastic, vinyl, or PVC and does not exceed 12 inches by 6 inches by 12 inches, a one-gallon clear plastic freezer bag, or a small clutch that does not exceed 4.5 inches by 6.5 inches. The policy makes exceptions for all medically necessary items after proper inspection, and it tells fans to visit the team website for guidelines specific to the stadium. That is the pattern to expect: a size rule, a clear-bag rule, and a medical exception that you have to ask for and be inspected for. Bring the travel letter from your care team, ask for the medical screening gate when you arrive, and arrive early enough that the inspection does not make you miss warm-ups.

For every other venue, check first. School gyms, college fields, and tournament sites do not follow the NFL policy, and their rules vary more, not less. Check the venue bag policy on the host school or tournament page before the trip, and call or email the host if the page does not say. A medical exception is commonly available but must be confirmed with the venue, and the confirmation should come before the trip, not at the gate. If the venue will not allow the full game-day bag, ask what it will allow, and rebuild the bag around that answer: the sugar packets and the meter in a pocket or a clear bag, the glucagon device with the trainer, the spares with the team staff. The bag is negotiable. The low kit is not.

6. Heat and cold on the field

A tournament weekend can put the same athlete on a hot field on Saturday and a cold one on Sunday, and the diabetes tech feels both. The rules here come from the device makers, and they are narrower than most people assume.

Heat. A Dexcom G7 sensor worn on the body operates from 50F to 107.6F, but spare sensors must be stored from 36F to 86F, in a cool dry place, kept in their sterile packaging. The spares are the weak point. A bag left in the sun on the sideline or in a hot car between games can push past the 86F storage ceiling fast, and a sensor that baked in the heat may look fine and fail on day two. Keep the spares in the bag with you, in the shade, never in a parked car. Keep insulin cool and out of direct sun too: our insulin cooling guide covers the options. The other heat problem is adhesion. Sweat loosens the adhesive faster than anything else, which is why sensors peel off on hot tournament days. Dexcom says to use the overpatch that comes with each sensor, and it is required to keep the sensor on for the full wear. Our adhesion playbook covers the full routine, and our G7 heat and cold guide covers the temperature numbers in full.

Cold. A worn sensor sits against warm skin under your clothes, so normal cold days do not push it out of range, and body heat does the work for you. The spares are the concern again: do not leave them in a freezing car overnight or in an unheated equipment bag on a cold field, because the storage floor is 36F. The weak link in the cold is your phone. Phones give up in extreme cold long before the sensor does, and if your phone is your pump controller, a dead phone means no pump controls and no CGM readings. Keep the phone in an inside pocket, close to your body, on very cold days, and carry a power bank, because cold drains batteries faster. If you use an Omnipod 5, plan the pod changes around the tournament schedule: a Pod lasts up to 72 hours, so a three-day tournament needs a change mid-trip, and that change should happen at the hotel, not at the field.

When the numbers look wrong. Outside its operating range, a sensor may throw errors, drop signal, or give readings you cannot trust. The rule does not change with the weather: if the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. Use your fingerstick meter until the sensor settles or you replace it. That is the standing guidance for any suspicious reading, and a hot or cold field is exactly the kind of situation it exists for.

7. The trip home, and the next one

The trip home is part of the playbook, not the end of it. The overnight low after a tournament day is the most likely low of the whole trip, because the exercise is done and the glucose uptake is still elevated. Check before bed on the last night, keep the sugar packets on the nightstand, and keep CGM sharing on until you are home, so someone can see the numbers on the flight or the drive. If the trip crossed time zones, reset the pump clock to local time on landing, and read the trip in your CGM data the next day with the post-trip reset playbook. Then restock before the next trip, because the next tournament is usually closer than it looks.

That is the loop the Dexcom U athletes are in: travel, play, check, restock, travel again. The survey says the fear is fading and the participation is rising, and the reason is not that the management got simpler. It got more detailed, and the athletes who stay in it are the ones with a written plan, a coach who knows it, and a CGM that shows them the trend before the low arrives. If you are the athlete, build the plan before the trip. If you are the parent, build it with them. If you are the coach, ask for it. The game is hard enough without diabetes being a surprise.

Game-Day Travel with Type 1 Diabetes: The Away-Trip Checklist

  • Four to six weeks before a tournament trip: book the pre-trip appointment with your care team, bring the itinerary and the game schedule, and leave with a travel letter, a game-day glucose plan, and a plan for a device that has to come off during competition; our pre-trip appointment guide lists the questions to ask
  • Pack 20% extra supplies, more if the tournament can run long; all insulin and supplies in the carry-on at your seat, never in checked baggage; divide supplies between bags
  • Game-day bag at the bench or sideline: sugar packets (1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60), fingerstick meter, glucagon device, spare sensor and spare pod, phone charger and power bank; coach, trainer, and one teammate know where it is
  • Within 72 hours of the flight: call TSA Cares at 1-855-787-2227 if you want help at the checkpoint; carry the travel letter; check the venue bag policy on the host page and confirm the medical exception with the venue
  • Night before: pack both bags, charge every device, set up glucose sharing so someone at home can see your numbers, and do any pod or sensor change that would otherwise fall mid-flight or mid-game
  • At the airport: tell the officer you have diabetes before screening begins; ask for a pat-down or a private screening if you prefer it; keep the low kit at your seat, not in the overhead bin
  • On game day: check glucose before the game, during long sessions, and after; keep fast-acting carbohydrate in reach; tell the coach or trainer the moment you feel low; check again before the next meal after a low
  • Heat and cold: spare sensors in the bag with you, in the shade, never in a parked car; insulin cool and out of direct sun; overpatch on the sensor; phone in an inside pocket on cold days; if readings do not match how you feel, use the fingerstick meter
