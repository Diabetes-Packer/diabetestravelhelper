United Kingdom Travel Guide

Traveling with Diabetes in the United Kingdom: The Pharmacy, the Food, and the Trip (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

London in July, Edinburgh in August, the Lake District in September: the United Kingdom is the easiest big trip an American with diabetes can take, and it still has rules that catch people out. It is also the sixth country on this site to get its own destination guide, after Japan, Mexico, Spain, Italy, and France, and the reason is simple. The United Kingdom is the one country in this series where the diabetes rules are mostly familiar: the insulin brand names are the same, the language is English, and the airport has a card made for your devices. The differences are specific, and they are the ones this guide covers: a new entry permission you must get before you fly, a controlled-drug rule that does not cover insulin, a pharmacy system that needs a local prescription, and a food label that hides the carbs from the front of the pack. None of it is hard. All of it is specific. This guide covers the import rules, the pharmacy, and the trip itself, in that order, so you can sort the paperwork at home and enjoy the trip once you land.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. I have not traveled to the United Kingdom with diabetes. This guide is not a trip report, and it does not pretend to be one. It is built from the published guidance of the United Kingdom government, the Civil Aviation Authority (CAA), the agency that regulates aviation in the United Kingdom, the National Health Service (NHS), the public health service in the United Kingdom, the United States Department of State, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Diabetes UK, the charity for people with diabetes in the United Kingdom, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), the body that sets treatment guidance in England and Wales, and the House of Commons Library. Where a rule comes from one of those sources, this guide says so. Where something is my own travel habit, it is labeled as mine, and it is kept separate from the official rules. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own travel plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not going, tell your care team.

1. The import rule in one paragraph, then the details

The United Kingdom does not run a certificate system like Japan's. There is no import form to file before you fly, and no office that stamps your insulin. The paperwork is a prescription and a letter, and the rule depends on what the medicine is. The United Kingdom government page on taking medicine in or out of the United Kingdom sets the traveler rule: medicine containing a controlled drug must be carried in your hand luggage, and it may be taken away at the border if you cannot prove it was prescribed for you. If you are not a United Kingdom resident and you bring medicine in, you need a letter of proof that the medicine was prescribed for you. The letter must include your name, the dates you are traveling, a list of your medicine including how much you have, the doses and the strength, and the signature of the person who prescribed it. You can only bring up to 3 months supply of a controlled drug, and if you need more than that, a United Kingdom doctor or pharmacist must prescribe the rest once you are in the country. Our customs and border rules guide covers the general version of this system for any country, and our international travel guide covers the wide version of traveling abroad with diabetes. The United Kingdom is where the general version gets specific.

Here is the part that matters most for insulin: insulin is not a controlled drug in the United Kingdom. The controlled-drug rules above cover medicines the law regulates because they can be misused, such as opioid painkillers, benzodiazepines, and stimulants. Insulin is not on that list, so no Home Office licence applies to it, and the 3-month limit does not apply to insulin either. That does not mean you travel light on paperwork. The National Travel Health Network and Centre (NaTHNaC), which runs TravelHealthPro, the United Kingdom travel health service, advises travelers to carry medicine in its original pharmacy packaging, carry a copy of the prescription, and carry a letter from the prescriber that lists medicines by their generic names. A letter can help at border checks and if you need to replace medicine or get medical help abroad. The CDC makes the same point on its United Kingdom packing list: bring copies of all prescriptions, make sure the prescriptions include generic names, and pack extras of important health supplies in case of travel delays. If the trip runs longer than the supply you can carry, our long-stay abroad playbook covers the planning for a trip that outlasts the supply in your bag.

Two more rules from the State Department page, and one new one. First, the United Kingdom is not in the Schengen area, so the Schengen passport rules do not apply, but you still need a passport valid for the duration of your stay, with 1 blank page. No vaccine is required to enter the United Kingdom. Second, the new rule: all United States citizens traveling to the United Kingdom for tourism, family visits, business meetings, conferences, or short-term study for 6 months or less must have an Electronic Travel Authorization (ETA) before travel. An ETA costs 20 pounds per person, including children, and it is valid for multiple journeys over two years or until your passport expires, for stays of up to 6 months at a time. Apply on the UK ETA app or online at GOV.UK, and expect a decision usually within 3 working days. The fee rose to 20 pounds on 8 April 2026, and from 25 February 2026 the rule is strictly enforced, which means carriers will not board eligible visitors without one. If you hold British or Irish citizenship as well, you must enter on a United Kingdom or Irish passport and cannot obtain an ETA on a foreign passport. Our travel letter guide covers what the letter should say, and our pre-trip appointment guide covers the full appointment, including asking for generic-name prescriptions before you leave. A prescription that lists the generic name is the one that travels. A brand name that only exists in your home country does not.

2. Insulin at the border: pens, syringes, and what to carry

Insulin travels the same way it travels everywhere: in the bag that stays with you, in its original box with the pharmacy label on it. Syringes have one packing rule of their own. Keep the syringes with the injectable medicine they accompany, and make sure the doctor's letter mentions them. The CDC says needles and syringes for diabetes need a letter from your doctor on letterhead stationery. Do not plan to bring a loose syringe on its own. Pack the syringes with the insulin they belong to, and list them in the letter, so the letter and the bag agree with each other. Liquid medicine in a container over 100 ml needs proof it is prescribed to you at United Kingdom airport security, a letter from your doctor or a copy of your prescription, and security staff may open the container to screen it, so keep that proof where you can reach it.

Two habits from my own travel routine, kept separate from the border rules. Everything diabetes-related rides in my carry-on, never in checked luggage, and I pack 20 percent extra of everything: pods, sensors, insulin, test strips, lancets, ketone strips, and the low kit. Our packing list does the counting for the trip length. The 20 percent buffer covers the pod that fails on a rainy afternoon in London and the sensor that peels off in the humidity on a summer day in Edinburgh. Bring backup insulin for injections in case a pod fails mid-trip, and keep your insulin plan written down somewhere that is not the pump. The flight there follows the same airport rules as any trip, covered in our TSA rules guide for the United States departure and our airport security abroad guide for the arrival side, and the United Kingdom arrival has its own card, covered in section 5.

3. What needs extra paperwork, and what is not sold here

Some medicines need extra paperwork before you travel, and no letter fixes a missing prescription. The United Kingdom regulates controlled drugs at the border through the Home Office. The rule for those medicines is plain: carry them in your hand luggage, carry a letter from your prescriber with your name, your travel dates, and a list of your medicines with doses, strength, and quantities, and bring no more than 3 months supply. If you bring more than 3 months supply, or get more medicine posted to you, it will be taken away. That covers opioid painkillers, benzodiazepines and sleeping pills, and stimulants, including methylphenidate, which is a Schedule 2 controlled drug in the United Kingdom. One category of controlled drug needs more than a letter: Schedule 1 drugs cannot be brought into the United Kingdom without a licence, so contact the Drug and Firearms Licensing Unit (DFLU) before you travel if any medicine you take falls in that group. Check every medicine by its active ingredient, not its brand name, before you pack. The active ingredient is what the law sees. The brand name is not.

Adderall is the one that catches travelers by surprise. Adderall, the mixed amphetamine salts used for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), is not licensed in the United Kingdom. No United Kingdom pharmacy can supply it, because it holds no United Kingdom marketing authorisation. The nearest medicines in the United Kingdom are dexamfetamine and lisdexamfetamine, sold as Elvanse, and methylphenidate is sold as Ritalin, Concerta, Equasym XL, and Medikinet. If you take Adderall, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about switching to a medicine that is sold in the United Kingdom, and arrange the paperwork for the medicine you carry. The answer cannot be found at the border.

Cannabis is a separate problem. Cannabis is a Class B drug under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971, and it is illegal to possess, produce, supply, import, or export cannabis in the United Kingdom. Since November 2018, cannabis-based medicines can be prescribed by specialist doctors, and two such medicines, Sativex and Epidyolex, hold United Kingdom marketing authorisations, but that is a specialist prescription route, not a travel route. Do not bring cannabis or tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) products into the United Kingdom. Leave them at home.

4. The pharmacy: insulin needs a prescription, and yours from home does not work here

The pharmacy on the high street is called a chemist, and you will find one on most high streets in London, Edinburgh, and every town in between. Boots pharmacies are on high streets across the country, and supermarket pharmacies inside Tesco, Asda, and Morrisons usually stay open later than the independents. Sunday hours vary: many pharmacies close early on Sunday and some are closed, so check the hours before you walk, and NHS 111 holds the bank-holiday rota. The first thing to understand is the rule that matters most: insulin is not sold over the counter. It needs a prescription. The same rule holds in Spain, Italy, and France, our third, fourth, and fifth country guides, where insulin also needs a local prescription.

So the pharmacy visit, if you need one, has a fixed sequence. First, see a doctor in the United Kingdom. A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in the United Kingdom: a United Kingdom pharmacy does not work from American paper. A General Practitioner (GP), the family doctor, may agree to see you as a temporary patient, and a private doctor is the faster route if no practice will take you. Bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product. Tell the doctor the generic name of your insulin, not just the brand: insulin aspart, insulin lispro, insulin glargine, insulin degludec. The pharmacist and the doctor both work from the generic name, and the generic name is what survives the brand change at the border. The good news is that the brand names are the same as at home. The NHS lists NovoRapid and Humalog among its rapid-acting insulins, and Lantus and Tresiba among its long-acting insulins, so NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid in the United Kingdom, and both are insulin aspart. Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine. Our finding insulin abroad playbook covers the name game in full, including the concentration check that matters more than the brand name.

Two more things to know before you walk in. First, most insulin pens in the United Kingdom use the same U-100 standard, 100 units in each milliliter, so a pen dial counts the same way it does at home. Tresiba is the exception to watch for: it also comes in a pen at 200 units per milliliter, so check the concentration printed on the box before you use it, because a syringe is calibrated for one concentration only. Second, visitors pay. In England, the prescription charge is 9.90 pounds per item, a figure frozen since 1 May 2024 and frozen again for 2025 to 2026. Prescriptions are free in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. Medical care is not free for visitors in the United Kingdom, and United States Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for medical care outside the United States. Keep every receipt for your travel insurance claim, and check your policy before the trip so the clinic visit is not the first time you read it. Our travel insurance guide covers what to declare and what to look for.

5. Your devices in the United Kingdom: the app, the sensors, and the airport

Start with the phone, because it holds the one trap that ruins more international trips than any other. The Dexcom G7 app is region-locked at install: Dexcom's own user guide states the app must be installed in your country of residence, and the app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when you first install it. The check happens at install time. An app that is already installed and working keeps working abroad, and travelers report normal operation on international trips as long as nobody deletes the app mid-trip. So install the G7 app and update it to the latest version before you leave, turn off auto-updates before the trip so nothing about your diabetes setup changes mid-trip without your say-so, and do not delete the app while you are in the United Kingdom. A deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country, and that is a long trip without CGM data. Our G7 app region-lock guide covers the full prep list, and nothing in it is specific to the United Kingdom: the same rule applies everywhere you land.

The receiver is the backup that makes the app problem a non-issue. Dexcom's own international travel guidance says that if you have a receiver, it makes a great backup to your phone app. The receiver has no app store, no region check, and no install step. It pairs with your sensor and shows readings anywhere on earth. Pack it with its USB cable and charger, plus a plug adapter for the United Kingdom. Our devices charging guide covers plugs and power on the road. If you do not already own a receiver, ask Dexcom about getting one before a big international trip. Bring the fingerstick meter too, as the final fallback, and keep it in your day bag rather than buried in your hotel room.

Buying replacement sensors in the United Kingdom is a pharmacy problem, not a shop problem. The G7 is sold in the United Kingdom: the Omnipod 5 can now connect with the Dexcom G7 in the United Kingdom, and the Omnipod 5 is approved there for people with type 1 diabetes aged two years and older, available on the NHS for those who are eligible. The Omnipod 5 became available in the United Kingdom in 2023, and the NHS England rollout of hybrid closed loop systems started in April 2024. A replacement sensor bought in the United Kingdom still means a foreign pharmacy, a local prescription, and regional compatibility questions you do not want to solve on vacation. Pack more sensors than the trip math says you need, with the spares in your carry-on, and do not plan to shop. The same applies to pump supplies. Carry spare Pods and a backup injection plan with syringes and a vial, and keep your basal rates written down somewhere that is not the pump. Our pod failure backup plan covers the sequence when a Pod fails away from home.

The United Kingdom airport is the one place in this series where the screening rules are written down on a card made for you. The Medical Device Awareness Card was launched in 2019, sponsored by the Civil Aviation Authority (CAA) and the Airport Operators Association (AOA), and you can print it from the CAA website before you fly. The card tells the security officer what you are wearing and what the rules are, and it tells you what to do. The CAA guidance is plain: insulin pumps and CGMs must not be screened by x-ray or pass through the security scanner, and they are screened by alternative methods instead. You should never be asked to remove a medical device from your body for screening. If you are carrying a spare device, take it out of your cabin bag before the x-ray and let the security officer know, so it gets a hand search instead. Insulin pens can go through all the airport scanners and detectors. If you wear a Sunflower lanyard, the hidden disability lanyard many United Kingdom airports provide, staff know you may need extra help or understanding. Show the card as you reach the checkpoint, tell the officer what you are wearing and where it is, and ask for the alternative screening if the scanner is the next step. Our Omnipod 5 airport guide covers the United States side of the same trip, and our scanner comparison guide covers which machine is safe for which device.

6. Time zones: one clock, and the eight-hour gap

The United Kingdom keeps one clock. London, Edinburgh, Cardiff, and Belfast all use Greenwich Mean Time (GMT), which is the same as Coordinated Universal Time, written as UTC+0, in winter, and British Summer Time (BST), UTC+1, in summer. The clocks move on the same dates as the rest of Europe: forward on the last Sunday in March and back on the last Sunday in October. In 2026 that means 29 March and 25 October. The United Kingdom is one hour behind Paris, Rome, Madrid, and the rest of continental Europe, so a trip that pairs London with Paris needs two clock settings, not one. The gap between the United Kingdom and home changes only because home moves its clocks, not because the United Kingdom does. From the United States West Coast, London is 8 hours ahead, and from the United States East Coast, London is 5 hours ahead. The gap narrows by one hour for about two to three weeks each spring and about one week in late October to early November, when the United States and the United Kingdom change their clocks on different Sundays, so check the current gap in the week you fly. Either way, it is a large enough jump that the insulin timing needs a plan before the flight, not after landing.

The plan follows the rule in our time zone insulin guide. If you use a pump, change the pump clock to local time when you land, so your basal rates, the background insulin the pump drips all day, run on local time. Most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, so this is one manual step at the gate or the hotel. If you take multiple daily injections, keep your long-acting insulin on home time on the travel day, then switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone when you arrive, for example bedtime. Meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch: take it when you eat, wherever you are. The guide covers the eastbound and westbound cases in full, and the timing shift for a United Kingdom trip is large enough that the careful planning earns its keep. Run your route through our time zone planner before you fly, and confirm the plan with your care team. The planner adjusts timing only, never dose amounts. Dose changes are a conversation with your care team, not something a tool or a blog post should decide.

7. Eating in the United Kingdom: the label that hides the carbs

The United Kingdom is one of the easiest countries in the world to count carbs in, once you know where to look, and one of the hardest if you do not. The split is between the front of the pack and the back of the pack, and the supermarket sits squarely on the easy side once you flip it over. The front of the pack carries the traffic light label, a voluntary system most major supermarkets use. The label shows fat, saturates, sugars, and salt in green, amber, or red, with the share of an adult's reference intake. What it does not show is carbohydrates. Diabetes UK says it plainly: carbohydrates are not included in the front-of-pack information, because there is no set rule for what counts as low, medium, or high carbohydrate in a food. So the traffic lights will never tell you the carb count, and a green light does not mean a low-carb food. The back of the pack is where the carbs live. The nutrition declaration is mandatory on pre-packed food, and it must list energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, and salt, stated per 100 grams or per 100 milliliters. The carbohydrate line reads carbohydrate, the same word as at home, and many packs add a per-portion column too. Compare two products by the per 100 gram column, not the per-portion column, because the portions are whatever each maker chose. This guide gives no carb counts for specific foods, because the number on the package in front of you is the one that counts, and it is printed right there.

Restaurant food has no label, so it works the way eating out works everywhere. Ask what is in the dish, estimate the carbohydrate from the bread, the potatoes, or the pastry, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. I pre-bolus in my usual way before meals. That habit travels with me, and the United Kingdom does not change it. A pub meal often comes with chips and bread on the side, so the side dishes count too: estimate them with the rest of the meal, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. Our eating out playbook covers the ordering and estimating routine in full, and our pre-bolus guide covers the timing habit. For lows, keep sugar packets in your day bag. I treat lows with sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, and that is the low kit that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day in any country. Do not let the low kit be something you buy after you need it. Pack it before you leave the hotel.

Water is the other half of eating safely in the United Kingdom, and the news is good. Tap water in the United Kingdom is safe to drink everywhere, and it is tested against exacting standards by the Drinking Water Inspectorate, with compliance consistently above 99.9 percent. In a pub or restaurant, licensed premises must provide free tap water on request, so ask for a glass of tap water and skip the bottle. A stomach bug is a diabetes problem, not just a comfort problem, because vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the plan for that day.

Vaccines are a question for the care-team appointment, not for the airport. No vaccine is required to enter the United Kingdom, and the appointment is the place to check that routine vaccines are up to date. Our travel vaccines playbook covers the appointment in full.

8. Summer in the United Kingdom: mild days, the odd heatwave, and the hotel fridge

Summer is the season this guide is written for, and the United Kingdom summer is mild by American standards. London July afternoons average a high of 75F, with nights near 58F, on the Met Office normals for the Heathrow station, and July is the warmest month. July also brings about 8 days of rain, usually brief, so a light rain layer belongs in the day bag next to the sugar packets. Heatwaves happen in some years, pushing past 82F, and many hotels and most Tube lines have no air conditioning, so a hot spell feels hotter than the number suggests. The CDC's heat guidance says the same thing in plainer terms: drink plenty of fluids, wear loose, lightweight, light-colored clothing, plan outdoor activities for the cooler parts of the day, and rest often in the shade. If you are outside for many hours in the heat, eat salty snacks and drink water to stay hydrated.

All-day walking is the other half of the summer plan, and it pulls glucose down. A day that starts at the Tower, crosses London on foot, and ends with dinner across town can mean hours on foot without trying. Activity lowers glucose, so check more often than usual on a walking day, carry sugar packets in your pocket rather than in the bag you left at the hotel, and keep a snack in the day bag for the walk back. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. The low kit is the same one that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day: sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60.

Insulin follows the same heat rule it follows everywhere. Unopened insulin belongs in the fridge at 36 to 46F, and in-use insulin is fine at room temperature below 77F. A London afternoon in a heatwave can sit above 77F, so in-use insulin needs its cooling wallet from the moment you leave the hotel. Our insulin cooling guide covers the methods, and our hotel insulin storage guide covers the minibar trap in detail: hotel minibar fridges can freeze items at the back, so keep unopened insulin away from the cooling element and check that it has not frozen. Insulin freezes at 32F, and frozen insulin must be discarded.

Then there is the sun, which is the one place where your devices meet a real limit on a United Kingdom trip. Dexcom publishes exact temperature numbers for the G7. The sensor's operating range is 50F to 107.6F, and its storage range is 36F to 86F. A worn sensor sits against warm skin, so a normal day does not push it out of range, but a long day in direct sun can push a spare past its storage ceiling. Keep sun exposure short where you can, and check the readings against your fingerstick meter after a long hot day. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. Heat loosens adhesive too. Press the sensor and pod edges down firmly after swimming, and pack extra overpatches. Our G7 heat and cold guide has the full temperature table, and our adhesion playbook covers keeping a sensor and a pump stuck through holiday trips.

If the trip is a winter trip instead, the Scottish Highlands bring the cold routine: a worn sensor sits against warm skin under your clothes, so normal winter days do not push it out of its operating range, but spare sensors must be stored between 36F and 86F, so do not leave them in a freezing car overnight. Our skiing playbook covers the cold and altitude routine for a ski trip, and the same habits apply to a cold morning walking around Edinburgh or York.

9. If something goes wrong: numbers, clinics, and the insurance call

Save these numbers in your phone before you land. Dial 999 for any emergency. It is the number for the ambulance, the police, and the fire brigade, and it is free from any phone. Dial 112 as well: it works in the United Kingdom and across Europe. If you need urgent health advice but it is not an emergency, call NHS 111, which runs 24 hours a day, seven days a week, and is free from landlines and mobiles. NHS 111 can connect you to a pharmacy, an out-of-hours GP appointment, a minor injuries unit, or an urgent treatment centre. If you need the United States Embassy, call +44-20-7499-9000 in London. The consulates in Edinburgh and Belfast are listed in the State Department page if your trip takes you to Scotland or Northern Ireland. The United Kingdom has no reciprocal healthcare agreement with the United States, so there is no card that covers you. Emergency treatment in Accident and Emergency is free for everyone, and a GP appointment is free if a practice agrees to see you as a temporary patient, but once you are admitted to a ward or booked for follow-up treatment, the hospital will charge you. In England, overseas visitors are charged 150 percent of the NHS rate for admitted care. Check your travel insurance before the trip, so the call is not the first time you read it, and keep every receipt from any clinic or pharmacy visit.

If you develop symptoms of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous complication where the blood turns acidic when insulin runs too low, get urgent help right away. Vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation, and that is a hospital visit, not a wait-and-see day. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the ketone checks and the plan for that day. Carry glucagon in your day bag, and make sure your travel companion knows where it is and how to use it. Our glucagon emergency kit guide covers what is in the kit and how to keep it travel-ready. If the trip goes wrong in a bigger way, a bag lost or stolen, our lost or stolen supplies playbook covers the exact call order, and the travel insurance emergency line is the call that arranges care, including a local doctor visit. If the trip involves driving, your United States licence works for 12 months from your last entry, and no International Driving Permit (IDP) is legally required, because your licence is printed in English. Remember that the United Kingdom drives on the left, and carry your licence and your passport with you. Our driving abroad guide covers licenses, rental cars, and the rules of the road.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I buy insulin over the counter in the United Kingdom?

A: No. Insulin needs a prescription in the United Kingdom, and a United States prescription cannot be filled there. See a doctor in the United Kingdom, show your prescription and your doctor's letter so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the United Kingdom prescription to a pharmacy, called a chemist. The brand names are the same as at home: NovoRapid, Humalog, Lantus, and Tresiba are all prescribed in the United Kingdom.

Q: Will UK customs accept my syringes and my prescription from home?

A: Yes, for your own personal use. Insulin is not a controlled drug in the United Kingdom, so no Home Office licence applies to it. Carry your insulin in its original boxes with the pharmacy labels, carry a copy of your prescription and a letter from your prescriber that lists your medicines by their generic names, and keep the syringes with the insulin they accompany. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says needles and syringes for diabetes need a letter from your doctor on letterhead stationery.

Q: Can a UK pharmacy fill my prescription from home?

A: No. A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in the United Kingdom. If you run out of insulin, see a doctor in the United Kingdom, show your prescription and doctor's letter so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the United Kingdom prescription to a pharmacy. In England, the prescription charge is 9.90 pounds per item; prescriptions are free in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland.

Q: What medicines need extra paperwork, and what about Adderall?

A: Medicines that are controlled drugs need the most paperwork: carry them in your hand luggage, carry a letter from your prescriber with your name, your travel dates, and a list of your medicines with doses and strength, and bring no more than 3 months supply. That covers opioid painkillers, benzodiazepines, and stimulants such as methylphenidate. Adderall is not licensed in the United Kingdom, so no UK pharmacy can supply it. The nearest UK medicines are dexamfetamine and lisdexamfetamine, sold as Elvanse. If you take Adderall, talk to your prescriber well before the trip. Cannabis is illegal in the United Kingdom, so do not bring cannabis or THC products.

Q: Will my Dexcom G7 app work in the United Kingdom?

A: Yes, if it was already installed and set up at home. The Dexcom G7 app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when it is first installed, so install and update it before you leave and do not delete it while you are abroad: a deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country. Bring the Dexcom receiver as backup. It has no app store and no install check, and it pairs with your sensor anywhere. The G7 is sold in the United Kingdom, and the Omnipod 5 can now connect with the Dexcom G7 there.

Q: What are the emergency numbers in the United Kingdom?

A: Dial 999 for any emergency. It is the number for the ambulance, the police, and the fire brigade, and it is free from any phone. Dial 112 as well: it works in the United Kingdom and across Europe. If you need urgent health advice but it is not an emergency, call NHS 111, which runs 24 hours a day and is free. If you need the United States Embassy, call +44-20-7499-9000 in London. Emergency treatment in Accident and Emergency is free for everyone, but hospital treatment after that is charged to overseas visitors, so travel insurance is the cover that matters.
