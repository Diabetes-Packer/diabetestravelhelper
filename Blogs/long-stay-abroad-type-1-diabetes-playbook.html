Long-Stay Guide

Long Stays Abroad with Type 1 Diabetes: Beyond the 90-Day Supply Rule (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 10 min read

Every guide on this site so far assumes a trip: one or two weeks, a bag packed once, insulin counted once, and home again before anything runs out. A semester abroad is not a trip. A 3 to 6 month sabbatical is not a trip. A winter snowbird stay, a working holiday, a year of study in another country: these are moves, and a move breaks the system a trip relies on. The border limit, the supply count, the insurance policy, and the routine all assume you are coming back soon. Past about 90 days, each of those assumptions quietly stops working, and the fix is not a bigger suitcase. It is a local system: a local doctor, a local prescription, the right insurance for the length of stay, and a routine that lives in the new place instead of in your luggage. November is when these stays get made. Winter long stays are being booked now, and spring semester-abroad plans are being filed now. If yours is one of them, this guide is the system to build before you go.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes (T1D) for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. I have not done a semester abroad or a 6-month sabbatical, and this guide does not pretend I have. The long-stay stories in it belong to the travelers who published them, and they are named wherever their words appear: Nadia Halfway, who did five Erasmus stays with type 1 diabetes; the writer at type1travelling.com, who turned a year-long working holiday in Australia into 15 months across seven countries; and the family at Hippocratic Adventures, who did four 3 to 5 month sabbaticals with a son who has type 1 diabetes. Every number below comes from an official source, a manufacturer, or one of those travelers, and every place where the evidence runs out is marked as such. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before a long stay about your own plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. A stay of months needs a pre-trip appointment further out than a holiday does, because the prescriptions, the insurance, and the local doctor all take time to arrange. If you are unsure whether a different insulin is safe for you, or what to do when your usual insulin is not sold where you are going, ask your care team before you travel, not at a pharmacy counter.

1. Why 90 days is the wall

Ask why every diabetes travel guide stops at two weeks, and the answer is a number nobody prints on a packing list: about 90 days. That is the quantity most countries will let a visitor bring in for personal use, and it is the length most travel insurance policies are built for. A two-week trip never meets either limit. A 6-month stay meets both on day one, and the trip playbook has no answer for what happens next. The wall is not a single rule. It is the same number showing up in five different places at once: the border, the pharmacy, the insurance policy, the prescription, and the routine. Here is the border half of it, verified against the official sources.

The pattern is consistent enough to plan around, and our customs and border rules guide covers it in full. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) will allow foreign nationals visiting the US to bring or ship a 90-day supply of drug products, and if the stay runs longer than 90 days, additional medication may be sent to them, with documentation showing it is for the visitor's own use: a copy of the visa or passport, a letter from a doctor, and a copy of the prescription in English. The UK caps what a visitor may bring in at three months, and the GOV.UK page is blunt about the excess: if you bring more than three months supply, or get more medicine posted to you, it will be taken away. Canada allows a 90-day supply or a single course of treatment, whichever is less, and visitors staying more than three months may receive an additional 90-day supply every three months by mail or courier. Australia's traveller's exemption covers up to three months of medicine, carried in original packaging with a prescription or a doctor's letter. Japan is the outlier: one month of prescription medicine without special procedures, and more than that needs a Yakkan Shoumei, Japan's medicine import certificate, applied for in advance. New Zealand asks for a declaration on the arrival card when the quantity is over three months.

Read those five lines together and the shape of a long stay appears. You can legally carry about three months of insulin into almost every destination a long stay goes to, and you cannot legally carry six. That is the wall, and it decides the whole shape of the plan. The first three months can ride in your luggage. The rest has to come from somewhere else: a top-up abroad, a local prescription, or a shipment arranged before you leave. A quantity that covers the trip plus a delay reads as personal use. A quantity that covers a year reads as something else, and no letter fixes that reading. So the question for a long stay is never how much you can carry. It is where the second half of the stay comes from, and that is what sections 2 through 5 answer.

One more border habit belongs here, because it scales with the length of the stay. Keep the documentation trio from the customs guide in your carry-on for the whole stay, not just the flight out: original packaging with pharmacy labels, a copy of your prescription listing each insulin by its generic name, and a doctor's letter on letterhead. On a two-week trip the letter answers one border officer. On a 6-month stay it answers the border, the local doctor, the pharmacist, and the insurer, and each of them may want a copy. Bring several copies, keep the original safe with your travel documents, and photograph the letter and the prescription labels too, so a lost paper copy is an inconvenience, not a crisis.

2. The supply math for months

A two-week trip gets its supply count from a packing list. A 6-month stay gets its count from your own usage, because a generic list multiplied by twelve is a guess with a 6-month error bar. The traveler at type1travelling.com puts it first in her long-term guide, and it is the least glamorous and most important step in this whole playbook: track how much insulin, how many sensors, and how many pods you actually use for a month before the trip. A rough idea of usage is enough for a holiday. For a stay of months, the accurate record is what tells you how much to carry, how long it will last, and when you will need to top up, and it also stops you carrying an excessively large amount that wastes luggage space and invites border questions.

The device math is fixed by the wear times, and it is worth doing out loud so the numbers stop feeling abstract. A Dexcom G7 sensor lasts up to 10 days, with a 12-hour grace period at the end, and it cannot be restarted after the session ends. That means a 6-month stay needs about 18 sensors before any buffer, and a 3-month stay needs about 9. An Omnipod 5 Pod lasts up to 72 hours of continuous insulin delivery, so a 6-month stay needs about 60 pods, and a 3-month stay needs about 30. Add this site's 20 percent extra buffer on top of the count, the same rule as every trip on this site, and the buffer matters more on a long stay, not less, because a failed pod in month four is a pharmacy visit, not a spare from the suitcase. Nadia Halfway's numbers show the same shape from the other side: for a 5-month Erasmus stay in Malaga she arrived with a 6-month insulin supply, a full month of buffer, and her mother later sent two boxes with clothes, needles, and test strips. Her own rule, from her FAQ, is to bring enough for the full stay plus at least one month extra, always in hand luggage, never in checked baggage.

Two warnings ride with the math. First, do not count on shipping insulin to yourself. Nadia is plain about it: shipping medicines is not indicated, and she only did it as an exception during the Covid pandemic, when one parcel of needles and strips was lost in the post. A lost parcel on a two-week trip is a pharmacy visit. A lost parcel in month four of a semester is a gap with no home pharmacy to call. If family or friends visit, they can bring supplies with them, which is how Nadia restocked, but the plan should not depend on the post. Second, ask your insurer about a vacation override before you leave. The family at Hippocratic Adventures reports that many insurers allow a vacation override for 2 to 3 months of medication just by asking, and that some allow two overrides in a row for 4 to 6 months total, though some insurers have a per-year limit. Their rule is the one to follow here: call your insurance company and ask what its vacation override policy is, well in advance, because the pharmacy may need to request additional refills from your provider to fill that many months. Your insurer's rule is the one that counts, not theirs.

3. Topping up abroad: the name game, the prescription rule, and the emergency route

On a long stay, topping up is not a contingency. It is the plan. The first three months ride in your luggage. The rest comes from a pharmacy where you are living, and the pharmacy visit works the same way it does on a holiday, except you will do it more than once. Our finding insulin abroad playbook covers the full sequence. Here is the version a long stay needs.

Start with the name game, because the brand name is usually the first thing that fails at a foreign pharmacy counter. The generic name is the one that travels. NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid almost everywhere else. Both are insulin aspart, a rapid-acting insulin. Humalog and Lantus keep their names in the United States and the European Union: Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine. Learn the generic name of every insulin you use, including the long-acting one, and write it on a card you can show the pharmacist, along with the concentration. Concentration is the check that matters more than the brand name, and on a long stay you will buy insulin in more than one country, so you will meet it more than once. U-100 insulin holds 100 units in each milliliter, and U-40 insulin holds 40. A syringe is calibrated for one concentration only: U-100 insulin drawn in a U-40 syringe delivers 2.5 times the intended amount, and U-40 insulin drawn in a U-100 syringe delivers 40 percent of the intended amount. Match the number on the syringe to the number on the vial, every time, and do not use them if they do not match.

Then the prescription rule, which changes at every border and is the part of the top-up plan you must settle before you leave. The traveler at type1travelling.com reports buying insulin over the counter, which means without a prescription, in Vietnam, Malaysia, and Egypt, and needing a local prescription in Australia even with her home prescription in hand. In Spain, a traveler bought insulin in a Barcelona pharmacy with a photo of the home prescription. The UK and Australia both require a prescription from a local doctor, so plan on a local doctor visit there, not a walk-in purchase, and budget the time for it. That is the same rule the UK student in the diabetes.co.uk forum thread was asking about: a Chinese student planning a master's in the UK wanted to know whether prescribing insulin there is accessible, and the answers pointed to registering with a GP (General Practitioner) through the university's student medical services. Treat every over-the-counter example as a report from one traveler, not a guarantee, and always ask the pharmacist in front of you. The pharmacist is the best source of the local answer: whether insulin is sold over the counter or needs a local prescription, whether they can order your insulin, and which larger pharmacy nearby stocks it.

The UK has one more route worth knowing, because it is the route that saves a long stay when the local prescription is not yet sorted. Under regulation 225 of the Human Medicines Regulations 2012, a community pharmacist in the UK can supply a prescription-only medicine you were already prescribed, without a prescription, when there is a genuine immediate need and it is not practical to get a prescription without undue delay. The maximum is 30 days' treatment for most prescription-only medicines. It is a professional decision by the pharmacist, not a right, so bring your prescription and your doctor's letter and explain the situation plainly. It buys a month, not a semester, and the month is for getting the local prescription in place.

Pick your top-up city before you leave home, and pick it for its pharmacies, not its scenery. A big city with large pharmacies beats a small town every time, and the local diabetes association in the destination can say which towns and cities stock insulin most reliably. The finding-insulin playbook covers the manufacturer step too: contact the maker of your insulin and ask whether it is sold in your destination, whether it is over the counter or prescription only, and roughly what it costs, for every country on a multi-country trip, because availability changes at each border. And keep the US fallback in mind if your route passes through the States. Walmart sells ReliOn NPH and Regular human insulin for about $25 per vial, a price Novo Nordisk's own patient page states, and in many US states these older human insulins are sold behind the pharmacy counter without a prescription, though state law varies and a pharmacist may still ask for one. They are not the same as the newer analog insulins such as Humalog, NovoLog, or Lantus, and they work differently. Switching to them is a care-team decision, never a pharmacy-counter decision.

4. A local doctor and a local prescription

The top-up plan in section 3 only works if someone local can write the prescription, and on a long stay that someone is a doctor you have actually met, not a name on a list. The sooner you have that doctor, the less the stay depends on the suitcase. The route depends on where you are, and the two best-documented routes are the UK student route and the Australia reciprocal route.

In the UK, the route runs through the visa. International students pay the Immigration Health Surcharge (IHS) as part of the Student visa application, and GOV.UK lists it at 776 pounds per year for students and their dependants, paid upfront for the full length of the visa. Paying it gives access to NHS services on broadly the same basis as a UK resident. Once you arrive, register with a local GP, a General Practitioner, before you can access most NHS services. Registration is free, and many universities have an on-campus or nearby health centre specifically for students, which is the place the diabetes.co.uk forum answers point to: contact the university's student medical services before you leave home, because it will not be the first time they have handled this, and they should be able to make sure you do not have a problem. Bring a letter from your prescriber, or the contact details of your prescriber, with the duration of your course and the amounts and names of the insulins you use, in English if possible, as a starting point for the clinic. Travel with enough insulin to cover the journey and some time afterwards, just in case, because registration takes time to process.

In Australia, the route runs through the reciprocal agreement. The writer at type1travelling.com used the UK-Australia reciprocal healthcare agreement during her one-year working holiday to sign up to Medicare and NDSS, Australia's healthcare system and diabetes service scheme, and get GP appointments and medication prescriptions at the same rate as Australian residents. That is the agreement doing exactly what it is for: turning a visitor into someone a local doctor can treat. A home-country prescription cannot be used at an Australian pharmacy, so the key to a long stay is to switch to an Australian prescription through a GP before the medication you brought runs out. The same shape holds wherever a reciprocal agreement exists: find the local doctor early, bring the letter and the prescription, and let the local system take over before the carried supply ends.

Wherever you land, tell the local doctor the full picture at the first visit, not the third. Bring the prescription, the doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product. Ask the local diabetes association in the destination for advice on how to access insulin, test strips, and sensors as a foreign national, because associations often know which clinics and pharmacies other travelers have used. And keep your care team at home in the loop. They can advise on the equivalent a local doctor or pharmacist is offering, and they are the ones who know your history when a local doctor asks a question you cannot answer. On a long stay, the home team and the local doctor are two halves of one care team, and the handover between them is a conversation to have before you leave, not after something goes wrong.

5. Insurance: travel insurance is not expat health insurance

This is the section where a long stay most often goes wrong, because the two products sound alike and are not. Travel insurance is short-term cover for a trip. International health insurance, also called expat health insurance, is annual cover for a life lived abroad. Buying the first when you need the second leaves the routine uncovered, and the gap usually shows up in month four, when the carried supply ends and the local doctor's bill arrives.

Cigna Global, one of the insurers that sells both, draws the line plainly on its own site. Travel insurance is short-term medical cover for people visiting another country on holiday or while backpacking, usually six months or less, and it covers emergency treatment, while long-term care means returning to the country where you reside permanently. International health insurance is medical coverage for expatriates living abroad for a long period, an annual agreement, covering pre-existing conditions, hospitalisations, and other health-related costs. The GOV.UK foreign travel insurance guidance makes the same point from the other side: travel insurance is not intended for permanent residence abroad. Our travel insurance guide covers what to declare, what it covers, and what to check before you book, and it is written for a trip. For a stay of months, the questions change: what is the maximum trip length on the policy, are pre-existing conditions covered, is routine care included, and does the policy cover you for the whole stay or does it cap each trip inside an annual policy?

The gap the two products leave is the real problem, and one family has lived it four times. The family at Hippocratic Adventures did four 3 to 5 month sabbaticals with a son who has type 1 diabetes, and they report that the gap between arrival and eligibility for a national health system is usually 6 to 18 months depending on visa category, with work visas tending to qualify faster than retirement or student visas, and the rules varying not just by country but sometimes by region within a country. During that gap, they say, expat health insurance fits better than travel insurance, because you are past the point where a per-trip policy makes sense and not yet at the point where the national system covers you. They also tried, without success, to see a diabetes doctor in Spain and France without a national ID number or residency visa, and gave up. Treat their 6 to 18 months as their reported experience, not a rule for every country, and check the eligibility rule for your own visa before you leave. The question to ask your insurer is the same one they asked: what covers the months between arrival and national-system eligibility, and does the policy treat a pre-existing condition the way you need it to?

Three practical rules follow. First, declare type 1 diabetes as a pre-existing condition on any policy you buy, and get the answer in writing before you pay. A policy that does not know about the diabetes is a policy that may not cover it. Second, buy the cover before you leave, because most long-stay policies must be bought in your home country before departure. Third, do not name a product here as a recommendation. This guide names no insurer as a pick, because the right policy depends on your citizenship, your destination, your visa, and your health history, and the comparison has to be done against the actual policy documents for your country. Ask your insurer, read the policy, and keep the emergency number and the policy number where you can reach them.

6. Reciprocal healthcare: the list to check before you rely on it

Reciprocal healthcare is the third leg of a long stay, after the supply plan and the insurance, and it is the one most travelers have never heard of until they need it. A reciprocal healthcare agreement is a deal between two countries that lets visitors use the other country's public health system, often on the same terms as a resident. It is not insurance, and it is not a substitute for insurance. It is a discount on the cost of medically necessary care while visiting, and on a long stay it can be the difference between a local doctor visit you can afford and one you put off.

Start with the list your home country publishes. The UK publishes its full list on GOV.UK, under UK reciprocal healthcare agreements with non-EU countries, and the page says plainly that if a country is not listed, the UK does not have an agreement with it. The list includes Australia, New Zealand, and a set of smaller territories, and for each country it sets out what is covered, what you pay, and what documents you need. Check the list for your destination before you rely on it, and check what the agreement actually covers, because coverage varies by country. A UK resident visiting an EU country uses a different card: a UK-issued Global Health Insurance Card (GHIC), or in some cases a European Health Insurance Card (EHIC), gives free or discounted medical treatment when visiting an EU country. The GHIC is free to apply for through the NHS, and it is the card to carry in Europe alongside your insurance.

Two traveler reports show what the agreements do in practice, and what they do not do. The writer at type1travelling.com used the UK-Australia agreement to enroll in Medicare and get GP appointments and prescriptions at the same rate as Australian residents, and to get her routine blood, eye, and feet tests done during a stay of over a year. Nadia Halfway, writing about the EU, is careful about the limit: in the EU, the EHIC gives you the right to medical care, but you cannot simply walk into a pharmacy and get insulin, not even if you pay. In that case, you need to go to the hospital. The agreement gets you into the system. It does not turn a pharmacy into a prescription. If you are American, there is no GOV.UK-style list to check, because reciprocal agreements of this kind are a feature of other countries' systems. Your coverage abroad comes from your insurance, not from a treaty, so the insurance section above is the one that matters for you. And wherever you are from, keep travel insurance as well: reciprocal cover is for medically necessary care while visiting, and it does not cover evacuation, repatriation, or the costs the agreement leaves out.

7. Settling into a local routine

A trip runs on a packing list. A long stay runs on a routine, and the routine is the part of the plan that takes the longest to build, because it is built one habit at a time in the new place. The travelers who have done this all say the same thing in different words: do not change your habits, adapt them to a new routine. Nadia Halfway kept the same routine she had at home through five Erasmus stays: regular meals, insulin at the right time, regular check-ups, and a couple of blood tests along the way. The work is not learning a new system. It is transplanting the old one into a new kitchen, a new fridge, and a new time zone.

Start with the clock. Our time-zones guide covers the flight, and the same rules govern the stay. If you use a pump, change the pump's clock to local time when you land, because most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, and every basal rate fires at the wrong hour if the clock is wrong. If you take multiple daily injections, keep your once-daily long-acting insulin at home time on the travel day, then switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone on arrival. Meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch: take it when you eat, no matter what the clock says. After the first week, the time zone question is over, and the routine question begins: same meals, same times, same checks, in the new place.

Next, the safety net, because a long stay is long enough for something to go wrong, and the net has to work across borders. Dexcom Follow, the sharing feature that puts your glucose numbers on someone else's phone, now works across country lines. The newest Dexcom Follow app includes cross-regional syncing, so a sharer and their followers can connect across country lines: you can be in Spain and your follower can be in Ohio, and the sharing link still works, as long as both phones have internet at the same time. Both sides need the updated app, and when a follower logs into the new app with the same email the invitation was sent to, the existing sharer connection is auto-imported and data populates within about a minute. Update the app and confirm the link is active at least a week before the trip. Our CGM sharing playbook covers the setup, the airplane mode trap that pauses sharing, and the rule that both phones have to be online. If you are living alone abroad, the solo travel playbook covers the rest of the plan that works when nobody is sitting beside you.

Then the flat, the fridge, and the people around you. Tell your new flatmates and friends about your condition, explain why the insulin must stay cool, and make sure they know what to do on nights out in case of emergency. That is Nadia's advice, and it is the cheapest insurance in this guide: a flatmate who knows where the sugar packets are is worth more than a second suitcase of supplies. If you share a fridge in a guesthouse or hostel, label your insulin as refrigerated medication, and tell reception staff it is there, so it is not thrown away in a fridge clear-out. Our hotel storage guide covers the minibar fridge trap and the room-temperature rules. Carry a doctor's medical certificate translated into English, as Nadia advises, because it comes in handy if you are stopped at the airport to have the amount of insulin you are carrying checked, and it is the document a local doctor and a pharmacist will both want to see.

Finally, the routine tests. On a two-week trip, nobody schedules a blood test. On a stay of over a year, the writer at type1travelling.com says it is wise to think about getting routine blood, eye, and feet tests done during the trip, and a reciprocal agreement is a good place to get them done because the cost is usually lower. Nadia's version of the same habit is simpler: every couple of months, blood and urine tests to check HbA1c, the three-month average blood sugar measure. Build the tests into the stay the way you build them into life at home: a date, a doctor, and a result you actually read. A long stay is long enough for diabetes to drift, and the drift is easier to catch in month three than in month nine.

One more piece of paperwork belongs in the routine, and it is free. If you are a US citizen, enroll in STEP, the Smart Traveler Enrollment Program, before you leave. STEP is a free service from the US Department of State that lets US citizens and nationals traveling or living abroad register their trip with the nearest US embassy or consulate, so the embassy can contact you in an evacuation or other emergency. Enrollment is voluntary. Enroll at step.state.gov, and if you enrolled before September 2024, re-enroll, because the system was rebuilt and old registrations did not carry over.

8. The paperwork pack: one folder, five items

Everything in this guide comes down to five items in one folder, carried in your carry-on and photographed onto your phone. If the folder is complete before you leave, every section above has a next step. If it is not, the first week of the stay is spent fixing paperwork instead of settling in.

1. The doctor's letter. On letterhead, stating you have type 1 diabetes, listing what you carry, and signed by your prescriber. Carry it translated into English, and into the local language if English is not widely read where you are going. The travel letter guide covers the full one-page format.

2. Generic-name prescriptions. A copy of your prescription for each insulin and device, listing the generic name, because the generic name is the one that travels. Bring the prescription for the full stay, and ask your care team for the extra months in writing before you leave. Our pre-trip appointment guide lists the questions to ask and what to leave with, and for a long stay the appointment should be further out than the usual four to six weeks, because the prescriptions, the insurance, and the local doctor all take time.

3. The insurance documents. The policy number, the emergency number, and the page that says what is covered, printed and on your phone. If you are a student, the visa paperwork that proves you paid the Immigration Health Surcharge, and the reference number that came with it.

4. The local contacts. The name and address of the GP or clinic you registered with, the nearest hospital, the nearest large pharmacy, and the local diabetes association. Write them down before you need them, because the night you need them is the worst night to search for them.

5. The home contacts. Your care team at home, your pharmacy at home, and the person at home who follows your CGM numbers. Tell them the plan, including the hours you will be offline, so a blank screen at home reads as "they are flying" instead of "something is wrong."

One last note on money, because a long stay is also a budget question. This guide gives no price for insulin abroad beyond the US ReliOn figure, because prices vary by country, by pharmacy, and by week, and any number printed here would be wrong somewhere by the time you read it. Ask the manufacturer for the price in your destination, ask the pharmacist when you arrive, and ask your insurer what it reimburses. The only price that matters is the one on the shelf where you are standing.

What to do before a long stay

Here is the full list, in this order:

Long Stays Abroad with Type 1 Diabetes: The Checklist

  • Months before: book the pre-trip appointment with your care team, further out than the usual 4 to 6 weeks; bring the itinerary and leave with a travel letter, generic-name prescriptions for the full stay, a time zone plan, a sick-day plan, and a backup plan for a failed pod
  • Track your own usage for a month: insulin, sensors, and pods actually used, then multiply by the months away and add 20% extra; Dexcom G7 up to 10 days with a 12-hour grace period, Omnipod 5 Pod up to 72 hours
  • Check the border limit for every destination: US 90 days for foreign nationals, UK 3 months, Canada 90 days, Australia 3 months, Japan 1 month plus Yakkan Shoumei; carry about 3 months, plan the rest as a top-up, not a bigger suitcase
  • Ask your insurer about a vacation override, and ask what its policy covers for the whole stay; read the maximum trip length, the pre-existing condition rule, and whether routine care is included; buy the cover before you leave
  • Check the reciprocal healthcare list your home country publishes (GOV.UK for UK residents), and apply for a GHIC or EHIC for EU travel; do not plan on reciprocal cover replacing insurance
  • Pick the top-up city before you leave, contact the insulin maker about availability and price in each destination, and learn the generic name and concentration of every insulin you use
  • Arrange the local doctor before arrival where you can: UK students register with a GP through university student medical services after paying the Immigration Health Surcharge; in Australia, enroll in Medicare under the reciprocal agreement and see a local GP for a local prescription
  • Pack the first months in your carry-on, never in checked baggage; divide supplies between bags; bring the documentation trio and several copies of the letter, plus photos of the letter and the prescription labels
  • Set up CGM sharing before you leave: update the Dexcom Follow app on both sides, confirm the link is active, and tell your followers the hours you will be offline; pack the fingerstick meter and the low kit with sugar packets (1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60)
  • Enroll in STEP at step.state.gov if you are a US citizen; write down the local GP, the nearest hospital, the nearest large pharmacy, and the local diabetes association before you need them
  • After landing: change the pump clock to local time, switch long-acting insulin to the usual clock time in the new time zone, tell your flatmates about the insulin in the fridge, and book the routine blood, eye, and feet tests for the stay

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How much insulin can I bring into another country for a long stay?

A: The pattern across the countries travelers visit most is about 90 days, or about three months. The US Food and Drug Administration will allow foreign nationals visiting the US to bring or ship a 90-day supply, and visitors staying longer can have more sent to them. The UK caps what a visitor may bring in at three months, Canada allows a 90-day supply or a single course of treatment, whichever is less, and Australia's traveller's exemption covers up to three months. Japan is the outlier at one month without a certificate, and more needs a Yakkan Shoumei, Japan's medicine import certificate, applied for in advance. A stay longer than the limit needs a local plan, not a bigger suitcase.

Q: Can I get more insulin while I am abroad?

A: Yes, and on a long stay you should plan to. The route depends on the country. Some travelers report buying insulin over the counter, which means without a prescription, in Vietnam, Malaysia, and Egypt, and in Spain one traveler bought insulin with a photo of the home prescription. The UK and Australia require a prescription from a local doctor, so plan on a local doctor visit there. In the UK, a community pharmacist can also make an emergency supply of up to 30 days of a medicine you were already prescribed, under regulation 225 of the Human Medicines Regulations 2012, when there is a genuine immediate need. In the US, Walmart sells ReliOn human insulin for about $25 a vial behind the pharmacy counter in many states, though it is older human insulin, not the newer analog insulins, and switching to it is a care-team decision.

Q: How do I count supplies for a 3 to 6 month stay?

A: Count from your own usage, not from a generic list. Track how much insulin, how many sensors, and how many pods you actually use for a month before the trip, then multiply by the months away and add this site's 20 percent extra buffer. The device math is fixed by the wear times: a Dexcom G7 sensor lasts up to 10 days with a 12-hour grace period, so a 6-month stay needs about 18 sensors before the buffer, and an Omnipod 5 Pod lasts up to 72 hours, so the same stay needs about 60 pods. Pack the first months in your carry-on, plan the top-up city before you leave, and do not count on shipping insulin to yourself: one traveler lost a parcel of needles and strips in the post, and shipping medicines is not recommended.

Q: Does travel insurance cover a 6-month stay?

A: A standard trip policy usually does not. Travel insurance is short-term cover, usually six months or less, built for emergencies on a trip, and long-term care means returning to your home country. International health insurance is the other product: an annual agreement for people living abroad, covering routine care and pre-existing conditions. The two are different products, not two sizes of the same one, so read the policy for the maximum trip length, whether pre-existing conditions are covered, and whether routine care is included. Our travel insurance guide covers what to declare and what to check before you book, and the gap between arrival and eligibility for a national health system can run months, so ask your insurer what covers that gap before you leave.

Q: How does reciprocal healthcare work for a long stay?

A: A reciprocal healthcare agreement is a deal between two countries that lets visitors use the other country's public health system, often on the same terms as a resident. The UK publishes its full list on GOV.UK, and if a country is not on the list, the UK has no agreement with it. A UK-issued Global Health Insurance Card, or GHIC, or in some cases a European Health Insurance Card, or EHIC, gives free or discounted medical treatment when visiting an EU country. One traveler used the UK-Australia agreement to enroll in Medicare and get GP appointments and prescriptions at the same rate as Australian residents. Reciprocal cover is for medically necessary care while visiting, not a substitute for insurance, so check what your agreement covers before you rely on it.

Q: Will my CGM sharing still work if my followers are in another country?

A: Yes, with the current app on both sides. The newest Dexcom Follow app includes cross-regional syncing, so a sharer and their followers can connect across country lines: you can be in Spain and your follower can be in Ohio, and the sharing link still works. Both phones need mobile data or Wi-Fi at the same time, and both sides need the updated app. When a follower logs into the new app with the same email the invitation was sent to, the existing sharer connection is auto-imported and data populates within about a minute. Update the app and confirm the link is active at least a week before the trip, and do not discover a stale app version from a hotel room.
