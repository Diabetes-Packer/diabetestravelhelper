Traveling Solo with Type 1 Diabetes: The Safety Playbook (2026)

Updated October 2026 · 11 min read

Picture the trip: a Thanksgiving-week flight you booked alone, a hotel room with one key card, and nobody sitting next to you who knows what your diabetes looks like. Solo travel with type 1 diabetes (T1D) is not a medical problem to solve. It is a planning problem. Every safety job a travel companion would do has a substitute: the companion who notices the low becomes CGM (continuous glucose monitor) sharing with someone at home; the person who calls for help becomes a written plan your follower can execute from another time zone; the hand on your shoulder becomes a nightstand kit and a medical ID.

This guide builds that substitute system before the holiday season and November Diabetes Awareness Month. It covers setting up CGM sharing as your remote companion, the severe-low plan that works when nobody is in the room, hotel and hostel safety, the phone as your lifeline, and crossing borders alone. Every product and policy fact below was verified against the current manufacturer page, ADA (American Diabetes Association) guidance, or official travel source in October 2026.

The rule that organizes everything else. A severe low is defined, by the ADA's 2026 Standards of Care, as very low blood sugar with altered mental or physical functioning that requires another person's help to recover. Solo, that "another person" cannot be in the room, so the plan has to work from a distance: a follower who can see your numbers, an emergency number they can call, a location they already know, and gear within your reach. Every section below answers the same question: what happens if you cannot act for yourself?

Before wheels-up: set up your remote companion

CGM sharing turns one person at home into your travel companion's eyes. Dexcom's Share feature lets you share your CGM data with up to 10 designated followers, who watch your glucose on the separate Dexcom Follow app. Abbott's LibreLinkUp lets a Libre sensor user invite up to 20 family members, friends, or caregivers to view readings and receive notifications and alarms. Both systems move data through the manufacturer's servers, so the two phones do not need to be near each other. Both require an internet connection on both phones, and LibreLinkUp sharing requires signing up for Abbott's free LibreView account.

Pick one primary home contact, the person most likely to notice and act. Then write the plan together and test it once at home, not on day one of the trip:

What the follower watches for. The low alert you set for the trip, and the predictive alert that matters most solo. On the Dexcom G7, the Urgent Low Soon alert warns you within 20 minutes of a predicted severe low (55 mg/dL), which buys time to treat before it becomes a can't-help-myself situation. Discuss alert settings with your care team before the trip; solo travel is a reason to keep alarms loud and alerts on, not a reason to silence them.

What the follower does. Text first, call second. If you do not answer within a few minutes, they call the local emergency number for your destination. In the United States that is 911. Anywhere in the European Union it is 112, the single EU-wide emergency number, free from any fixed or mobile phone. For everywhere else, look up the local number before you land and write it into the plan. That is why the follower always knows where you are sleeping that night, hotel name and room number included.

When the follower stands down. Tell them what a false alarm looks like (a compression low from sleeping on the sensor, a brief dip after a workout) and how you will signal "I am fine" so one midnight alert does not turn into a panicked 3:00am phone tree.

Test the whole loop at home: trigger a low alert scenario, confirm the follower sees it, confirm they can reach you, confirm they know your address.

The severe-low plan with no companion

The ADA's 2026 Standards of Care are clear on the threshold: level 1 hypoglycemia is glucose below 70 mg/dL and at or above 54 mg/dL; level 2 is below 54 mg/dL; level 3 is severe, defined by altered mental or physical functioning that requires another person's help, regardless of the number. Level 3 is the event you are planning for, and the ADA says glucagon should be prescribed for everyone taking insulin or at high risk for hypoglycemia, with preparations that do not need reconstitution preferred.

That is why the solo nightstand kit is non-negotiable: glucagon where you can reach it from the bed, fast sugar beside it, your phone with alarms loud. The everyday low still gets sugar packets (1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60). The severe low gets glucagon, which is why the ADA's rule for close contacts applies to your follower even at a distance: your home contact should know which product you carry and how it is used, even if they will never be the one to give it.

The three modern options travel the same way they are described in the full glucagon travel guide: Baqsimi is a 3 mg nasal powder, single use, no inhaling needed, and it works even with a cold; Gvoke is a ready-mixed liquid injection that stores at true room temperature; Zegalogue is the fridge-by-default option with a 12-month room-temperature window. Which one you carry is a conversation with your care team. The solo rule is simple: one dose on the nightstand every night, not in the suitcase, not in the bathroom.

Where you sleep: the hotel room and hostel playbook

Alone in a room, your first safety device is the room itself.

Hotel rooms. Check in, then do the two-minute setup. Nightstand gets the kit: glucagon, fast sugar, phone with the charger plugged in, CGM receiver if you use one. Do not hang the do-not-disturb sign unless you mean it; if you are a heavy sleeper with low awareness overnight, let the front desk know at check-in that you have type 1 diabetes and that a missed wake-up call could be a medical emergency. Housekeeping has knocked on a lot of doors; they would rather knock twice than too late.

Hostels and shared rooms. Solo does not have to mean friendless. Tell one bunk neighbor or the hostel staff, the short version: "I have type 1 diabetes. If I seem confused or I will not wake up, call this number." Show them where your glucagon is. Travelers look out for each other; give them the information to do it.

The night rules. Fast sugar lives on the nightstand or in the bed, never in another room. The phone does not go on silent overnight. If you use a CGM, its low alarms stay loud; the night is exactly when the solo plan carries the most weight. Talk with your care team about your overnight plan before the trip, because the travel-day variables are new.

Your phone is your lifeline

Solo travel makes the phone a medical device. Protect it like one.

The charging plan. Phone and power bank charge every night in the room. The power bank rides in your personal item, not in checked luggage. Top up at every outlet you pass: airports, trains, cafes. If the phone dies, sharing dies with it, which is why the nightstand kit never depends on the phone. The full travel power routine, outlets and backup batteries included, is in the devices charging guide.

The lock screen. A lock-screen note that says "Type 1 diabetes" with your follower's name and number turns a stranger with your phone into someone who can call your safety contact. It costs two minutes and works when you cannot speak.

Location sharing. Turn on phone location sharing with your follower for the trip. If the follower's alert fires and you do not answer, the dot on the map tells them which hotel to name to the emergency operator.

The numbers that matter. Save three things before you land: the local emergency number for your destination, your follower's number with the country code, and, for U.S. travelers, the State Department's emergency help line, 1-888-407-4747 from the U.S. or Canada and +1-202-501-4444 from anywhere else, for consular help when you cannot reach the embassy. Emergency operators can receive your phone's location information when you cannot speak.

Crossing borders alone

The solo rules stack on top of the normal international rules, they do not replace them. The international travel guide covers the full version; the solo additions are short.

The travel letter matters more solo. Your diabetes travel letter explains your supplies in the local language of screening. Nobody is there to vouch for you at a foreign checkpoint, so the letter does the talking. The travel letter guide has the template.

Language card. Carry a card, paper or on the phone, with three phrases in the local language: "I have type 1 diabetes," "I need sugar," and "please call an ambulance." If you cannot speak, the card can.

Keep supplies on your body. In a foreign pharmacy or a long train ride, nothing replaces having your insulin, fast sugar, and glucagon on you. The carry-on packing list shows the kit layout that keeps the essentials reachable. Checked luggage is never the plan.

The solo travel day

The day itself runs on the same rules as any diabetes travel day, with one emphasis: everything lives on you, because there is nobody to hand the bag to.

Fast sugar on your person, not in your bag. Pockets, a waist pack, the jacket you are wearing. The daypack can be stolen, left on a train, or checked at a museum entrance; the sugar in your pocket cannot.

Pre-bolus without the optional reminder. Eat out the way you eat at home: pre-bolus before the meal so the insulin is working when the food arrives. An optional phone reminder can help if you are someone who wants one; it is never required, and it is never the plan.

Check before and during active days. Unfamiliar walking, heat, and sightseeing move blood sugar in ways a desk day never does. Glance at the CGM before you set out and during the day; treat a downward trend early with the sugar on your person.

Pack 20 percent more, minimum. This site's rule is 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says. Solo, treat it as the floor, not the ceiling: there is no companion's bag to borrow from, and a pharmacy in a foreign language is the wrong place to discover you are short. The solo travel checklist

Here is the full kit, in packing order:

  1. CGM sharing set up and tested with one home contact before departure: Dexcom Share with the Follow app, or LibreLinkUp with a LibreView account.
  2. The written follower plan: what alert fires, the text-then-call order, the local emergency number for your destination, and your hotel name and room number every night.
  3. Glucagon in its original packaging (Baqsimi, Gvoke, or Zegalogue, per your care team), with one dose on the nightstand every night.
  4. Fast sugar on your person for the everyday lows: sugar packets (1 at 60 to 70, 2 below 60), plus another fast carb where it fits.
  5. 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says, packed so the essentials are always on you, not in the suitcase.
  6. The charging plan: phone and power bank charged nightly, power bank in the personal item, CGM low alarms loud overnight.
  7. The lock-screen note: "Type 1 diabetes" with your follower's name and number.
  8. A medical ID or wallet card, the way the ADA's emergency-preparedness guidance recommends, so emergency personnel can identify your needs if you cannot speak.
  9. The numbers saved before you land: the local emergency number (911 in the US, 112 anywhere in the EU), your follower's number with the country code, and the State Department help line for U.S. travelers (1-888-407-4747 from the U.S. or Canada, +1-202-501-4444 from abroad).
  10. Your diabetes travel letter and prescription list, doing the talking at foreign checkpoints. The travel letter guide has the template.
  11. Travel insurance with a 24-hour emergency assistance line, so one call reaches help that knows your situation. See the travel insurance guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can you travel alone with type 1 diabetes?

A: Yes. Traveling solo with type 1 diabetes is safe when the safety net is built before departure: CGM sharing with one home contact, glucagon packed and within reach, fast sugar on your body, a charged phone, and a written plan your follower knows by heart. The solo differences are all about replacing the job a companion would do.

Q: What should my CGM follower do if I stop responding to a low alert?

A: The plan you write together before the trip. The usual order: the follower texts and calls you first; if there is no answer within a few minutes, they call the local emergency number for your destination (911 in the US, 112 anywhere in the EU), giving your hotel name and room number. That is why the follower always knows where you are sleeping that night.

Q: Does CGM sharing work when traveling internationally?

A: It works wherever your phone has an internet connection, because sharing travels through the manufacturer's servers, not phone to phone. Before the trip, sort out data for your destination (roaming, an eSIM, or a local SIM) and make sure your follower's phone has internet too. Test sharing on the first evening and confirm your follower can see your readings.

Q: What if I do not have anyone to set up as my CGM follower?

A: Brief the hotel front desk or a hostel staff member that you have type 1 diabetes, where your glucagon is, and that a missed wake-up call could be a medical emergency. Wear a medical ID, keep glucagon and fast sugar on the nightstand, and carry travel insurance with a 24-hour emergency assistance line so one call reaches help that knows your situation.

Q: What if my phone dies during a solo trip?

A: That is the scenario the charging plan exists for: a power bank in the personal item and nightly charging of the phone and CGM in the room. The full travel power plan is in the devices charging guide. A dead phone also means no sharing, which is why the nightstand kit (glucagon, fast sugar, medical ID) never depends on the phone.

Q: Do I need an alarm schedule or check-in timer when traveling solo?

A: No. CGM alarms and your follower's alerts are the safety system; an optional phone reminder can help if you are someone who wants a check-in rhythm, but it is never required. The rule that matters is the written plan: the follower knows the destination's emergency number, your hotel and room, and exactly what to do if you do not respond.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Which glucagon product you carry, your CGM alert settings, and your overnight low plan are conversations with your care team. Sharing features and emergency numbers change, so confirm the current setup for your devices and your destination before you travel. If you have a severe low on a trip, tell your care team afterward.
