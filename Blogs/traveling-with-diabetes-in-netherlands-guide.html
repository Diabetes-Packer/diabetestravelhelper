Traveling with Diabetes in the Netherlands: The Pharmacy, the Food, and the Trip (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Amsterdam in January, the canals in July, and the bulb fields in between: the Netherlands is a gentle European trip for an American with diabetes, and it still has rules that catch people out. It is also the tenth country on this site to get its own destination guide, after Japan, Mexico, Spain, Italy, France, the United Kingdom, Canada, Germany, and Portugal, and the reason is simple. The Netherlands is a cold winter in this series, and the pharmacy rule is the same wall as Germany and Portugal: insulin is prescription-only, a United States prescription cannot be filled directly at an apotheek, and a Dutch doctor has to write a new one. The differences are specific, and they are the ones this guide covers: a personal-use import rule with the prescription, a certificate system for medicines that fall under the Dutch Opium Act, a pharmacy system where only one kind of shop sells medicine, an out-of-hours GP service for the evenings and weekends, and a food label that puts the carbs per 100 grams where you can read them. None of it is hard. All of it is specific. This guide covers the import rules, the pharmacy, and the trip itself, in that order, so you can sort the paperwork at home and enjoy the trip once you land.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. I have not traveled to the Netherlands with diabetes. This guide is not a trip report, and it does not pretend to be one. It is built from the published guidance of the Dutch government, Netherlands Worldwide, the United States Department of State, the European Commission, Dexcom, Insulet, the Dutch emergency services, and the Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute (KNMI). Where a rule comes from one of those sources, this guide says so. Where something is my own travel habit, it is labeled as mine, and it is kept separate from the official rules. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own travel plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not going, tell your care team.

1. The import rule in one paragraph, then the details

The Netherlands does not run a certificate system like Japan's. There is no import form to file before you fly, and no office that stamps your insulin. The paperwork is a prescription and a letter, and the amount turns on a simple rule from the Dutch government. The government says you can bring most medicines into the Netherlands as long as you can prove that they are for your own use. Traveler guides describe the usual amount as up to a 3-month supply. The medicine must be for your own use. Our customs and border rules guide covers the general version of this system for any country, and our international travel guide covers the wide version of traveling abroad with diabetes. The Netherlands is where the general version gets specific.

Here is the part that matters most for insulin: insulin is a prescription drug, but it is not a narcotic, so the controlled medicine rules in section 3 do not apply to it. The personal-use limit does apply, so pack for the trip length plus the 20 percent buffer in section 2, and do not carry a six-month stockpile across the border. If the trip runs longer than the supply you can carry, our long-stay abroad playbook covers the planning for a trip that outlasts the supply in your bag. Do not plan to have prescription medicine mailed to you in the Netherlands: the rule that matters is the one at the border, and the medicine you need is the medicine you carry with you.

Three more things to know before you fly. First, the entry rules, from the State Department page. United States citizens do not need a tourist visa to visit the Netherlands for less than 90 days, and no vaccinations are required. Your passport must have 3 months validity beyond your date of departure, and it must have 2 blank passport pages. The Netherlands does not accept the 12-page United States emergency passport for visa-free entry, so do not travel on one. Declare cash, gold, or high-value securities totaling 10,000 euros or more on entry and exit. Second, the border itself has changed. The Entry/Exit System, known as the EES, is fully operational at Schengen borders since 10 April 2026: it records your passport details, fingerprints, and a photo at the border, and it has replaced passport stamping. ETIAS, the European Travel Information and Authorisation System, is not yet in operation, and no applications are being taken. The European Union has not announced a start date. When it does start, the fee is set at 20 euros. You do not need one for this trip, and you should not pay any website for one today. Third, check every medicine against the Dutch Opium Act before you pack, as section 3 covers in full. Our travel letter guide covers what the letter should say, and our pre-trip appointment guide covers the full appointment, including asking for generic-name prescriptions before you leave. A prescription that lists the generic name is the one that travels. A brand name that only exists in your home country does not.

2. Insulin at the border: pens, syringes, and what to carry

Insulin travels the same way it travels everywhere: in the bag that stays with you, in its original box with the pharmacy label on it. The Dutch government says to carry your medicines in their original packaging, and in your hand luggage when you fly. Syringes have one packing rule of their own. Keep the syringes with the injectable medicine they accompany, and make sure the doctor's letter mentions them. Do not plan to bring a loose syringe on its own. Pack the syringes with the insulin they belong to, and list them in the letter, so the letter and the bag agree with each other. The Netherlands' airport security rules, covered in section 5, are friendlier than most on this point: liquid medicine you need during the trip is exempt from the 100 ml limit, so a larger volume is fine in the carry-on, with a prescription or a medical certificate to show for it.

Two habits from my own travel routine, kept separate from the border rules. Everything diabetes-related rides in my carry-on, never in checked luggage, and I pack 20 percent extra of everything: pods, sensors, insulin, test strips, lancets, ketone strips, and the low kit. Our packing list does the counting for the trip length. The 20 percent buffer covers the pod that fails on a long walk through Amsterdam and the sensor that peels off on a rainy January day. Bring backup insulin for injections in case a pod fails mid-trip, and keep your insulin plan written down somewhere that is not the pump. The flight there follows the same airport rules as any trip, covered in our TSA rules guide for the United States departure and our airport security abroad guide for the arrival side, and the Netherlands arrival has its own rules, covered in section 5.

3. What needs extra paperwork, and what cannot cross the border

Some medicines need extra paperwork before you travel, and the Netherlands is the country in this series where the paperwork has a name you should learn before you pack. Medicines that fall under the Dutch Opium Act, the Opiumwet, need an official certificate to cross the border. The Dutch government gives four examples: strong painkillers, sleeping pills and drugs to reduce anxiety, such as Valium or Seresta, medication for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), such as Ritalin or Concerta, and medicinal cannabis. The certificate for travel into a Schengen country is called a Schengen certificate. It must be signed by your doctor, it states that the medicine is required for your own medical use, and it is valid for 30 days. The Dutch government says to allow up to four weeks for the application to be processed, so start early. Ask your prescriber and the Dutch embassy which authority issues the certificate for a traveler flying in from the United States. If you change planes at Schiphol, carry the certificate with you: Dutch customs can check you on a transfer too.

Two documents that are not the certificate. A medication passport, the printout of your prescription history that a pharmacy can give you, is useful medical information, but the Dutch government says plainly that it is not a travel document and does not replace a required Schengen certificate or legalized medical certificate. A prescription on its own is not the certificate either. The certificate is the document, and the prescription and the letter travel with it.

Check every medicine by its active ingredient, not its brand name, before you pack. The active ingredient is what the law sees. The brand name is not.

Adderall is the one that catches travelers by surprise, and in the Netherlands the surprise is the same as in Germany and Portugal: it is not sold there at all. Adderall, the mixed amphetamine salts used for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), has no marketing authorisation in any European Union member state, so a Dutch pharmacy cannot sell it to you and there is no local replacement under that brand name. Methylphenidate is sold in the Netherlands, as Ritalin and Concerta, and lisdexamfetamine is sold as Elvanse. Stimulant medicines are controlled substances under Dutch law, so if you take one, the 30-day rule above is the rule for it, with the certificate, the prescription, and the letter to show for it. If you take a stimulant medicine, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about what happens if you run out, and carry the certificate, the prescription, and the letter with the medicine. The answer cannot be found at the border. Bring your full trip supply from home, and do not rely on local pharmacies to restock.

Cannabis is a separate problem, and it is the one that catches travelers who assume a border works like a coffee shop. Drug possession, including cannabis, is illegal in the Netherlands. The Dutch tolerance policy allows personal possession of small amounts of cannabis and purchase of the same at licensed coffee shops, but the State Department page is clear that cannabis remains illegal in the United States, and bringing any amount of any illegal substance back to the United States is a crime. Do not bring cannabis, edibles, extracts, or CBD products into the Netherlands, and do not try to take any home. Leave them at home.

4. The pharmacy: insulin is prescription-only, and the prescription has to be Dutch

The pharmacy in the Netherlands is called an apotheek, and you will find one on most main streets in Amsterdam, Rotterdam, and every town in between, marked by a green cross. The first thing to understand is the rule that matters most, and it is the same wall as Germany and Portugal: insulin is prescription-only in the Netherlands. The Dutch term is receptgeneesmiddel, a medicine that needs a prescription, and the pharmacist has no discretion to be nice about it. Do not confuse the apotheek with a drogist. A drogist, a drugstore such as Kruidvat or Etos, sells no prescription medicine at all, including insulin. The apotheek is the only place in the Netherlands where you can legally buy prescription medicine, and all medicines are kept behind the pharmacist's counter. You cannot pick insulin off a shelf. You ask the pharmacist, and the pharmacist works from the prescription.

So the pharmacy visit, if you need one, has a fixed sequence, and the honest version has two steps, not one. First, see a doctor in the Netherlands. A prescription issued outside the European Union is not valid in the Netherlands, so a United States prescription cannot be filled directly at a Dutch pharmacy, and a Dutch doctor has to write a local prescription. The usual first stop is a general practitioner, a huisarts, or a private clinic. A prescription issued in another European Union country is valid in the Netherlands and can be filled at a Dutch pharmacy, which is the rule for a traveler who lives in the European Union, not for an American flying in from the United States. Second, take the Dutch prescription to the pharmacy. Dutch prescriptions are electronic: the general practitioner sends the prescription directly to your chosen pharmacy. As a visitor you pay the full amount for the visit and for the medicine, then claim it back from your travel insurance. Bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product. Tell the doctor the generic name of your insulin, not just the brand: insulin aspart, insulin lispro, insulin glargine, insulin degludec. The pharmacist and the doctor both work from the generic name, and the generic name is what survives the brand change at the border. The good news is that the brand names are the same as at home. NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid in the Netherlands, and both are insulin aspart. Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine. Our finding insulin abroad playbook covers the name game in full, including the concentration check that matters more than the brand name.

Two more things to know before you walk in. First, most insulin pens use the same U-100 standard, 100 units in each milliliter, so a pen dial counts the same way it does at home. Check the concentration printed on the box before you use a new pen, because a syringe is calibrated for one concentration only. Second, mind the hours. Most pharmacies keep weekday hours. The duty pharmacy, the dienstapotheek, covers nights and Sundays, and the nearest one is listed on apotheek.nl. Do not wait until Sunday night to find it. Third, visitors pay. United States Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for medical care outside the United States, and medical care is not free in the Netherlands. Private and some public hospitals often need payment up front or proof of good insurance, and credit card payment is not always available, so keep every receipt for your travel insurance claim, and check your policy before the trip so the clinic visit is not the first time you read it. Our travel insurance guide covers what to declare and what to look for.

5. Your devices in the Netherlands: the app, the sensors, and the airport

Start with the phone, because it holds the one trap that ruins more international trips than any other. The Dexcom G7 app is region-locked at install: Dexcom's own user guide states the app must be installed in your country of residence, and the app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when you first install it. The check happens at install time. An app that is already installed and working keeps working abroad, and travelers report normal operation on international trips as long as nobody deletes the app mid-trip. So install the G7 app and update it to the latest version before you leave, turn off auto-updates before the trip so nothing about your diabetes setup changes mid-trip without your say-so, and do not delete the app while you are in the Netherlands. A deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country, and that is a long trip without CGM data. Our G7 app region-lock guide covers the full prep list, and nothing in it is specific to the Netherlands: the same rule applies everywhere you land.

The receiver is the backup that makes the app problem a non-issue. Dexcom's own international travel guidance says that if you have a receiver, it makes a great backup to your phone app. The receiver has no app store, no region check, and no install step. It pairs with your sensor and shows readings anywhere on earth. Pack it with its USB cable and charger. The Netherlands uses Type C and Type F plugs at 230 volts, so bring a plug adapter: United States plugs do not fit Dutch outlets. Most modern phone chargers and laptops are dual voltage and only need the adapter, but check the label on each device before you plug it in. Our devices charging guide covers plugs and power on the road. If you do not already own a receiver, ask Dexcom about getting one before a big international trip. Bring the fingerstick meter too, as the final fallback, and keep it in your day bag rather than buried in your hotel room.

Buying replacement sensors in the Netherlands is a pharmacy problem, not a shop problem. The G7 is distributed in the Netherlands, and the Omnipod 5 launched in the Netherlands in June 2024 and works with the G7 there. A replacement sensor bought in the Netherlands still means a foreign pharmacy, a local prescription question, and regional compatibility questions you do not want to solve on vacation. Pack more sensors than the trip math says you need, with the spares in your carry-on, and do not plan to shop. The same applies to pump supplies. Carry spare Pods and a backup injection plan with syringes and a vial, and keep your basal rates written down somewhere that is not the pump. Our pod failure backup plan covers the sequence when a Pod fails away from home.

Across the European Union, liquids in hand luggage are limited to containers of up to 100 ml, in one transparent resealable bag of up to 1 litre. Liquid medicine you need during the trip is exempt from that limit: insulin and its accessories can be carried in the amount needed for the flight, with a prescription or a medical certificate to show for it. Keep medication in its original packaging with the patient information leaflet where possible, and carry the doctor's letter with it. Tell the screening officer about the pump or CGM and where it sits, and ask for alternate screening if the scanner is the next step. Our Omnipod 5 airport guide covers the United States side of the same trip, and our scanner comparison guide covers which machine is safe for which device.

6. Time zones: one clock, and the gap that narrows twice a year

The whole Netherlands keeps one clock, which makes it one of the simplest time zones in this series, and the gap is still large enough to plan around. The Netherlands runs Central European Time, UTC+1, in winter, and Central European Summer Time, UTC+2, in summer. In 2026 the clocks move forward on Sunday 29 March and back on Sunday 25 October, the same Sundays as the rest of the European Union. Amsterdam is 6 hours ahead of New York as the standard gap, and 9 hours ahead of Los Angeles. The gap narrows twice a year, because the United States and the Netherlands do not move their clocks on the same Sundays. The United States moves forward on 8 March and back on 1 November 2026, so from 8 to 29 March and from 25 October to 1 November, Amsterdam is 5 hours ahead of New York and 8 hours ahead of Los Angeles. A trip that falls in one of those windows needs the narrowed gap in the plan, not the standard one.

The plan follows the rule in our time zone insulin guide. If you use a pump, change the pump clock to local time when you land, so your basal rates, the background insulin the pump drips all day, run on local time. Most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, so this is one manual step at the gate or the hotel. If you take multiple daily injections, keep your long-acting insulin on home time on the travel day, then switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone when you arrive, for example bedtime. Meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch: take it when you eat, wherever you are. The guide covers the eastbound and westbound cases in full, and a six-hour jump is large enough that the careful planning earns its keep. Run your route through our time zone planner before you fly, and confirm the plan with your care team. The planner adjusts timing only, never dose amounts. Dose changes are a conversation with your care team, not something a tool or a blog post should decide.

7. Eating in the Netherlands: the label that puts the carbs per 100 grams

The Netherlands is one of the easiest countries in the world to count carbs in, because the label puts the number where you can read it, in grams per 100 grams. Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 requires the vast majority of pre-packed foods to bear a nutrition declaration, and it must show the energy value and the amounts of fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, and salt. The amounts must be expressed per 100 g or per 100 ml, in a legible tabular format on the pack. The Dutch label reads Koolhydraten, with waarvan suikers, of which sugars, indented beneath it. The per-100-gram basis is the useful part: it lets you compare two products directly, and it lets you scale the number to the portion in front of you. Compare two products by the carbohydrate line, not by the front of the pack. Front-of-pack nutrition information is not mandatory under the current EU rules, so the back of the pack is where the carb count lives. This guide gives no carb counts for specific foods, because the number on the package in front of you is the one that counts, and it is printed right there.

Restaurant food has no label, so it works the way eating out works everywhere. Ask what is in the dish, estimate the carbohydrate from the bread, the potatoes, or the rice, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. I pre-bolus in my usual way before meals. That habit travels with me, and the Netherlands does not change it. A December evening at a Christmas market is the same routine in a colder setting: the stalls sell food with no label, so estimate the dish, pre-bolus in your usual way before eating, and keep the sugar packets in your pocket rather than in the bag you left at the hotel. Our Christmas markets playbook covers the market routine in full. Our eating out playbook covers the ordering and estimating routine, and our pre-bolus guide covers the timing habit. For lows, keep sugar packets in your day bag. I treat lows with sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, and that is the low kit that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day in any country. Do not let the low kit be something you buy after you need it. Pack it before you leave the hotel.

A beer at a brown cafe is the drink that catches people out, because a glass of beer is still alcohol, and alcohol can push glucose down hours later. Eat with it, check more often on a drinking day, and keep the sugar packets in your pocket. Our drinking alcohol playbook covers the routine for a drinking day in full.

Water is the other half of eating safely in the Netherlands, and the news is good. Tap water is generally safe to drink. A stomach bug is a diabetes problem, not just a comfort problem, because vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the plan for that day.

Vaccines are a question for the care-team appointment, not for the airport. No vaccine is required to enter the Netherlands, and the appointment is the place to check that routine vaccines are up to date. Our travel vaccines playbook covers the appointment in full.

8. Winter in the Netherlands: the cold season, and the rain that comes with it

Winter is the season this guide is written for, and the Dutch winter is cold in a way that changes the diabetes routine, not just the packing list. Amsterdam January afternoons average a high of 42F, with nights near 34F, and July is the other end of the range, with afternoons averaging a high of 70F and nights near 56F. The Dutch weather service, the Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute, puts Amsterdam January at a 43F high and a 34F low, and July at a 73F high and a 56F low, which lines up with the same numbers. January also averages about 12 rainy days, so pack a waterproof layer and a dry bag for the day bag. Wherever you go, the cold rule for insulin is the same rule it follows everywhere. Unopened insulin belongs in the fridge at 36 to 46F, and in-use insulin is fine at room temperature below 77F. Insulin freezes at 32F, and frozen insulin must be discarded, so a January night in Amsterdam averages just above freezing, but a cold snap can still push a bag left out overnight below freezing. Keep in-use insulin close to your body on a cold day, inside your coat rather than in an outer pocket, and never leave insulin or spare sensors in a car overnight. Our insulin cooling guide covers the methods, and our hotel insulin storage guide covers the minibar trap in detail: hotel minibar fridges can freeze items at the back, so keep unopened insulin away from the cooling element and check that it has not frozen.

All-day walking and cycling is the other half of the Netherlands plan, and it pulls glucose down. A day that starts at the Rijksmuseum, crosses Amsterdam on foot or by bike, and ends with dinner across town can mean hours on the move without trying. Activity lowers glucose, so check more often than usual on a moving day, carry sugar packets in your pocket rather than in the bag you left at the hotel, and keep a snack in the day bag for the way back. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. The low kit is the same one that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day: sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60.

Then there is the heat, which is the one place where your devices meet a real limit on a Netherlands trip. Dexcom publishes exact temperature numbers for the G7. The sensor's operating range is 50F to 107.6F, and its storage range is 36F to 86F. A worn sensor sits against warm skin under your shirt, so a normal summer day does not push it out of range, but a spare sensor left in a hot car can climb above its storage ceiling in an afternoon. Keep spares cool, inside, and check the readings against your fingerstick meter after a long hot day. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. Heat loosens adhesive too. Press the sensor and pod edges down firmly after coming inside from the heat, and pack extra overpatches. Our G7 heat and cold guide has the full temperature table, and our adhesion playbook covers keeping a sensor and a pump stuck through holiday trips.

Summer has the other edge: July and August bring the warmest days, and in-use insulin belongs below 77F, so keep it out of direct sun and out of a hot car. The Dutch summer is mild next to the south of Europe, but a car in the sun still gets hot enough to ruin insulin.

9. If something goes wrong: numbers, clinics, and the insurance call

Save these numbers in your phone before you land. Dial 112 for the police, an ambulance, or the fire service. It is free from any phone, and English-speaking operators are available. If you need the police but it is not an emergency, call 0900 8844. If you need medical help urgently but it is not life-threatening, call the out-of-hours GP service, the huisartsenpost: the doctors there will tell you where to go or whether to wait for your own doctor, and you should not go straight to a hospital emergency department. If you need the United States Consulate General, call +31-20-575-5309 in Amsterdam, at Museumplein 19, 1071 DJ Amsterdam. The emergency after-hours line is +31-70-310-2209. The United States Embassy in The Hague, at John Adams Park 1, 2244 BZ Wassenaar, does not provide consular services, so Amsterdam is the number that matters. The Netherlands has no reciprocal healthcare agreement with the United States, so there is no card that covers you. A visitor pays for medical care out of pocket, and medical care is not free in the Netherlands. Private and some public hospitals often need payment up front or proof of good insurance, and credit card payment is not always available. Check your travel insurance before the trip, so the call is not the first time you read it, and keep every receipt from any clinic or pharmacy visit.

If you develop symptoms of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous complication where the blood turns acidic when insulin runs too low, get urgent help right away. Vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation, and that is a hospital visit, not a wait-and-see day. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the ketone checks and the plan for that day. Carry glucagon in your day bag, and make sure your travel companion knows where it is and how to use it. Our glucagon emergency kit guide covers what is in the kit and how to keep it travel-ready. If the trip goes wrong in a bigger way, a bag lost or stolen, our lost or stolen supplies playbook covers the exact call order, and the travel insurance emergency line is the call that arranges care, including a local doctor visit. If the trip involves driving, your United States licence works while you are in tourist status, with no Dutch licence needed. The blood alcohol limit is 0.05 percent, and it drops to 0.02 percent for drivers who have held a licence for less than five years. Some rental desks ask for an International Driving Permit as well, so check with the rental company before you fly. Remember that the Netherlands drives on the right, the same side as at home, and watch for bikes on the red-paved bike paths. Carry your licence and your passport with you. Our driving abroad guide covers licenses, rental cars, and the rules of the road.

The Netherlands is generally safe, and the State Department rates it Level 2, Exercise increased caution due to terrorism, its second level. The risk that matters most for a traveler with diabetes is petty theft: pickpocketing, purse snatching, and passport theft are common in tourist areas, and criminals target tourists in popular locations. Watch out for pickpockets and bicycle thieves, especially in crowded areas, on public transport, and at train stations. Keep your diabetes day bag in front of you on a crowded tram, and do not leave valuables in a rental car.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I buy insulin in the Netherlands without a prescription?

A: No. Insulin is prescription-only in the Netherlands, and a pharmacist cannot sell it to you without a prescription from a Dutch doctor. A prescription from the United States cannot be filled directly at a Dutch pharmacy, because a prescription issued outside the European Union is not valid in the Netherlands, so if you run out of insulin, see a doctor in the Netherlands, a general practitioner, known as a huisarts, or call the out-of-hours GP service, the huisartsenpost, show your prescription and your doctor's letter so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the Dutch prescription to a pharmacy. As a visitor you pay the full amount for the visit and for the medicine, and you claim it back from your travel insurance. The brand names are the same as at home: NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid in the Netherlands, and both are insulin aspart. Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine.

Q: How much medicine can I bring into the Netherlands?

A: The Dutch government says you can bring most medicines into the Netherlands as long as you can prove that they are for your own use. Traveler guides describe the usual amount as up to a 3-month supply. Carry your insulin in its original boxes with the pharmacy labels, carry a copy of your prescription and a letter from your prescriber that lists your medicines by their generic names, and keep the syringes with the insulin they accompany. Medicines that fall under the Dutch Opium Act have their own rule: only a 30-day supply, with an official certificate, a valid medical prescription, and a letter from your prescribing doctor that states the diagnosis, the medicine by its generic name, the dose, and that the medicine is medically necessary. The certificate is called a Schengen certificate, and the Dutch government says to allow up to four weeks for the application to be processed, so start early. Confirm the requirements with the Dutch embassy before you travel. If you change planes at Schiphol, carry the certificate with you: Dutch customs can check you on a transfer too.

Q: Can a Dutch pharmacy fill my prescription from home?

A: No, if your prescription is from the United States. A prescription issued outside the European Union is not valid in the Netherlands, so a Dutch pharmacy cannot fill it directly. If you run out of insulin, see a doctor in the Netherlands, show your prescription and your doctor's letter so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the Dutch prescription to a pharmacy. Only a pharmacy, an apotheek, sells prescription medicine in the Netherlands. A drogist, a drugstore such as Kruidvat or Etos, sells no prescription medicine, including insulin. Most pharmacies keep weekday hours, so check the duty pharmacy, the dienstapotheek, if you need one outside normal hours.

Q: What about Adderall and other controlled medicines?

A: Adderall, mixed amphetamine salts, has no marketing authorisation in any European Union member state, so a Dutch pharmacy cannot sell it to you, and there is no local replacement under that brand name. Methylphenidate is sold in the Netherlands as Ritalin and Concerta, and lisdexamfetamine is sold as Elvanse. Stimulant medicines are controlled substances under Dutch law, so if you take one, carry no more than a 30-day supply, with an official certificate, a valid medical prescription, and a letter from your prescribing doctor that states the diagnosis, the medicine by its generic name, the dose, and that the medicine is medically necessary. Talk to your prescriber well before the trip about what happens if you run out, because the answer cannot be found at the border. Bring your full trip supply from home, and do not rely on local pharmacies to restock. Do not bring cannabis, edibles, extracts, or CBD products across the border in either direction: cannabis is illegal in the Netherlands, even though the tolerance policy allows small amounts for personal use and purchase at licensed coffee shops, and bringing any amount back to the United States is a crime.

Q: Will my Dexcom G7 app work in the Netherlands?

A: Yes, if it was already installed and set up at home. The Dexcom G7 app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when it is first installed, so install and update it before you leave and do not delete it while you are abroad: a deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country. Bring the Dexcom receiver as backup. It has no app store and no install check, and it pairs with your sensor anywhere. The G7 is distributed in the Netherlands, and the Omnipod 5 launched in the Netherlands in June 2024 and works with the G7 there. A replacement sensor or Pod bought in the Netherlands still means a foreign pharmacy and a local prescription question, so pack more than the trip math says you need and do not plan to shop. The Netherlands uses Type C and Type F plugs at 230 volts, so bring a plug adapter: United States plugs do not fit Dutch outlets.

Q: What are the emergency numbers in the Netherlands?

A: Dial 112 for the police, an ambulance, or the fire service. It is free from any phone, and English-speaking operators are available. If you need the police but it is not an emergency, call 0900 8844. If you need medical help urgently but it is not life-threatening, call the out-of-hours GP service, the huisartsenpost, and it will tell you where to go or whether to wait for your own doctor: do not go straight to a hospital emergency department. If you need the United States Consulate General, call +31-20-575-5309 in Amsterdam, at Museumplein 19, 1071 DJ Amsterdam. The emergency after-hours line is +31-70-310-2209. United States Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for medical care outside the United States, so a visitor pays for medical care out of pocket, and travel insurance is the cover that matters.
