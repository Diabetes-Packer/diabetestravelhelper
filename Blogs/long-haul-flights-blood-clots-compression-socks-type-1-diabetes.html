Long-Haul Flights with Type 1 Diabetes: Blood Clots, Compression Socks, and Moving in Your Seat (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Thanksgiving is 20 days away, November is Diabetes Awareness Month, and the holiday flights are the long ones: five, six, eight hours in a seat that does not let your legs move. A long flight is a blood clot risk in a way a short hop is not, and diabetes is on the CDC list of conditions that raise that risk. The good news is that the risk for any one traveler is small, and the precautions are simple: move, pick the right seat, and wear compression socks if your care team says they suit you. This guide is the honest version of that advice: what a clot on a flight actually is, why type 1 diabetes belongs on the risk list, what the evidence really says about compression socks, who should check with their care team before wearing them, and the warning signs to watch for two weeks after you land.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes (T1D) for 20 years and I fly with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets. This guide does not invent a story about me getting a clot or wearing compression socks on a flight, and it does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team, and section 7 lists exactly what to ask them before the trip.

Talk to your care team before a long flight about your own clot risk. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If you have had a blood clot before, take blood thinners, or have circulation problems, nerve damage, or foot sores, ask your care team before the trip whether compression socks and a long flight are safe for you. If you develop the warning signs in section 7, seek urgent or emergency care right away, using the steps there.

1. What a blood clot on a long flight actually is

Deep vein thrombosis, or DVT, is a blood clot that forms in a deep vein, usually in the leg. A pulmonary embolism, or PE, is what happens when part of that clot breaks off and travels to the lungs, where it blocks blood flow. A PE can be fatal. Together, DVT and PE are called venous thromboembolism, or VTE, the medical name for the pair. The mechanism is simple: when you sit still for hours, blood moves slowly through the veins in your legs, and slow-moving blood clots more easily. Cramped sitting also presses the back of the knee against the seat edge, which slows the flow further.

  • Long-distance means more than four hours of sitting still. The CDC counts any trip where you sit still for more than four hours as long-distance travel, whether it is a flight, a drive, a train, or a bus. For flights under four hours, the risk of a clot with symptoms is negligible. The longer you sit still, the greater the risk.
  • The risk is real but small. The CDC Yellow Book says long-distance air travel might raise the overall risk of a clot two to four times, but the studies disagree with each other. Two studies put the absolute risk for flights over four hours at about 1 in 4,656 and 1 in 6,000 person-flights. In plain words: it happens, and it is uncommon.
  • Most travel clots happen to people who already have another risk factor. The CDC Yellow Book reports that 75 to 99 percent of people who developed a travel-associated clot had at least one preexisting risk factor. For travelers with no preexisting risk factors, the risk is low. That is why this guide spends as much time on your personal risk list as on the flight itself.
  • It is the sitting, not the cabin. The same increase in clot risk shows up with long bus, car, and train trips, which tells researchers the risk comes mainly from prolonged limited mobility rather than the air in the cabin. You do not need a theory about cabin pressure to explain it, and this guide does not offer one.

2. Why type 1 diabetes is on the risk list

The CDC lists diabetes among the conditions that raise clot risk, in these words: "Certain other long-term conditions, such as heart or lung conditions, or diabetes." Diabetes affects blood vessels and circulation over time, which is why the standard flight precautions matter more for a traveler with diabetes than for a traveler without it. What the CDC does not do is give a number for diabetes alone. No peer-reviewed study or official source gives a flying-specific risk number for diabetes, so this guide does not invent one. The honest framing is qualitative: diabetes is on the list, the size of its contribution on a flight is not measured, and the personal call belongs to your care team.

The rest of the CDC risk list is worth reading in full, because risk stacks. The more items on this list that apply to you, the greater your chance of a clot on a long trip:

  • Older age: risk increases after age 40
  • Overweight or obesity
  • Recent surgery or injury, within 3 months
  • Use of estrogen-containing contraceptives, such as birth control pills, rings, or patches
  • Hormone replacement therapy
  • Pregnancy and the postpartum period, up to 3 months after childbirth
  • A previous blood clot
  • An inherited blood clotting disorder
  • Family history of blood clots
  • Active cancer or recent cancer treatment
  • Certain other long-term conditions, such as heart or lung conditions, or diabetes
  • Limited mobility, for example a leg cast
  • A catheter placed in a large vein
  • Varicose veins

Two details from the CDC Yellow Book are worth knowing before you pick a seat. Travelers shorter than 5 feet 3 inches and taller than 6 feet 3 inches appear to be at increased risk: shorter travelers may be more prone to seat-edge pressure behind the knee, because airline seats are higher than car seats and cannot be adjusted, and taller travelers have less leg room. And the risk does not end at landing: most travel-associated clots occur within the first one to two weeks after the flight, and the risk returns to baseline by eight weeks.

3. Moving in your seat: the plan that matters most

Movement is the precaution with the broadest agreement behind it, and it costs nothing. The CDC, the NHS, and the chest physicians' guideline all say the same thing in different words: get up, walk, and keep your calf muscles working while you sit. Here is the plan, in the order that matters.

  1. Get up and walk every one to two hours. The CDC advises walking around every one to two hours on a long trip. The NHS says to get up and move around every hour or so. A short walk to the back of the cabin and back is enough to get the calf muscles pumping. Set a silent alarm on your phone so a nap does not eat the whole flight.
  2. Pick an aisle seat when you can. In one study reported in the CDC Yellow Book, travelers in window seats had about twice the clot risk of travelers in aisle seats, and travelers with a body mass index of 30 or higher in window seats had about six times the risk. Aisle seats look protective mainly because the person in one is freer to get up and move. If the aisle is taken, a window seat is still fine: it just means being deliberate about standing up instead of waiting for a reason.
  3. Do the seated exercises between walks. The CDC suggests these, and they are worth doing every hour or so: extend your legs straight out and flex your ankles, pulling your toes toward you; pull each knee up toward your chest and hold it there for 15 seconds, and repeat up to 10 times; raise and lower your heels while keeping your toes on the floor; raise and lower your toes while keeping your heels on the floor; tighten and release your leg muscles. These movements help blood flow in your legs while you are stuck in the seat.
  4. Wear loose clothing. The NHS lists loose clothing among its long-journey precautions, and tight clothing around the waist or behind the knee works against the blood flow you are trying to keep moving.
  5. Count the delay as flight time. If the plane sits on the ground for two hours before takeoff, that is two hours of sitting still, and the clock does not care that the wheels never left the runway. The long-layovers playbook covers the full delay plan, including getting up and walking the terminal while you wait.

4. Compression socks: what the evidence actually shows

Graduated compression socks, also called flight socks, are tightest at the ankle and gradually looser as they go up the calf, which helps push blood upward toward the heart. They are the most talked-about travel precaution and the most misunderstood, because the evidence for them is strong in one place and missing in another. Here is what the best review actually found.

  • The Cochrane review: fewer clots found on scans, no proof about the clots that hurt you. Cochrane is an independent research group that pools the results of clinical trials. Its 2021 review of compression stockings for airline passengers pooled 12 trials with 2,918 passengers on flights over five hours. Stockings cut symptomless DVT, the clots found by scanning passengers who felt nothing, from 47 cases without stockings to 3 with them, an odds ratio of 0.10, rated high-certainty evidence. But no deaths, no lung clots, and no clots with symptoms happened in either group, so the review could not assess whether stockings prevent those outcomes. It also found low-certainty evidence that stockings reduce leg swelling. The stockings were well tolerated, with no significant adverse effects reported.
  • What that means in plain words. Compression socks are proven to reduce the clots that show up on a scan in people who feel fine. They are not proven to prevent the clots that cause pain, swelling, and trips to the emergency department, because no trial has been large enough to measure that. Guidelines still recommend them for higher-risk travelers, which is a judgment based on the indirect evidence plus the low risk of wearing them, not a trial result.
  • Who the guidelines recommend them for. The American College of Chest Physicians makes a weak recommendation, based on low or very low-quality evidence, for at-risk travelers on trips over six hours: frequent walking, calf muscle exercise, an aisle seat, and properly fitted below-knee graduated compression stockings providing 15 to 30 mmHg of pressure at the ankle. For travelers with no extra risk, the same guideline does not recommend stockings. Movement comes first either way: stockings are an addition to walking, never a replacement for it.
  • What level to buy. Travel socks are most commonly sold at 15 to 20 mmHg, which is millimeters of mercury, the unit compression is measured in. That is the usual travel range. The firmer 20 to 30 mmHg range is a step up that a doctor may recommend if you are at higher risk. For reference, the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence classes are Class 1 at 14 to 17 mmHg, Class 2 at 18 to 24 mmHg, and Class 3 at 25 to 35 mmHg. Buy below-knee graduated socks, fitted to your ankle and calf measurements, and try them on before the trip, not at the airport. Wear them through the flight: the trials had passengers wearing them for the whole flight.
  • Fit matters more than the number on the packet. A sock that is too tight can cut off circulation at the top band, and a sock that is too loose does nothing. Measure your ankle and calf in the morning, when swelling is lowest, and follow the maker's size chart. If the socks leave deep marks, hurt, or make your toes cold or numb, take them off and ask your care team before wearing them again.

5. Who should ask their care team before wearing compression socks

Compression socks squeeze the legs, and for most travelers that is the point. For some travelers, squeezing is exactly the problem. The list below is who should ask first, and it is not a list to self-assess your way past. If any item applies to you, the answer comes from your care team, not from this guide.

  • Peripheral artery disease (PAD), which is narrowed arteries in the legs. Compression presses on arteries as well as veins. The UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence says arterial insufficiency should be excluded by measuring the ankle-brachial pressure index, or ABPI, before prescribing compression stockings: an ABPI below 0.8 means stockings are contraindicated, as severe arterial insufficiency is likely; an ABPI of 0.8 to 1.3 means stockings are safe to wear; an ABPI above 1.3 means compression should be avoided, as the arteries may be calcified and hard. If you have PAD, leg pain when walking, or cold feet with weak pulses, do not buy compression socks without asking first.
  • Severe nerve damage in the feet and legs. If you cannot feel your feet properly, you may not feel a sock that is too tight, bunched up, or cutting into the skin, and that is how pressure damage starts. The same guidance says the condition of the skin should be checked, because fragile skin may be damaged while putting stockings on or taking them off.
  • Foot ulcers, sores, or broken skin. Venous ulcers should be healed before using compression stockings, and any open sore on the foot or leg is a reason to ask first. A sock over a sore hides it and presses on it at the same time.
  • Uncontrolled diabetes or diabetes complications. This is the group the research does not cover well, which is itself the reason to ask. The one study that tested compression in people with diabetes is described below, and its patients were screened and stable, not uncontrolled.
  • A previous blood clot, or blood thinners. If you have had a DVT or PE before, the CDC advises talking with your doctor before you travel to learn your individual risks. If you take blood thinners, follow your care team's recommendations on medication use, and do not change anything yourself for a flight. After a DVT or PE, delay flights until at least two weeks after starting blood-thinning medicine.

What the diabetes study actually found, and what it did not. A 2020 study in BMJ Open Diabetes Research and Care, by Rother and colleagues, tested medical compression stockings on 94 people: 44 with diabetes, 45 with peripheral artery disease, and 5 healthy controls. The stockings were class I, at 18 to 21 mmHg, and class II, at 23 to 32 mmHg, worn for at least three hours. During the whole study, no compression-related adverse events occurred, and the measurements of blood flow in the skin showed no tendency for reduced microperfusion, which is blood flow in the smallest vessels, in the sitting and standing positions. The authors concluded compression is safe and feasible in patients with diabetes or peripheral artery disease, within the study's inclusion criteria. The limits matter as much as the result: the diabetes group had type 2 diabetes only, not type 1; every patient was screened first, with leg swelling, palpable foot pulses, no wounds, and enough sensation to feel a problem; the wear time was a few hours, not a long-haul flight; and the study measured blood flow in the skin, not clots on a flight. It supports the statement that compression did not harm circulation in screened patients. It does not prove compression socks are safe for every person with diabetes on a plane.

6. Water, alcohol, and the things that do not prevent a clot

Two pieces of standard flight advice deserve a closer look, because the evidence behind them is thinner than the advice sounds. This section states the split plainly, so you can decide what is worth doing and what is just habit.

  • Water: sensible, not proven. The NHS lists drinking plenty of water among its long-journey precautions, and staying hydrated on a dry cabin is sensible general advice. But the CDC Yellow Book is blunt about the evidence: there is no evidence for an association between dehydration and travel-associated clots, and no direct evidence that drinking water prevents one. Staying hydrated is reasonable and unlikely to harm, but it cannot be recommended specifically to prevent a clot. Drink water because it is good for you on a long flight, and because getting up to refill the bottle and use the toilet is itself movement, not because the water is stopping a clot.
  • Alcohol: the NHS says avoid it, and the reason is movement. The NHS lists avoiding alcohol among its long-journey precautions. The CDC Yellow Book adds the mechanism: alcohol can make you pass more urine, and it can make you sleepy and still, which promotes immobility. A drink that puts you to sleep for four hours in a window seat is four hours of not moving, and not moving is the risk. The diabetes side of alcohol on a trip, including lows, is covered in the drinking-alcohol playbook.
  • Aspirin: do not start it for a flight on your own. The American College of Chest Physicians advises against using aspirin or blood thinners to prevent clots in long-distance travelers. Aspirin is not a substitute for moving, and it is not a substitute for compression socks either. If your care team already has you on aspirin or a blood thinner for another reason, keep taking it exactly as they told you. Any blood-thinner decision for a flight belongs to your care team, not to this guide and not to the pharmacy at the airport.

7. After landing: the two-week watch, and the care-team conversation

The flight ends at baggage claim, but the clot window does not. Most travel-associated clots occur within the first one to two weeks after the flight, so the watch continues at home. If you notice any of the signs below in the two weeks after a long flight, act on them the same day.

  • DVT warning signs, in one leg or arm: swelling; pain or tenderness you cannot explain; skin that is warm to the touch; redness or discoloration of the skin. About half of people with DVT have no symptoms at all, so a leg that looks or feels different after a flight is worth a call even if it does not hurt much. Contact your doctor as soon as possible.
  • PE warning signs, which mean emergency care: difficulty breathing; a faster than normal or irregular heartbeat; chest pain or discomfort that usually worsens with a deep breath or coughing; anxiety; coughing or coughing up blood; lightheadedness or fainting. If you have any of these, seek medical help immediately. Do not wait to see whether it passes, and do not drive yourself.

Book the care-team conversation before the trip, not from a hotel room after landing. The pre-trip appointment guide covers the full appointment, and these are the clot questions to add to it:

  • Should I wear compression socks on this flight, and if so, what level?
  • Do I have circulation problems, nerve damage, or foot sores that change the answer?
  • I have had a blood clot before, or I take blood thinners: is there anything different I should do for this flight?
  • What should I do if my leg swells or hurts in the two weeks after landing, and who do I call?
  • Is there anything about this trip that changes the plan: a very long flight, several legs in a row, or a recent surgery or injury?

Packing for the seat is small, and it rides with the rest of the diabetes kit, counted with the 20% extra buffer this site uses on every trip:

  • Below-knee graduated compression socks, fitted and tried on before the trip, if your care team says they suit you.
  • Loose clothing for the flight, and a change of socks for after landing.
  • A water bottle you can refill on the plane.
  • Sugar packets for lows: one packet at 60 to 70, two packets below 60, then check again.
  • A blood glucose meter and strips, for the moments when CGM readings do not match how you feel.
  • Your travel insurance emergency number, saved in your phone and written on paper. The travel-insurance guide covers what the policy should include.

If you have no extra risk factors, the plan is the same, just lighter. The CDC is clear that the risk of a clot is generally very small, and lowest for travelers with no other risk factors. You still move every hour or two, you still pick the aisle seat when you can, and you still watch your legs for two weeks after landing. Compression socks are optional for you, not required, and the guideline does not recommend them for travelers with no extra risk. The flight is the moment to move. The two weeks after it are the moment to watch.

Seat sorted? Sort the rest of the travel day. A long flight rarely ends at landing: delays, connections, and long waits in the terminal are part of the same sitting problem. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How long does a flight have to be before blood clots are a risk?

A: The CDC counts any trip where you sit still for more than four hours as long-distance travel, whether it is a flight, a drive, a train, or a bus. The CDC's Yellow Book says long-distance air travel might raise the overall risk of a clot two to four times, but the data are inconsistent, and the absolute risk stays low: two studies put it at about 1 in 4,656 and 1 in 6,000 person-flights for flights over four hours. For flights under four hours, the risk of a clot with symptoms is negligible. The risk climbs with the length of the trip and with any other risk factors you already have.

Q: Do compression socks actually prevent blood clots on flights?

A: Partly, and the honest version matters. A Cochrane review of 12 trials with 2,918 airline passengers on flights over five hours found high-certainty evidence that graduated compression stockings cut symptomless clots, the ones found on scans in people who felt fine, from 47 cases without stockings to 3 with them. But no deaths, no lung clots, and no clots with symptoms happened in either group, so the review could not tell whether stockings prevent the clots that actually hurt you. The same review found low-certainty evidence that stockings reduce leg swelling. Guidelines still recommend them for higher-risk travelers, based on that indirect evidence plus the low risk of wearing them.

Q: What compression level should I buy for a flight?

A: Travel socks are most commonly sold at 15 to 20 mmHg, which is millimeters of mercury, the unit compression is measured in. That is the usual travel range. The chest physicians' guideline makes a weak recommendation, based on low or very low-quality evidence, of 15 to 30 mmHg at the ankle for at-risk travelers on trips over six hours. The firmer 20 to 30 mmHg range is a step up that a doctor may recommend if you are at higher risk. Buy below-knee graduated socks, tightest at the ankle and looser up the calf, fitted to your ankle and calf measurements, and try them on before the trip, not at the airport. If you have circulation problems, nerve damage, or foot sores, ask your care team which level, if any, is right for you before buying.

Q: Can I wear compression socks if I have diabetes?

A: Often yes, but ask your care team first, because diabetes alone is not a yes or no answer. A 2020 study in BMJ Open Diabetes Research and Care put medical compression stockings on 44 people with diabetes and 45 people with peripheral artery disease, which is narrowed arteries in the legs, and found no compression-related adverse events and no reduced blood flow in the skin while sitting or standing. But those patients were screened first: the diabetes group had type 2 diabetes, palpable foot pulses, no wounds, and enough sensation to feel a problem. The study was short, and it measured blood flow, not clots on a flight. If you have peripheral artery disease, severe nerve damage, foot ulcers or sores, or uncontrolled diabetes, your care team should decide whether compression is safe for you.

Q: What are the warning signs of a blood clot after a flight?

A: A clot in the leg, called deep vein thrombosis or DVT, most often shows as swelling in one leg or arm, pain or tenderness you cannot explain, skin that is warm to the touch, or redness or discoloration of the skin. About half of people with DVT have no symptoms at all. If you notice any of these, contact your doctor as soon as possible. A clot that has traveled to the lungs, called a pulmonary embolism or PE, shows as difficulty breathing, a faster than normal or irregular heartbeat, chest pain that usually worsens with a deep breath or coughing, anxiety, coughing or coughing up blood, or lightheadedness or fainting. Those symptoms mean seek medical help immediately. Most travel clots show up in the first one to two weeks after the flight, so keep watching your legs after you land.

Q: How often should I get up and move on a long flight?

A: Get up and walk around every one to two hours, and move your legs in your seat between walks. The CDC suggests extending your legs straight out and flexing your ankles, pulling your toes toward you, pulling each knee up toward your chest and holding it for 15 seconds up to 10 times, raising and lowering your heels with your toes on the floor, raising and lowering your toes with your heels on the floor, and tightening and releasing your leg muscles. The NHS says to get up and move around every hour or so. An aisle seat helps, because in one study window-seat travelers had about twice the clot risk of aisle-seat travelers, probably because aisle passengers are freer to get up.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your clot risk, your compression socks, and your treatment decisions are conversations with your care team, ideally at an appointment four to six weeks before your next trip. Guidance on blood clots, compression levels, and flying after a clot changes over time, so confirm the current details for your destination and your health before you travel. If you develop the warning signs of a blood clot after a flight, seek urgent or emergency care right away.
