Train Guide

Train Travel with Type 1 Diabetes: The Rail Playbook (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 9 min read

Road trips, cruises, and flights all have guides on this site. Trains were the missing one, and Thanksgiving week is the right moment to fix that, with Christmas-market travelers crossing Europe by rail in the same weeks. Here is the short version before the detail: trains are the easiest transport mode for type 1 diabetes (T1D). There is no airport checkpoint on Amtrak, your bags stay with you for the whole ride, you can bring your own food, and there is an outlet at your seat. The hard parts are different from flying: trains run late, the WiFi drops in tunnels and rural stretches, and meal times slide with the schedule. This guide covers all of it, in the order you will meet it.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets. This guide does not describe a train trip I took. Every rule below comes from the train operators' own published pages, Dexcom, the insulin label, or the American Diabetes Association (ADA), and every place where the rule runs out is marked as such. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before a long train trip about your own plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. Train schedules, dining service, and onboard amenities change over time, so confirm the current details for your route before you travel, and ask your care team whether the trip is safe for you, especially if you have had a severe low, lost awareness of lows, or have another condition that affects your safety on the road.

This guide is the rail companion to the rest of the site, and it does not repeat them. The road trip guide covers driving days, the cruise playbook covers ships, and the TSA rules guide covers the airport checkpoint this guide skips. The CGM sharing playbook covers the connectivity rules in full, the time zone guide covers insulin timing across zones, and the packing list covers what goes in the bag. Read the packing list before this one if you have not packed yet, and keep this guide for the ride itself.

1. Boarding: no airport checkpoint

The biggest difference between a train and a plane is the first five minutes. Amtrak does not run an airport-style checkpoint. There is no metal detector, no body scanner, and no bag X-ray as a routine step before boarding. You walk into the station, find your platform, and get on the train. Your pump and your CGM (continuous glucose monitor) stay on, your supplies stay in your bag, and nobody asks you to take your shoes off or pour out a drink. That is the whole boarding procedure for diabetes purposes.

Two honest caveats. First, random bag inspections do happen from time to time, so keep your supplies in a bag you can open quickly and keep the prescription copies in your phone. Second, carry a photo ID. Amtrak requires passengers 15 and older to present acceptable photo identification when buying tickets, checking baggage, and onboard when paying fares or if otherwise requested. A standard driver's license works; the REAL ID rule for domestic flights does not apply to trains.

If you need a hand with bags at the station, Amtrak's Red Cap service is free at major stations, including New York, Chicago, Los Angeles, Seattle, Washington DC, Boston South Station, and Philadelphia. Red Caps are uniformed Amtrak staff who carry bags, help passengers with disabilities and senior citizens, and operate wheelchair lifts and ramps. Accept help only from a uniformed Red Cap. If you travel with a wheelchair or need step-free boarding, ask when you book: Amtrak sleeper cars include an Accessible Bedroom on the entry level with space for a wheelchair, and coach cars have accessible seating.

2. Your bags stay with you

This is the rail advantage that matters most for diabetes. Amtrak lets each passenger bring one personal item, up to 25 lbs and 14 by 11 by 7 inches, plus two carry-on bags, up to 50 lbs and 28 by 22 by 14 inches each. Those bags stay with you: the personal item goes under the seat, the carry-ons go in the overhead rack or the luggage tower at the end of the car. Your insulin, your pump supplies, your CGM spares, and your sugar packets ride at your feet or over your head for the entire trip, never in a hold you cannot reach.

Checked baggage is the trap to avoid. It is only offered on some trains and stations, and once a bag is checked you cannot reach it until arrival. That is a bad place for anything diabetes-related, and the rule is the same as on every other mode of travel on this site: all diabetes supplies ride in the bag at your seat, never in checked luggage. Pack 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says, because a train delay stretches the trip, and the extra supplies turn a late train into an inconvenience instead of a crisis.

Keep the day bag where you can reach it without standing up. The personal item under the seat is the right home for the low kit: sugar packets, your fingerstick meter, and your glucagon device. The bigger bag in the overhead rack holds the spares. If you sleep in a coach seat or a sleeper room, the low kit stays within arm's reach of the bed, not in the luggage tower.

3. Eating on the train

Long-distance Amtrak trains have a dining car or flexible dining service, and most trains have a cafe car selling snacks, sandwiches, and drinks. If you book a private sleeping car room, meals are included in the fare, with a menu covering breakfast, lunch, and dinner. Coach passengers buy food in the cafe car or the dining car. The food itself is ordinary train food: sandwiches, salads, hot entrees, coffee, and snacks. Nothing about it is diabetes-specific, so eat the way you eat everywhere else on a trip.

My travel eating routine works on a train exactly as it works on land. A low-carb, high-protein breakfast stabilizes the day, and I order protein-first for the rest of the day. For the big dining-car dinner, pre-bolus the way you do at home. The one train-specific wrinkle is timing: meal service follows the train's schedule, and the train's schedule slips, so lunch can land an hour late. Carry food that covers a slipped meal, and bolus when you eat, not when the clock says you should have eaten.

Bringing your own food is allowed, and for diabetes it is the most reliable option. Amtrak allows your own food and drinks for consumption at your seat or in a private sleeping car room. Two limits from Amtrak itself: staff cannot heat your food in their ovens or store it in their refrigerators, so pack food that is ready to eat, and food in the dining and lounge cars is limited to what you buy there. Amtrak also warns that trains are sometimes delayed and food supplies may become limited, which is the cafe car running low on a late train. Pack more food than the trip should need, keep sugar packets in your pocket rather than in the bag, and grab extras at every station stop with a coffee shop.

4. Delays: plan for the train to be late

Here is the number to plan around. Amtrak's September 2026 Host Railroad Report put long-distance customer on-time performance at 50.0 percent, which counts passengers who arrived within 15 minutes of the scheduled time. Passengers who arrived late averaged 98 minutes behind schedule. The 12-month rolling figure was 55.4 percent, against a federal standard of 80 percent. In plain terms: on a long-distance train, arriving late is common, and a late arrival of around an hour and a half is ordinary. The planning rule is the same either way: the train will probably not arrive when the timetable says it will.

What a delay does to the diabetes plan is simple: it stretches it. A meal you expected at noon lands at 1:30, the cafe car runs low, and a connection gets tight. The fix is also simple, and it is all packed before you leave. Carry enough food and fast-acting carbs to cover the trip plus the delay, not just the trip. Keep your phone charged so the CGM low alarm stays loud and you can check the train status. Treat a low early rather than waiting for the next station, because the next station is the thing that moved. If a connection is at risk, tell the conductor: they know the connection picture, and they can tell you whether the connecting train is held or missed.

If you get sick on the train, treat it as a sick day at home: keep taking your insulin, and test ketones every 4 to 6 hours, because the ADA lists nausea and vomiting among the warning signs of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous buildup of ketones when the body runs short of insulin. Pack ketone strips in your day bag next to the sugar packets. The long layovers and delays playbook covers the same delay logic for flights, and the sick-day playbook covers getting ill on a trip in full.

5. Power and staying connected

Coach seats on Amtrak come with an electric outlet at the seat, plus a reading light and a tray table. That is the charging plan: phone, CGM receiver, and pump controller charge at your seat for the whole ride. Bring a power bank anyway, as backup for a dead outlet or a seat change, and keep the charging cable in the day bag, not in the overhead rack. Our device charging guide covers the full power kit for any trip.

WiFi is the weaker link. Amtrak offers free basic WiFi on select trains and stations, and the company is clear about what it does: general web browsing only, no streaming music or video, no large downloads. It is not on every train, and it drops in tunnels and rural stretches where the cell signal feeding it disappears. That matters for CGM sharing, and the rule is the one from our CGM sharing playbook, re-checked for this guide: Dexcom Follow and LibreLinkUp need an internet connection on both your phone and your follower's phone. Your own readings keep working through a dead zone, because the sensor talks to your phone over Bluetooth, a short-range wireless link that needs no internet. Sharing is the part that pauses, and your followers see a frozen number until the connection returns. Before the trip, text your followers the hours you will be offline, so a blank screen reads as "they are on a train" instead of "something is wrong." Keep your CGM low alarm loud, and pack a fingerstick meter where you can reach it, because the meter works with no signal at all.

6. Sleeping on the train

Overnight trains are where rail travel earns its keep, and the diabetes plan for a night on a train is mostly the plan for a night anywhere, with the bed moving. On Amtrak, private rooms come in four types: Roomettes, with two seats by day that become upper and lower beds by night; Bedrooms, with twice the space and an in-room sink, restroom, and shower; Family Rooms for two adults and two children; and Accessible Bedrooms on the entry level with space for a wheelchair. Every private room includes complimentary meals, lounge access at major stations, and priority boarding, and a dedicated First Class attendant provides turndown service, helps with meals, and helps with luggage. Coach is the cheaper overnight option: wide reclining seats with legroom, no middle seat, and the same outlet at the seat.

On a sleeper, the overnight low plan is the same as the hotel-room rules in our solo travel playbook: CGM low alarm loud enough to wake you, sugar packets and glucagon within arm's reach of the bed, not in the luggage tower, and your phone charging beside you. If you travel with a companion, they sleep knowing where the glucagon is, the way the glucagon guide describes. If you travel alone, the remote system from the solo playbook is the backup: CGM sharing with a home contact who can see your numbers and call for help if you stop answering, with the understanding that sharing pauses in dead zones, as section 5 explains.

Europe's overnight trains work the same way, on a smaller scale. Austria's OBB Nightjet runs overnight trains across central Europe with three comfort levels: seats, couchette compartments for 4 to 6 people with bunks and bedding, and sleeping car compartments for 1 to 3 people with made-up beds and a breakfast in the morning. A bed in a sleeping car includes lounge access, mineral water, a snack, a welcome drink, and breakfast. The diabetes rules do not change with the border: sugar and glucagon within reach of the bunk, CGM alarm loud, and the sharing caveat for rural stretches.

7. Trains in Europe: Eurostar and Christmas-market trains

European trains split into two kinds for diabetes purposes, and Eurostar's London trains are the stricter kind. Eurostar's own Help Centre says its security checks are very similar to those at an airport, just without the limits on liquids. Bags go through an X-ray, items may need to come out of luggage before scanning, and passport and border checks for both countries happen before you board, so you walk straight off the train on arrival. The diabetes-friendly difference is the liquids rule: unlike airlines, Eurostar puts no volume limit on liquids, so full-size toiletries and drinks pass through security. Luggage is generous, with two bags up to 85 cm plus one small hand luggage for Standard and Plus adult tickets, and there are no food restrictions on board, so you can bring your own food from home.

On trains between Belgium, France, the Netherlands, and Germany, there is no security or passport check at all. Eurostar's guidance is to look for your train on the departure board and head straight to the platform, arriving at the station 30 minutes before departure. Keep your passport and your diabetes travel letter handy anyway: border checks still happen on international routes that are not Eurostar, and our diabetes travel letter guide covers what the letter should say. For the December trips, the same rules carry the Christmas-market traveler: our Christmas markets playbook covers cold weather, market food, and long days on foot, and this guide covers the train that gets you there.

8. Time zones on a cross-country train

A cross-country train crosses time zones slowly, which makes the insulin timing shift gentler than a flight but no less real. The framework is the same one in our time zone guide, and it is about timing only, never dose amounts.

If you use an insulin pump, change the pump clock to local time when you arrive. Most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, so this is one manual step, and it matters: get the clock wrong and every basal rate, the background insulin the pump drips all day, fires at the wrong hour. Meal insulin does not change at all: bolus when you eat, wherever you are. If you take multiple daily injections, meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch, and long-acting insulin follows home time on the travel day: keep your watch or phone on home time while you ride, take your once-daily long-acting dose at its usual time, and when you arrive, switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone. If you take background insulin twice a day, keep the two doses roughly 12 hours apart while traveling. Small shifts of three time zones or fewer barely disturb the rhythm. On the big jumps, east or west, the adjustment is a question for your care team before the trip, not a guess on the train. Run the full route through our free time zone planner before you ride.

9. Insulin temperature on a train

Trains are heated in winter and air-conditioned in summer, so the temperature risk on a train is smaller than on a road trip or a beach day, but the label numbers still rule. Unopened insulin belongs in a refrigerator at 36 to 46F. In-use insulin is fine below 86F for up to 28 days. Insulin freezes at 32F, and frozen insulin must be discarded, even after it thaws. The storage numbers above are the Humalog label numbers, and other insulins have their own labels, so check yours.

The practical rules follow from the numbers. Keep unopened insulin in the bag at your seat, not in checked baggage and not in a cold luggage compartment in winter. Keep in-use insulin in the day bag, away from direct heat from a vent or a sunny window. If the trip is long enough that unopened insulin needs to stay cool for hours, a FRIO cooling wallet keeps insulin at safe temperatures with no ice and no electricity, which is the same answer as on every other mode of travel. Our insulin cooling guide covers the full temperature playbook, and the Thanksgiving day playbook covers the holiday table at the other end of the ride.

Train travel checklist

Here is the full list, in this order:

Train Travel with Type 1 Diabetes: Packing and Planning Checklist

  • All diabetes supplies in the bag at your seat, never in checked baggage; pack 20% extra supplies, prescription copies in your phone
  • Low kit in the personal item under the seat: sugar packets (1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60), fingerstick meter, glucagon device
  • Photo ID in your pocket; accept bag help only from a uniformed Red Cap at major stations
  • Food for the trip plus a delay: ready-to-eat meals and snacks, since Amtrak staff cannot heat or refrigerate your food and cafe supplies can run limited on a late train
  • Phone charger and a power bank in the day bag; charge at the seat outlet
  • Text your CGM followers your offline hours before the trip; keep the low alarm loud; sharing pauses in tunnels and rural dead zones
  • Overnight: sugar and glucagon within arm's reach of the bed, CGM alarm loud, phone charging beside you
  • Eurostar London trains: airport-like security and passport checks before boarding, no liquids volume limit, bring your own food; other European trains: straight to the platform, arrive 30 minutes before departure
  • Pump: change the pump clock to local time on arrival; long-acting insulin on home time on the travel day; run the route through the time zone planner before you ride
  • Insulin temperature: unopened at 36 to 46F, in-use below 86F up to 28 days, discard if frozen; keep insulin in the bag at your seat
  • Pack ketone strips; if you get sick, test ketones every 4 to 6 hours

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do I go through airport-style security to board an Amtrak train?

A: No. Amtrak does not run an airport-style checkpoint: there is no metal detector, no body scanner, and no bag X-ray as a routine step before boarding, so your pump and CGM stay on and your supplies stay in your bag. Random bag inspections do happen from time to time, so keep supplies in a bag you can open quickly. Eurostar is the exception: its London trains use airport-like security with bag X-ray and passport checks before departure.

Q: Where should I keep my insulin and diabetes supplies on a train?

A: In the bag at your seat, never in checked baggage. Amtrak lets each passenger bring one personal item and two carry-on bags, and carry-on bags stay with you in the overhead rack or luggage tower. Checked baggage is only offered on some trains, and once a bag is checked you cannot reach it until arrival. Keep the day bag under the seat or at your feet, with sugar packets in your pocket, and pack 20 percent extra supplies.

Q: Can I bring my own food on an Amtrak train?

A: Yes. Amtrak allows your own food and drinks for consumption at your seat or in a private sleeping car room. Amtrak staff cannot heat your food or store it in their refrigerators, so pack food that is ready to eat. The cafe car sells snacks, sandwiches, and drinks on most trains, and private-room fares include meals, but Amtrak warns that food supplies can run limited when trains are delayed, so carry more than the trip should need.

Q: What happens to my CGM sharing on a train with tunnels and rural stretches?

A: Your own readings keep working: the sensor talks to your phone over Bluetooth, which does not need the internet. Sharing is the part that pauses. Dexcom Follow and LibreLinkUp need an internet connection on both your phone and your follower's phone, so a tunnel, a rural stretch, or a dead WiFi zone freezes what your followers see until the connection returns. Tell your followers your offline hours before the trip, and keep your CGM low alarm loud.

Q: Do I need to change my pump clock or insulin timing on a cross-country train?

A: Your pump clock, yes, when you arrive: change it to local time so your basal rates run on the new schedule, because most pumps do not adjust their own clocks. Meal insulin goes with meals whenever you eat. If you take long-acting insulin once a day, keep it on home time on the travel day and switch to your usual clock time in the new time zone on arrival. That is timing only: dose amounts stay with your care team.

Q: How is Eurostar different from Amtrak for a traveler with type 1 diabetes?

A: Eurostar's London trains work like a short flight at the station: airport-like security with bags through an X-ray, passport and border checks for both countries before you board, and gates that close before departure. The diabetes-friendly difference is that Eurostar puts no volume limit on liquids, so full-size toiletries and drinks pass through security. On trains between Belgium, France, the Netherlands, and Germany, there is no security or passport check: you walk to the platform, and Eurostar recommends arriving 30 minutes before departure.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your insulin plan, your glucose targets, and your treatment decisions are conversations with your care team, ideally at an appointment four to six weeks before your next trip. Train schedules, dining service, and onboard amenities change over time, so confirm the current details for your route before you travel. If you have a severe low on a train, tell the conductor and the people around you, treat it with the plan your care team set, and seek medical care at the next station.
