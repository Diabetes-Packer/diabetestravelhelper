Traveling with Diabetes in Spain: The Pharmacy, the Food, and the Trip (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Winter is the season this guide is written for: Christmas markets in Madrid and Barcelona, New Year trips to Madrid and Seville, and winter sun in the Canary Islands. It is also the third country on this site to get its own destination guide, after Japan and Mexico, and that is not an accident. Spain is one of the few places where the diabetes rules are genuinely different from the ones you already know: a three-month limit on the medicine you carry, a permit for controlled medicines, a pharmacy system that needs a Spanish prescription, and a food label system that lists the carbs in grams per 100 grams. None of it is hard. All of it is specific. This guide covers the import rules, the pharmacy, and the trip itself, in that order, so you can sort the paperwork at home and enjoy the trip once you land.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. I have not traveled to Spain with diabetes. This guide is not a trip report, and it does not pretend to be one. It is built from the published guidance of Spain's medicines agency, the Agencia Española de Medicamentos y Productos Sanitarios (AEMPS), the agency that regulates medicine in Spain, the United States Department of State, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the European Medicines Agency (EMA), the United Kingdom's Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO), the European Union's food information law, and Spain's weather agency, AEMET, the State Meteorological Agency. Where a rule comes from one of those sources, this guide says so. Where something is my own travel habit, it is labeled as mine, and it is kept separate from the official rules. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own travel plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not going, tell your care team.

1. The import rule in one paragraph, then the details

Spain does not run a certificate system like Japan's. There is no import form to file before you fly, and no office that stamps your insulin. The paperwork is a prescription and a letter, and the rule is that the letter must match the bag. Spain's medicines agency, the Agencia Española de Medicamentos y Productos Sanitarios (AEMPS), the agency that regulates medicine in Spain, sets the traveler rule: you may carry your personal medicine for up to three months of treatment, in its original packaging, and border officials may ask to see your prescription or a medical report. Our customs and border rules guide covers the general version of this system for any country, and our international travel guide covers the wide version of traveling abroad with diabetes. Spain is where the general version gets specific.

The three-month limit is the number that matters before anything else. The amount you carry must match your prescribed treatment, and AEMPS accepts a larger amount only as a justified exception, so do not pack a year of insulin and hope for the best. Carry the prescription too, and keep every medicine in its original pharmacy container with the label on it, so an officer can match the medicine to the paper without a conversation. The United States Department of State makes the same point on its Spain page: always carry your prescription medicine in its original packaging, along with your doctor's prescription, and check with the Spanish embassy to make sure each medicine is legal in Spain. If the trip runs longer than the supply you can carry, our long-stay abroad playbook covers the planning for a trip that outlasts the supply in your bag.

Two more rules from the same State Department page. First, do not ship medicine to Spain. Spanish rules do not permit the international shipment of medicines, Spanish customs will reject and return any medicine sent from the United States, and the United States Embassy cannot help you get back medicine taken by customs. Carry it with you. Second, no visa is needed for a stay under 90 days, and no vaccine is required to enter Spain. Our travel letter guide covers what the letter should say, and our pre-trip appointment guide covers the full appointment, including asking for generic-name prescriptions before you leave. A prescription that lists the generic name is the one that travels. A brand name that only exists in your home country does not.

2. Insulin at the border: pens, syringes, and what to carry

Insulin travels the same way it travels everywhere: in the bag that stays with you, in its original box with the pharmacy label on it. Syringes have one packing rule of their own. Keep the syringes with the injectable medicine they accompany, and make sure the doctor's letter mentions them. Do not plan to bring a loose syringe on its own. Pack the syringes with the insulin they belong to, and list them in the letter, so the letter and the bag agree with each other.

Two habits from my own travel routine, kept separate from the border rules. Everything diabetes-related rides in my carry-on, never in checked luggage, and I pack 20 percent extra of everything: pods, sensors, insulin, test strips, lancets, ketone strips, and the low kit. Our packing list does the counting for the trip length. The 20 percent buffer covers the pod that fails on a cold morning in Madrid and the sensor that peels off in the heat of the Canary Islands. Bring backup insulin for injections in case a pod fails mid-trip, and keep your insulin plan written down somewhere that is not the pump. The flight there follows the same airport rules as any trip, covered in our TSA rules guide for the United States departure and our airport security abroad guide for the arrival side.

3. What needs a permit, and what is not sold here

Some medicines need a permit before you travel, and no letter fixes a missing one. Medicines that contain a narcotic or psychotropic substance need a permit from AEMPS before you enter Spain, and you must apply at least 10 days before you travel. That covers opioid painkillers such as morphine, fentanyl, and oxycodone, benzodiazepines such as diazepam, and stimulant medicines for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) such as methylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine. The permit, once issued, is valid for three months. Check every medicine by its active ingredient, not its brand name, before you pack. The active ingredient is what the law sees. The brand name is not.

Adderall is the one that catches travelers by surprise. Adderall, the mixed amphetamine salts used for ADHD, is not sold in Spain. No medicine with that combination is authorized there, so a Spanish pharmacy cannot supply it. Methylphenidate is sold in Spain as Rubifen, Medikinet, Concerta, and Equasym, among others, and lisdexamfetamine is sold as Elvanse. If you take Adderall, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about switching to a medicine that is sold in Spain, and ask AEMPS about the permit for the medicine you carry. The answer cannot be found at the border.

Cannabis is a separate problem. Cannabis is not legal in Spain. Private personal use by an adult is not treated as a crime, but carrying or using cannabis in public is fined, and selling it or carrying it across the border is a criminal matter. Do not bring cannabis, THC, or CBD products into Spain. Leave them at home.

4. The pharmacy: insulin needs a prescription, and yours from home does not work here

Spain's pharmacy system is regulated by AEMPS, the agency that does the job the Food and Drug Administration does in the United States. The pharmacy on the corner is called a farmacia, and you find it by the green cross, the cruz verde, in the window. Medicines in Spain are sold only in pharmacies: supermarkets do not sell them. Most pharmacies keep split hours, about 9:30 to 2:00 and 5:00 to 9:30 on weekdays, with shorter hours on Saturday mornings, and a rota of duty pharmacies, called farmacias de guardia, covers nights, Sundays, and public holidays. A closed pharmacy posts the address of the nearest one on duty on its door, so you are never far from an open one. The system works well once you understand it, and the first thing to understand is that it is the opposite of Mexico's in the way that matters most: insulin is not sold over the counter. It needs a prescription.

So the pharmacy visit, if you need one, has a fixed sequence. First, see a doctor in Spain. A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in Spain: the State Department says medicines that need a prescription in the United States also need a local doctor's prescription in Spain, and some medicines prescribed in the United States do not have a local equivalent. There is one exception, and it matters if you travel from elsewhere in Europe. A prescription written by a doctor in one European Union (EU) country is valid in all other EU countries, so a prescription from France or Germany, for example, can be filled at a Spanish farmacia, although the medicine may have another name or may not be sold in Spain at all. Bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product. Tell the doctor the generic name of your insulin, not just the brand: insulin aspart, insulin lispro, insulin glargine, insulin degludec. The pharmacist and the doctor both work from the generic name, and the generic name is what survives the brand change at the border. NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid almost everywhere else, and both are insulin aspart. Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine. Our finding insulin abroad playbook covers the name game in full, including the concentration check that matters more than the brand name.

Two more things to know before you walk in. First, the familiar insulins are sold in Spain. Lantus, insulin glargine, holds a marketing authorization valid throughout the European Union, granted by the European Commission on 9 June 2000, and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) says it can only be obtained with a prescription. NovoRapid, insulin aspart, was authorized in the European Union on 7 September 1999, and Tresiba, insulin degludec, holds European Commission authorizations covering all European Union member states from January 2013. Most insulin pens in Spain use the same U-100 standard, 100 units in each milliliter, so a pen dial counts the same way it does at home. Tresiba is the exception to watch for: it also comes in a pen at 200 units per milliliter, so check the concentration printed on the box before you use it, because a syringe is calibrated for one concentration only. Second, visitors pay. Medical care is not free in Spain, and the State Department says United States Medicare and Medicaid do not cover expenses abroad. Keep every receipt for your travel insurance claim, and check your policy before the trip so the clinic visit is not the first time you read it. Our travel insurance guide covers what to declare and what to look for.

5. Your devices in Spain: the app, the sensors, and the sun

Start with the phone, because it holds the one trap that ruins more international trips than any other. The Dexcom G7 app is region-locked at install: Dexcom's own user guide states the app must be installed in your country of residence, and the app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when you first install it. The check happens at install time. An app that is already installed and working keeps working abroad, and travelers report normal operation on international trips as long as nobody deletes the app mid-trip. So install the G7 app and update it to the latest version before you leave, turn off auto-updates before the trip so nothing about your diabetes setup changes mid-trip without your say-so, and do not delete the app while you are in Spain. A deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country, and that is a long trip without CGM data. Our G7 app region-lock guide covers the full prep list, and nothing in it is specific to Spain: the same rule applies everywhere you land.

The receiver is the backup that makes the app problem a non-issue. Dexcom's own international travel guidance says that if you have a receiver, it makes a great backup to your phone app. The receiver has no app store, no region check, and no install step. It pairs with your sensor and shows readings anywhere on earth. Pack it with its USB cable and charger, plus a plug adapter for Spain. Our devices charging guide covers plugs and power on the road. If you do not already own a receiver, ask Dexcom about getting one before a big international trip. Bring the fingerstick meter too, as the final fallback, and keep it in your day bag rather than buried in your hotel room.

Buying replacement sensors in Spain is a pharmacy problem, not a shop problem. The G7 is sold in Spain: the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) lists Spain among the countries where the G7 is distributed, and the launch of the Omnipod 5 in Spain in July 2026 lists the FreeStyle Libre 2 Plus and the Dexcom G7 as the compatible sensors in Spain. A replacement sensor bought in Spain still means a foreign pharmacy, a local prescription, and regional compatibility questions you do not want to solve on vacation. Pack more sensors than the trip math says you need, with the spares in your carry-on, and do not plan to shop. The same applies to pump supplies. Carry spare Pods and a backup injection plan with syringes and a vial, and keep your basal rates written down somewhere that is not the pump. Our pod failure backup plan covers the sequence when a Pod fails away from home.

Then there is the sun, which is the one place where your devices meet a real limit on a Spain trip, even in winter. Dexcom publishes exact temperature numbers for the G7. The sensor's operating range is 50F to 107.6F, and its storage range is 36F to 86F. A worn sensor sits against warm skin, so a normal day does not push it out of range, but a long day in direct sun on a terrace in Seville or a beach in the Canary Islands can push a spare past its storage ceiling. Keep sun exposure short where you can, and check the readings against your fingerstick meter after a long hot day. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. Heat loosens adhesive too. Press the sensor and pod edges down firmly after swimming, and pack extra overpatches. Our G7 heat and cold guide has the full temperature table, and our adhesion playbook covers keeping a sensor and a pump stuck through holiday trips.

6. Time zones: two clocks, and the one-hour gap

Spain keeps two clocks, and the Canary Islands run one hour behind the mainland all year. Peninsular Spain and the Balearic Islands use Central European Time (CET), which is 1 hour ahead of Coordinated Universal Time, written as UTC+1, in winter, and Central European Summer Time (CEST), UTC+2, in summer. The Canary Islands use Western European Time (WET), UTC+0, in winter, and Western European Summer Time (WEST), UTC+1, in summer. Both clocks move on the same European dates: forward on the last Sunday in March and back on the last Sunday in October. The gap between Spain and home changes only because home moves its clocks, not because Spain does. From the United States West Coast, Madrid is 9 hours ahead all year, and the Canary Islands are 8 hours ahead. From the United States East Coast, Madrid is 6 hours ahead all year, and the Canary Islands are 5 hours ahead. From London, Madrid is 1 hour ahead all year, and the Canary Islands keep the same time as London. Either way, it is a large enough jump that the insulin timing needs a plan before the flight, not after landing.

The plan follows the rule in our time zone insulin guide. If you use a pump, change the pump clock to local time when you land, so your basal rates, the background insulin the pump drips all day, run on local time. Most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, so this is one manual step at the gate or the hotel. If you take multiple daily injections, keep your long-acting insulin on home time on the travel day, then switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone when you arrive, for example bedtime. Meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch: take it when you eat, wherever you are. The guide covers the eastbound and westbound cases in full, and the timing shift for a Spain trip is large enough that the careful planning earns its keep. Run your route through our time zone planner before you fly, and confirm the plan with your care team. The planner adjusts timing only, never dose amounts. Dose changes are a conversation with your care team, not something a tool or a blog post should decide.

7. Eating in Spain: the label that lists the carbs

Spain is one of the easiest countries in the world to count carbs in, once you know where to look, and one of the hardest if you do not. The split is between packaged food and restaurant food, and the supermarket sits squarely on the easy side. Under the European Union's food information law, Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, the nutrition declaration is mandatory on pre-packed food, and it must list energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, and salt. The carbohydrate number is in grams, stated per 100 grams or per 100 milliliters, and the carbohydrate line reads hidratos de carbono, with sugars listed under it. The label is in Spanish, so learn the words for the nutrition panel, or point your phone's camera at it with a translation app. Packaged food from a supermarket, a bakery, or a train station kiosk all carries the same table, which makes a sandwich, a yogurt, or a pack of crackers a known quantity instead of a guess. This guide gives no carb counts for specific foods, because the number on the package in front of you is the one that counts, and it is printed right there.

Restaurant food has no label, so it works the way eating out works everywhere. Ask what is in the dish, estimate the carbohydrate from the bread, the rice, or the potatoes, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. I pre-bolus in my usual way before meals. That habit travels with me, and Spain does not change it. A plate of paella is mostly the rice, a bocadillo is mostly the bread, and a plate of patatas bravas is mostly the potatoes. Our eating out playbook covers the ordering and estimating routine in full, and our pre-bolus guide covers the timing habit. For lows, keep sugar packets in your day bag. I treat lows with sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, and that is the low kit that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day in any country. Do not let the low kit be something you buy after you need it. Pack it before you leave the hotel.

Water is the other half of eating safely in Spain, and the news is good. The State Department says tap water in Spain is generally safe to drink and use in cooking. Do not drink from a tap or fountain marked agua no potable, which means not drinking water, and if a local notice says otherwise, follow it. A stomach bug is a diabetes problem, not just a comfort problem, because vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the plan for that day.

Vaccines are a question for the care-team appointment, not for the airport. No vaccine is required to enter Spain, and the appointment is the place to check that routine vaccines are up to date. Our travel vaccines playbook covers the appointment in full.

8. Winter in Spain: cold mornings, mild afternoons, and the hotel fridge

Winter is the season this guide is written for, and cold mornings are the thing to plan around. Spain is not one winter. Madrid sits on a high plateau, and its January days average a high of 51F, with nights near freezing: Spain's weather agency, AEMET, the State Meteorological Agency, gives Madrid Airport a January average high of 10.7 degrees Celsius, which is 51 degrees Fahrenheit, and an average low of 0.2 degrees Celsius, which is 32 degrees Fahrenheit. The south coast and the Canary Islands run warmer, and a winter day in Seville or Tenerife can feel like spring, but the inland nights still bite. The good news is that a worn sensor sits against warm skin under your clothes, so normal winter days do not push it out of its operating range. Body heat does the work for you. The weak links are the spares and the phone. Spare sensors must be stored between 36F and 86F, so do not leave them in a freezing car overnight or in an unheated bag on a cold tarmac. Keep them insulated in your carry-on, inside your jacket or bag rather than an outer pocket, and let a cold spare warm up indoors before you put it on. Phones give up in extreme cold long before the sensor does, so keep your phone in an inside pocket on bitter days and bring a portable charger, since cold drains batteries faster. Our skiing playbook covers the cold and altitude routine for a ski trip in the Pyrenees, and the same habits apply to a cold morning walking around Madrid or Segovia.

Insulin follows the same cold rule it follows everywhere. Unopened insulin belongs in the fridge at 36 to 46F, and in-use insulin is fine at room temperature below 77F. Insulin freezes at 32F, and frozen insulin must be discarded. On a winter trip that means care with the hotel fridge for the unopened supply. Hotel minibar fridges can freeze items at the back, so keep unopened insulin away from the cooling element and check that it has not frozen. On a warm day in the Canary Islands, the in-use insulin still needs its cooling wallet, because in-use insulin must stay below 77F. Our insulin cooling guide covers the methods, and our hotel insulin storage guide covers the minibar trap in detail. The one winter habit that matters most is the simplest: keep the supplies on your body or in the bag that stays with you, not in a cold car, a cold room, or a checked bag.

9. If something goes wrong: numbers, clinics, and the insurance call

Save one number in your phone before you land. Dial 112 for ambulance, fire, and police. It is the single European emergency number, it works nationwide, and it is free from any phone. The State Department says you may ask for an English-speaking attendant. If you need the United States Embassy, call +34-91-587-2200 in Madrid, or the Consulate General in Barcelona at +34-93-280-2227. If you travel from the United Kingdom, bring your Global Health Insurance Card (GHIC) or European Health Insurance Card (EHIC): either card covers medically necessary state healthcare in Spain on the same terms as a Spanish resident, but neither card covers private care or getting flown home, and neither card is a replacement for travel insurance. If you travel from the United States, there is no equivalent card: medical care in Spain is not free, and United States Medicare and Medicaid do not cover care abroad. Check your policy before the trip, so the call is not the first time you read it.

If you develop symptoms of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous complication where the blood turns acidic when insulin runs too low, get urgent help right away. Vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation, and that is a hospital visit, not a wait-and-see day. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the ketone checks and the plan for that day. Carry glucagon in your day bag, and make sure your travel companion knows where it is and how to use it. Our glucagon emergency kit guide covers what is in the kit and how to keep it travel-ready. If the trip goes wrong in a bigger way, a bag lost or stolen, our lost or stolen supplies playbook covers the exact call order, and the travel insurance emergency line is the call that arranges care, including a local doctor visit. If the trip involves driving, get an International Driving Permit (IDP) before you leave: Spain requires one to rent a car, and the police may impound the rental car and fine you on the spot without one. Our driving abroad guide covers licenses, rental cars, and the rules of the road.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do I need a permit to bring insulin into Spain?

A: No permit is needed for ordinary insulin. Spain's medicines agency, AEMPS, lets a traveler carry personal medicine for up to three months of treatment, in its original packaging, and border officials may ask to see your prescription or a medical report. The permit is for medicines that contain a narcotic or psychotropic substance: those need an AEMPS permit requested at least 10 days before you travel. Do not ship medicine to Spain: Spanish rules do not permit the international shipment of medicines, and customs will reject and return it.

Q: Can a Spanish pharmacy fill my prescription from home?

A: It depends where the prescription was written. A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in Spain: medicines that need a prescription in the United States also need a local doctor's prescription in Spain. A prescription written by a doctor in another European Union country is valid in Spain, although the medicine may have another name or may not be sold there. If you run out of insulin in Spain, see a doctor in Spain, show your prescription and doctor's letter so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the Spanish prescription, called a receta, to a farmacia.

Q: Can I buy insulin at a pharmacy in Spain without a prescription?

A: No. Insulin needs a prescription in Spain. Lantus, insulin glargine, holds a marketing authorization valid throughout the European Union, and the European Medicines Agency says it can only be obtained with a prescription. See a doctor in Spain for a receta, and bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product.

Q: What medicines need a permit, and what about Adderall?

A: Medicines that contain a narcotic or psychotropic substance need a permit from AEMPS before you travel, requested at least 10 days ahead. That covers opioid painkillers, benzodiazepines, and stimulant medicines for ADHD such as methylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine. Adderall is not sold in Spain: no medicine with that combination is authorized there, so a Spanish pharmacy cannot supply it. If you take Adderall, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about switching to a medicine that is sold in Spain. Cannabis is not legal in Spain, so do not bring cannabis, THC, or CBD products.

Q: Will my Dexcom G7 app work in Spain?

A: Yes, if it was already installed and set up at home. The Dexcom G7 app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when it is first installed, so install and update it before you leave and do not delete it while you are abroad: a deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country. Bring the Dexcom receiver as backup. It has no app store and no install check, and it pairs with your sensor anywhere.

Q: What are the emergency numbers in Spain?

A: Dial 112 for ambulance, fire, and police. It is the single European emergency number, it works nationwide, and it is free from any phone. If you need the United States Embassy, call +34-91-587-2200 in Madrid, or the Consulate General in Barcelona at +34-93-280-2227. If you travel from the United Kingdom, a GHIC or EHIC covers medically necessary state healthcare, but it is not a replacement for travel insurance.
