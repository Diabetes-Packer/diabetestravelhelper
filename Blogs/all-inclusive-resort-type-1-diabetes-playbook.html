Managing Diabetes While Traveling

All-Inclusive Resorts with Type 1 Diabetes: The Buffet, Cocktail, and Beach-Day Playbook (2026)

October is booking season for the winter escape: Mexico, the Caribbean, Hawaii. The flights get bought now, the resort gets toured on a phone screen, and somewhere between the swim-up bar photos and the buffet shots, a traveler with type 1 diabetes does the quiet math on what a week of unlimited food, sweet cocktails, heat, and long beach days does to blood sugar.

The all-inclusive resort breaks every routine at once. Portions are unmeasured, mealtimes drift, alcohol flows from noon, and the heat plus swimming quietly pushes blood sugar down while the buffet pushes it up. None of it is unmanageable. It just needs a playbook, because the resort is the one trip where winging it fails the fastest.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Everybody's diabetes behaves differently in heat, with alcohol, and with activity. Use this guide to plan, then confirm your personal plan, including any insulin adjustments, with your care team before you travel.

The buffet: walk it first, then plate it

A resort buffet is the opposite of a routine meal: no labels, no portions, and a dessert station engineered to end your resolve. The move is to treat the first lap as reconnaissance, not dinner.

  • Walk the full line before taking a plate. Decide the whole meal up front so you are not bolusing for round one and then discovering the carving station on round two. One plate, one plan.
  • Build the protein-first plate. Protein and nonstarchy vegetables go on first, carb sides take about a third of the plate. This is the same plate method that works at the Thanksgiving table, and it works at a buffet for the same reason: it caps the carbs without requiring a single label.
  • Dessert counts with the meal, not after it. If you want dessert, shrink the carb sides to make room and bolus once for the whole plate. A separate dessert bolus an hour later stacks on top of the meal insulin and turns the evening into a guessing game.
  • Take your mealtime insulin a few minutes before you start eating. Buffet food hits fast, especially the starches. A short head start flattens the spike; the pre-bolus timing trick works exactly the same at a resort.
  • Fruit is a carb like any other. The tropical fruit spread looks like the safe choice, and the fruit itself is fine, but a plate of mango and pineapple can carry as many carbs as the pastry table. Count it.

The cocktail carb ranking

The swim-up bar is where the most carbs on the resort hide in plain sight. Alcohol itself has almost no carbs; the mixers, juices, and liqueurs do. Rank your drinks before the bar opens:

  • Distilled spirits: about 0g carbs per shot. Vodka, gin, whiskey, rum, and tequila are effectively carb-free, per both the USDA nutrient database and diabetes.co.uk's alcohol guide. With a diet mixer or soda water, the drink stays near zero.
  • Dry wine: about 2g per small glass. Dry reds, dry whites, and brut sparkling wines sit around 2g per 125ml glass. Sweet and dessert wines run 3 to 5g and up.
  • Regular beer: about 13g per 12 oz. Beer is the carb-heavy outlier among the simple drinks (a Corona runs about 14g per 330ml bottle). Light beers cut that roughly in half.
  • Sugary mixers: up to 39g. Regular cola, tonic, and juice mixers can add up to 39g of carbs to a single drink, per diabetes.co.uk. The mixer, not the spirit, is what you are bolusing for.
  • Frozen and cream-based cocktails: the carb bombs. A frozen cocktail stacks juice, syrup, and liqueur on top of the alcohol. Diabetes.co.uk's rule of thumb applies here: if it sounds sweet, it probably is. Treat these like dessert when you bolus, because carb-wise, that is what they are.

The practical rule: bolus for beer and sweet cocktails the way you would for food. Spirits with diet mixers or soda, and dry wine, need no bolus for the carbs (the alcohol itself is a separate matter, covered next).

The alcohol rules that matter at a resort

Carbs are only half the alcohol story with type 1 diabetes. Alcohol itself can lower blood sugar hours after the drink, which is why the resort pattern, drinks from noon to midnight, deserves its own set of rules. These come from the endocrinologist checklist in our party-night guide, and they apply double at an all-inclusive:

  • Eat protein and fat before drinking. Never drink on an empty stomach. The ADA's guidance is 1 to 2 drinks with food, and a real meal first beats poolside snacks.
  • Skip the sugary mixers. They spike you now and the alcohol drops you later, the worst combination on the menu.
  • Raise your CGM low alarm to 80 to 90 and share your readings. CGM stands for continuous glucose monitor. A higher alarm threshold plus a sober person who can see your numbers (a partner, a friend, anyone at the table) is the safety net for the night.
  • Pick a drinking buddy who knows what a low looks like and what to do about it. Tell them before the first drink, not after the third.
  • Respect the overnight low. Alcohol leaves people with type 1 diabetes vulnerable to hypoglycemia overnight. The bedtime check matters more on drinking nights, and sugar packets on the nightstand are non-negotiable.

Heat, the pool, and the beach

Heat is the quiet variable at a beach resort. Research from the German Diabetes Center (DDZ) found that heat accelerates insulin absorption, which can lead to dangerous hypoglycemia, and that dehydration worsens blood sugar control. Diabetes.co.uk's hot-weather guidance adds that metabolism runs higher in hot, humid weather, raising hypo risk for insulin users. Add swimming and beach walking, and the resort stacks three low-drivers at once: heat, activity, and alcohol.

  • Test or check your CGM more often on hot, active days. Do not assume your home insulin needs apply at 90 degrees after two hours in the pool. Any insulin adjustment for heat or activity goes through your care team.
  • Keep fast-acting carbs within arm's reach at the pool. Sugar packets in a sealed plastic bag survive the pool deck: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, same as home.
  • Drink water steadily. Dehydration concentrates glucose in the blood and worsens control, and it is easy to fall behind when the day runs on cocktails.
  • Know your swim limits before the trip. The Omnipod Pod is rated IP28 (25 feet for 60 minutes) and the Dexcom G7 to 8 feet for 24 hours, but pump controllers are generally not waterproof. Check your own device's rating; the full breakdown is in the cruise playbook.
  • Never leave insulin in the beach bag in direct sun. A bag on hot sand cooks insulin fast. The in-use pen rides in the shade; the spares stay in the room.

Resort insulin storage in one paragraph

The room rules are the same as any hotel: the in-use pen lives at room temperature and never goes back in the fridge, spares stay at 36 to 46F, and the minibar is the trap, so ask for a medical fridge at check-in. For beach days, an evaporative cooling wallet keeps insulin in range without ice. The full rules are in the hotel insulin storage guide and the keep-insulin-cool guide.

The all-inclusive resort checklist

  • Walk the buffet before plating; build the protein-first plate with carb sides to a third.
  • Rank your drinks before the swim-up bar opens: spirits with diet mixers or soda, dry wine, beer and sweet cocktails bolused like food.
  • Protein and fat before drinking; CGM low alarm to 80 to 90 with a sober buddy watching.
  • Sugar packets in a sealed bag at the pool: 1 at 60 to 70, 2 below 60.
  • Check glucose more often on hot, active days; drink water steadily.
  • Medical fridge at check-in, never the minibar freezer compartment; cooling wallet for beach days.
  • Pack 20% more insulin and supplies than the trip math says, split so one lost bag never strands you.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I carb count at an all-inclusive buffet?

You mostly estimate. Walk the full buffet line before taking a plate, build a protein-first plate (protein and nonstarchy vegetables first, carb sides to about a third of the plate), and bolus for the carbs you can see. Dessert counts with the meal, not after it.

Can I drink alcohol with type 1 diabetes at a resort?

Yes, with the rules: eat protein and fat before drinking, never drink on an empty stomach, keep it to 1 to 2 drinks with food per ADA guidance, and set your CGM low alarm to 80 to 90 with a sober buddy watching, because alcohol raises the risk of overnight lows.

Which resort cocktails are lowest in carbs?

Distilled spirits (vodka, gin, whiskey, rum, tequila) with diet mixers or soda water are near zero carbs. Dry wine is about 2g per small glass. Regular beer is about 13g per 12 oz, and sugary mixers can add up to 39g. Frozen and cream-based cocktails are the carb bombs of the swim-up bar.

Does hot weather make blood sugar go low?

It can. Heat speeds up insulin absorption and raises your metabolism, which increases hypo risk, especially combined with swimming and walking. Test more often on hot, active days and keep fast-acting carbs within reach.

Can I swim with my insulin pump and CGM?

It depends on the device. The Omnipod Pod is rated IP28 (25 feet for 60 minutes) and the Dexcom G7 to 8 feet for 24 hours, but pump controllers are generally not waterproof. Check your own device's rating before the trip and keep spares dry.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Heat, alcohol, and activity affect everybody's diabetes differently, and procedures change. Confirm your personal plan with your care team before you travel. Insulin dosing and device decisions are personal: work with your care team on your plan.
