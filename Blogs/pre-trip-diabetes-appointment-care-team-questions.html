The Pre-Trip Diabetes Appointment: What to Ask Your Care Team Before You Travel (2026)

Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

Every diabetes packing list starts with supplies. Almost none of them start with the appointment that produces the supplies, the paperwork, and the plans. That appointment is the one this guide is about, and the calendar is the reason to write it now: Thanksgiving is about four weeks away and Christmas is about eight, which puts holiday travelers squarely inside the window that diabetes travel guidance names for this visit.

I have had type 1 diabetes (T1D) for 20 years and I fly with an Omnipod 5 and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. This guide is the appointment itself: when to book it, what to bring to it, the ten questions worth asking, and what to leave with. The guides it points to cover each answer in depth. The travel letter guide covers the letter, the time zone guide covers the timing plan, the sick-day playbook covers illness on a trip, and the travel insurance guide covers what to declare. This post links to those guides instead of repeating them.

Talk to your care team before any trip decision that touches your medicine. This guide explains what to ask at the appointment, not what your answers should be. Any change to your insulin type, schedule, or supplies belongs in a conversation with your care team, not in a blog post. This guide is general information, not medical advice, and clinic policies and travel rules change, so confirm the current details with your own care team before you travel.

1. When to book: four to six weeks out, and why that number is real

Two independent sources land on the same window. The Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine guidance for clinicians says to advise patients to schedule an appointment 4 to 6 weeks before their trip, and at that appointment to give the patient a healthcare provider travel letter and prescriptions to hand-carry en route. The NDSS travel booklet, published by Australia's national diabetes service, says the same thing in plainer language: see your diabetes doctor or educator four to six weeks before you travel.

The CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) traveler guidance sets the floor underneath that window: make an appointment with your healthcare provider or a travel health specialist at least one month before you leave, so they can help you get destination-specific vaccines, medicines, and information, and so that discussing your itinerary and planned activities lets them give more specific advice. The CDC destination pages repeat the same floor: visit your doctor at least a month before your trip to get vaccines or medicines you may need, and make sure you are up to date on all routine vaccines before every trip.

The reason the window is weeks, not days, is that some of what the appointment produces takes time. A vaccine series is the clearest example: the NDSS booklet says to allow at least 3 months before travel for vaccines or special medicine, because sometimes a series of injections is required. A letter and a prescription refill can be faster, but they still pass through a clinic's queue, and a plan that needs a second conversation, for example a time zone plan for a complicated route, needs a second slot. Booking four to six weeks out is not caution for its own sake. It is the time the paperwork actually takes.

That math is what makes late October the moment for holiday trips. A Thanksgiving flight is about four weeks out, which means the appointment window is open right now and closing. A Christmas flight is about eight weeks out, which means the window opens in a few weeks, and a vaccine series that needs three months is already late for Christmas, so ask the clinic what is still possible rather than assuming the series fits. If your trip is inside the four week window already, section 6 covers what to prioritize.

2. What to bring: the itinerary does half the work

The CDC's advice on this point is simple and worth following literally: discussing your itinerary and planned activities with your provider lets them give more specific advice. A care team cannot write a useful time zone plan for "somewhere in Europe in December." They can write one for a flight that leaves Tuesday evening, lands Wednesday morning, and crosses six time zones into a week of walking tours. Bring the specifics:

  • Flight details. Departure and arrival times, flight length, number of time zones crossed, and arrival local time. The time zone plan depends on direction and size of the shift.
  • The activity list. Long walking days, skiing, swimming, high altitude, big celebratory meals, alcohol at family gatherings. Activity and food change what the plan needs to cover.
  • Your device list. Pump model, CGM (continuous glucose monitor) model, meter, and glucagon product, with the settings that matter: pump settings printed out, not just stored in the pump. The clinician guidance says to compile a separate document detailing pump settings, including basal rates (the background insulin the pump drips all day), insulin sensitivity, and active insulin time, so that a failed pump can be replaced by injections.
  • Your medication list, with generic names. The generic name, also called the International Nonproprietary Name (INN), is the standardized name pharmacists worldwide recognize even when the brand on the box means nothing to them. Bring the list in generic names, and ask for the prescriptions the same way.
  • Your question list. The ten questions in section 3, printed or on your phone, so the appointment covers them instead of ending with the three you remembered.
  • Your recent lab work, if you have it. Ask whether anything is out of date before the trip. A1C, the lab test that reflects average blood sugar over about three months, is the usual check, and the appointment is the moment to ask whether yours is current, not a number to interpret yourself.

3. The ten questions worth asking

These are the questions that turn a routine checkup into a travel appointment. Each one ends with a document or a decision you can take home, not just a conversation.

Question 1: Can I have a travel letter, and what will it say?

Ask for a typed letter, and ask what it will contain. The clinician guidance says the letter should state that you have diabetes and list all the supplies you need, plus the specific medications and the devices that deliver them, and any food or medication allergies. The NDSS booklet spells out the contents further: full name, address, date of birth, a statement that you have type 1 diabetes, the medications, insulin delivery devices, and blood glucose measurement equipment you use, and a statement that you must be allowed to carry these items with you at all times. Ask the clinic about its lead time for producing the letter, and make several copies once you have it: the NDSS booklet says to keep the original safe and hand out photocopies, and to give a copy of all documentation to someone at home so extras can be sent to you if needed. The travel letter guide covers the full one-page format, including the honest note that US airport security does not require a letter at all, which is a different question from whether a foreign checkpoint or customs hall will want one.

Question 2: Can I have extra prescriptions, written in generic names?

Ask for extra prescriptions for every current medication, including glucagon, and ask that they be written with generic names. The clinician guidance is explicit on both points: prescriptions should be written with generic names to minimize confusion for travelers internationally, and they should provide enough medication to last throughout the trip. The NDSS booklet adds the glucagon line directly: get extra prescriptions for any of your current medications, including glucagon, because it can be hard to access the required medication in some countries. The same guidance warns that rules for filling prescriptions vary between states and countries, and that insulin strength may vary too, so a prescription filled abroad may need matching syringes. That is a question to ask at the appointment, not a surprise to meet at a foreign pharmacy. The how-much-insulin guide covers the quantity math: calculate what the trip needs, then add 20 percent, and pack that.

Question 3: What is my time zone plan, in writing?

Bring the flight details and ask for a written timing plan for the travel day and the first days after arrival. The clinician guidance frames the question correctly: if you cross multiple time zones, you will likely need to adjust your medication and food schedules, and the plan should be made before departure, not improvised at 35,000 feet. Ask specifically when to switch the pump clock to local time, how meal insulin timing works on a long travel day, and what to do about the gap between long-acting doses when the day gets shorter flying east or longer flying west. The time zone guide covers the timing framework: east shortens the day, west stretches it, meal insulin follows meals, and the adjustment differs by direction. Dose amounts are not part of this guide or that one. They are the care team's decision, and the written plan is how you carry that decision with you.

Question 4: What is my sick-day plan for this trip?

Ask for a written sick-day plan before you leave, not a general reassurance. The questions that matter: when to test for ketones, what to do if you cannot keep fluids down, and when a hotel room problem becomes an urgent care problem. The clinician guidance adds a practical item for this conversation: depending on the region, prescribing antibiotics or antidiarrheal medications may be necessary, and it recommends that patients with type 1 diabetes obtain a supply of them because they can become sick quickly. Whether that applies to your destination is a care-team call, and the appointment is where it gets made. The sick-day playbook covers the testing rules and the red flags in full, including the rule that insulin is never stopped during illness, even when you are not eating.

Question 5: What is my backup plan if the pump fails?

Every pump patient should leave the appointment with a backup plan, and the clinician guidance says so directly: patients with an insulin pump should have an alternate insulin method, such as pens or vials, plus basal insulin in case the pump fails, and should record all pump settings in case they need to convert to injections during travel. Ask for the backup prescription, ask what injection supplies to pack, and ask whether a loaner pump is an option for the trip: the NDSS booklet notes that most pump companies offer a loan pump for overseas travel, so ask your manufacturer rather than assuming one is available. Then check the device math against your trip dates. A Dexcom G7 sensor is indicated for up to 10 days with a 12-hour grace period at the end, and the G7 15 Day sensor for up to 15 days with the same grace period, so a sensor started the day before a two week trip will not finish the trip. An Omnipod 5 Pod delivers insulin for up to 3 days, or 72 hours, so count pod changes against the trip length and pack the 20 percent buffer on top. The lost or stolen recovery playbook covers the call order if the backup plan itself needs replacing mid-trip.

Question 6: Are my vaccines and destination medicines current?

Ask the clinic to check your routine vaccines and the destination-specific list together. The clinician guidance says to confirm that immunizations are up to date and to check the CDC travel pages for the vaccines needed for the region of travel, and notes that many special immunizations can be acquired only from a public health department, not from a clinician's office, which is one more reason the appointment comes weeks before departure. The CDC destination pages give the same instruction per destination: check the vaccines and medicines list and visit your doctor at least a month before your trip. This guide names no vaccine for any destination on purpose: the right list depends on where you are going, and the CDC page for your destination is the current source. Ask the clinic to print that page's list for your trip and to note anything that needs a public health department visit.

Question 7: Does my coverage change when I leave home?

Ask whether your health coverage changes once you leave your home state or country. The clinician guidance says to determine this before departure, because some insurers require patients to use a specific healthcare system abroad, and some regulate how long a patient can be in a hospital before being transferred home. The CDC traveler guidance makes the same point from the cost side: find out if your health insurance covers medical care abroad, because travelers are usually responsible for paying hospital and other medical expenses out of pocket at most destinations, and consider buying additional insurance covering health care and emergency evacuation. Bring the policy questions to the appointment and the appointment answers to the policy: the travel insurance guide covers the pre-existing condition waiver, the 14 to 21 day buying window, and the checklist to run before you pay.

Question 8: What is my emergency plan if something goes wrong abroad?

The NDSS booklet lists the pieces, and the appointment is where they become a plan: develop an emergency plan for diabetes complications while travelling, including a list of all emergency contacts (your doctor, diabetes nurse educator, general practitioner, and family members), and print off a list of hospitals or contact the embassy for the area you are visiting. Two more steps belong on the same list. First, enroll in the US State Department Smart Traveler Enrollment Program, known as STEP, before departure: the State Department says subscribers get updates and alerts from the US embassy or consulate for their destination by email, including security alerts, health alerts, weather or natural disaster alerts, and Travel Advisory updates, and that enrollment helps the embassy or consulate contact you or your emergency contact in an emergency. Second, save the local emergency number for your destination and your care team's on-call number offline, on paper as well as in your phone, so a dead battery or no signal does not take the plan with it.

Question 9: Should I wear medical ID on this trip?

Ask, and expect a yes for most trips. The clinician guidance says to encourage patients with diabetes, especially those who use insulin, to obtain a medical identification bracelet, necklace, or similar item stating insulin use and disclosing any allergies, so that emergency medical personnel know the condition when providing care. The NDSS checklist carries the same item: a medical alert bracelet with the international medical symbol, specifying type 1 diabetes. For travel, the useful version adds two lines the guidance does not spell out but the trip requires: where the glucagon is, and one emergency contact name and number. A bracelet speaks when you cannot, and a companion who knows where the glucagon is turns that bracelet into action. The glucagon guide covers the companion briefing: show the actual device, walk through the printed instructions, and agree on where it will live before wheels-up.

Question 10: What should I stop worrying about?

Ask this one last, and mean it. Every traveler carries one worry that the appointment can retire on the spot. Common candidates: whether a letter is needed for a domestic trip (US airport security does not require one, per the travel letter guide), whether a sensor change mid-trip is a problem (it is a calendar question, answered by the 10-day or 15-day wear window plus the grace period), or whether a pump clock question has a single right answer (it has a written plan, which you now carry). The point of the question is not reassurance in general. It is to leave the appointment with fewer open loops than you entered with, and a written answer for each one that remains.

4. What to leave with: the appointment output checklist

If the appointment worked, you leave with paper, not just advice. Run this list before you leave the clinic:

  1. The travel letter, typed, on letterhead, with your full name matching your passport, your diagnosis, every medication and device you carry, and your prescriber's signature, plus several photocopies.
  2. Extra prescriptions for every current medication, including glucagon, written in generic names, covering the trip plus the 20 percent buffer.
  3. A written time zone plan for the travel day and the first days after arrival, with the pump clock switch and the long-acting dose gap covered.
  4. A written sick-day plan: when to test for ketones, what to do if you cannot keep fluids down, and the red flags that mean urgent care.
  5. A written pump backup plan: backup pens or vials, backup injection supplies, and your pump settings printed out, not just stored in the pump.
  6. A device change calendar: sensor start dates against the 10-day or 15-day wear window plus the 12-hour grace period, and pod changes against the 72-hour pod life, so no change lands on a travel day unplanned.
  7. The vaccine and destination medicine list from the CDC page for your destination, with any public health department visit booked.
  8. The coverage answer: whether your health plan covers care at the destination, and whether travel insurance is in place with diabetes declared.
  9. The emergency plan: emergency contacts, a hospital list for the destination, the local emergency number, your care team's on-call number saved offline, and STEP enrollment confirmed.
  10. Medical ID on your person, with insulin use, allergies, glucagon location, and one emergency contact.
  11. The companion briefing booked, not just intended: a time before departure to show the glucagon device, walk through the instructions, and agree on where it lives.
  12. Sugar packets in your pocket for the travel day itself: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, because a low during a long appointment day or a delayed flight is still a low.

5. The appointment is also a packing decision

One thing the appointment changes that no packing list can: quantities. The clinician guidance says prescriptions should provide enough medication to last throughout the trip, and the NDSS booklet says to get prescriptions for all necessary medications you will need while away, because it can be hard to access the required medication in some countries. A prescription written for the home routine does not automatically cover a three week trip plus a buffer, and a refill that lands after departure does not help at all. Ask the clinic to write the trip quantity into the prescription itself, including the 20 percent buffer, and ask the pharmacy to fill it early enough that a backorder still leaves time for a second attempt. The how-much-insulin guide has the calculation, and the recovery playbook has the call order for the day the calculation meets reality.

6. If the trip is less than four weeks away

Book the appointment anyway. A late appointment still gets you the three things that matter most: the travel letter, the extra prescriptions in generic names, and the written time zone plan. Some vaccine series cannot be completed on a short timeline, so ask the clinic what is still possible and what to do instead of assuming the series fits. The rest of the checklist, the device change calendar, the emergency plan, the medical ID, and the companion glucagon briefing, can all be finished in a single week, and none of them needs a clinic visit. The four to six week window is the ideal, not a cutoff. A trip with a late appointment and a written plan beats a trip with no appointment and a good packing list.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How far ahead should I book the pre-trip diabetes appointment?

A: Four to six weeks before departure, per both the Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine clinician guidance and the NDSS travel booklet. The CDC traveler guidance sets the floor at one month: make an appointment with your healthcare provider or a travel health specialist at least one month before you leave, so there is time for destination-specific vaccines, medicines, and information. For vaccines that come as a series of injections, the NDSS booklet says to allow at least three months. If your trip is inside the four week window already, book anyway and prioritize the letter, the prescriptions, and the time zone plan.

Q: What should the diabetes travel letter say?

A: The clinician guidance says the letter should state that you have diabetes and list all the supplies you need, plus the specific medications and the devices that deliver them, and any food or medication allergies. The NDSS booklet spells out the contents further: full name, address, date of birth, a statement that you have type 1 diabetes, the medications, insulin delivery devices, and blood glucose measurement equipment you use, and a statement that you must be allowed to carry these items with you at all times. Make several copies, keep the original safe, and hand out photocopies at each border. The travel letter guide covers the full one-page format.

Q: What prescriptions should I ask for at the appointment?

A: Ask for extra prescriptions for every current medication, including glucagon, written with generic names, not just brand names. Generic names minimize confusion at a foreign pharmacy, where your brand may not exist. Pump users should also ask for backup pens and a backup injection plan, because the clinician guidance says every pump patient should have an alternate insulin method plus basal insulin in case the pump fails. Ask the clinic about its lead time for the letter and the prescriptions, and leave with enough medication to last the whole trip plus the 20 percent buffer.

Q: What time zone questions should I ask my care team?

A: Bring your flight details and ask for a written timing plan for the travel day and the first days after arrival: when to switch the pump clock to local time, how meal insulin timing works on a long travel day, and what to do about the gap between long-acting doses when the day gets shorter flying east or longer flying west. Do not guess the adjustment yourself. The time zone guide covers the timing framework in full, and dose amounts are a care-team decision, never a blog post decision.

Q: What should I ask about getting sick or needing care abroad?

A: Ask for a written sick-day plan before you leave, including when to test for ketones and what to do if you cannot keep fluids down. Ask whether your health coverage changes outside your home state or country, and consider travel insurance for international travel. Before departure, enroll in the US State Department Smart Traveler Enrollment Program, known as STEP, so the nearest embassy or consulate can send you alerts and contact you or your emergency contact in an emergency. The sick-day playbook covers the testing rules and red flags, and the travel insurance guide covers what to declare.

Q: What if my trip is less than four weeks away?

A: Book the appointment anyway. A late appointment still gets you the three things that matter most: the travel letter, the extra prescriptions in generic names, and the written time zone plan. Some vaccine series cannot be completed on a short timeline, so ask the clinic what is still possible and what to do instead. The rest of the checklist in this guide, the device change timing, the emergency plan, the medical ID, and the companion glucagon briefing, can all be finished in a single week.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your insulin type, schedule, and supplies are conversations with your care team, ideally at an appointment four to six weeks before your next trip. Vaccine recommendations, coverage rules, and travel advisories change, so confirm the current details for your destination with your care team and the CDC destination page before you travel. If you have a severe low or suspected diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA, the emergency where ketones build up because the body cannot use glucose) on a trip, get medical help and tell your care team afterward.
