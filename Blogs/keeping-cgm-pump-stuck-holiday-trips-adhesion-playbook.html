Keeping Your CGM and Pump Stuck on Holiday Trips: The Diabetes Device Adhesion Playbook (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

November 1 is the first day of Diabetes Awareness Month, and Thanksgiving is 25 days away. The holiday trips are about to start, and with them the worst week of the year for device adhesion: resort pools, long walks in winter coats, sunscreen on a beach day, sweat on a crowded travel day, and dry skin once the heat comes on. A sensor or a pod that peels off at home is an annoyance. A sensor or a pod that peels off on day two of a holiday trip is a lost device, a gap in your glucose data or your insulin delivery, and a scramble for spares you may not have packed.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes (T1D) for 20 years and I fly with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I take no diabetes pills, so nothing here is about pills. The adhesion rules below are not my own inventions: they come from Dexcom's and Insulet's own published guidance, read for this guide, and they are written for the exact pair of devices I wear. If you wear different devices, the same principles apply, but your own manufacturer's guide outranks this one for your device.

Talk to your care team before any trip decision that touches your insulin delivery or your glucose monitoring. This guide covers keeping devices attached, what to do when one comes off, and what to pack. It does not change any insulin dose, any schedule, or any treatment decision. If a device comes off and you are unsure what to do next, use a blood glucose meter for treatment decisions and contact your care team or the device maker. This guide is general information, not medical advice, and manufacturer guidance changes, so confirm the current details for your own devices before you travel.

1. Why trips are harder on adhesive than home

Adhesive sticks to clean, dry, oil-free skin. That is the whole rule, and a trip attacks it from every direction. At home, a sensor or pod goes on after a shower, on skin you know, and then mostly stays dry. On a trip, the same device meets a pool on day one, sunscreen reapplied three times, a bag strap rubbing the same arm for hours, a long walk that ends in sweat, and then a hotel room with dry heated air that dries the skin around the edges. Each one alone is survivable. Stacked across a holiday week, they are how a device that normally lasts its full session peels on day three.

The fix is not a secret product. It is timing and preparation, and both happen before the trip, not after the peel starts. The two sections below give the manufacturer rules for each device, then section 4 covers the water days, section 5 covers the moment a device actually comes off, and section 6 is the packing list that makes all of it survivable.

2. The Dexcom G7 sensor: the site prep that decides the whole session

A Dexcom G7 sensor session lasts up to 10 days, plus a 12-hour grace period at the end, and during the grace period the sensor keeps working as it did during the session. That is a long time for one adhesive patch, and Dexcom's adhesion guidance is clear that the first hour decides much of it. The sensor uses a medical-grade, pressure-sensitive, acrylic-based adhesive, designed to hold for the full wear period, but Dexcom is equally clear that bumps, catches on shirts and towels, and active days can loosen it early.

Site prep, from Dexcom's own tips page:

  • Flat, clean, completely dry, with some fat under the skin. Avoid spots where your skin folds or creases, because a crease lifts an edge every time you move.
  • No lotions or oils on insertion day. The site must be clean, dry, and free of lotions or oils before you insert the sensor. Dexcom's line is blunt: moisture is the enemy of good adhesion. Moisturize between sessions to keep skin healthy, but skip it on insertion day.
  • Trim hair with electric clippers, not a razor. Less hair means a better stick, and clippers avoid the irritation a razor can cause.
  • Start fresh. Remove adhesive residue from previous sensors before you apply a new one. Dexcom names skin-safe adhesive removers such as Uni-solve, Detachol, or Tac Away for this step.
  • Rotate your sites. Do not reuse the same spot back to back. Give your skin a break and switch it up each time.

Once the sensor is on, the first 12 hours are what Dexcom calls prime time: the longer you keep the sensor dry and sweat-free early on, the better it sticks. That has a direct trip implication. If you can, put a fresh sensor on the day before travel, not the morning of a flight or the afternoon you arrive at a resort pool. A sensor applied the night before a trip gets its prime 12 hours at home, dry, instead of spending them in airport sweat or pool water.

The overpatch is the second half of the system. It is the extra adhesive patch in the G7 box that goes over the sensor, and Dexcom's guidance says to apply it smoothly and evenly, because wrinkles weaken the hold, and to ask a friend for help or use a mirror if the site is hard to reach. If a trip is coming, order extra overpatches before you leave: Dexcom says G7 and G7 15 Day users can order overpatches free of charge through a My Dexcom Account. Pack them with the sensors, in the bag that stays with you, not in a suitcase that might arrive a day late.

When an edge starts to peel, Dexcom's fix is small and practical: trim the loose bits and secure the patch with medical tape. If the sensor gets wet, pat it dry gently. Do not rub it, and do not use heat to dry it off. Both of those sound harmless and both shorten the wear.

3. The Omnipod 5 pod: the same skin rules, plus the sunscreen trap

Insulet's pod placement guide gives the same foundation as Dexcom's, with pod-specific details that matter on a trip. Clean a spot the size of a tennis ball with an alcohol swab or an oil-free antibacterial soap, and let it air dry completely before applying the pod. The skin must be completely dry before you apply, especially after swimming or bathing. Place the pod so it lays flat, not on a curved area of the body, and avoid sites where it may dislodge easily, such as near folds of skin or directly under a waistband. Avoid irritated or swollen skin, open wounds, scabs, or cuts, and rotate sites rather than overusing one spot.

Insulet's guide has a table of the four skin problems that break adhesion, and each one maps to a trip moment:

  • Oily skin. Residue from soap, lotion, shampoo, or conditioner can keep the pod from sticking. Clean the site thoroughly with alcohol before applying, and be sure to let the skin air dry.
  • Damp skin. Dampness gets in the way of adhesion. Towel off and allow the site to air dry thoroughly. Do not blow on it.
  • Body hair. Hair gets between the skin and the pod and can keep it from sticking securely. Clip or shave the site for a smooth surface, and consider doing this 24 hours before applying the pod to prevent irritation.
  • Sweating. Sweat on the skin can prevent the pod from sticking securely. Insulet's tip is to apply unscented solid or spray antiperspirant in the insertion area, then wait 10 to 15 minutes before applying the pod.

When the pod is on, Insulet says to push it down firmly with the palm of your hand to secure the whole adhesive area, then run a finger around the adhesive 2 to 3 times to make sure it is fully secured, taking care not to wrinkle the adhesive pad during application. The sunscreen trap is the one most travelers miss: Insulet says to avoid spray sunscreen, body oils, lotions, personal care sprays, and bug sprays on or near the pod, because they can loosen the adhesive, irritate the infusion site, and damage or crack the pod's plastic, which could let water into the pod. Sunscreen goes on the skin around the device, never over the adhesive and never sprayed in its direction. The same rule covers the sensor: lotion and sunscreen belong around the device, not on it.

Two more Insulet trip rules. First, if possible, avoid getting the pod wet for at least 2 hours after applying it, so do not take a bath, shower, or swim with a brand new pod. Second, try to be cool and dry, not perspiring, when you change the pod. Both point to the same trip habit as the sensor rule: change devices in the cool, dry part of the day, ideally the evening before a travel day or a water day, not poolside in the heat.

4. Water days: what each device is actually rated for

Holiday trips mean water, and both devices are made for it, within limits that are worth knowing exactly rather than guessing.

  • Dexcom G7. Dexcom says the G7 and G7 15 Day sensors are waterproof and may be submerged under eight feet of water for up to 24 hours without failure when properly installed. That covers pools, showers, baths, and ocean swimming. It does not mean the readings keep flowing underwater: your display device may need to be closer than usual to pick up readings, and you may not get readings until you get out of the water.
  • Omnipod 5. Insulet says the pod is rated for submersion up to 7.6 meters (25 feet) for 60 minutes, and says to check the pod adhesive regularly, especially when swimming. The controller is not waterproof, so it stays dry while you swim.

The practical water-day routine follows from those limits and from the adhesion rules. Pat devices dry gently after swimming, do not rub them. Check the pod adhesive after every swim, and check the sensor edges too. If an edge has lifted, trim the loose bit and tape it down before the next swim rather than hoping it survives another round. And keep the fresh-device rule: no brand new sensor or pod goes straight into the pool. The first hours after application are the bond-setting hours, and water is the worst thing for a bond that has not set.

5. When a device comes off mid-trip: the order of operations

Even with perfect prep, devices come off on trips. Luggage straps catch them, towels catch them, and a long hot day does the rest. What matters is having decided the order of operations before it happens, because a device failure at a resort pool is a bad moment to improvise.

  1. Sensor off: it is done, replace it. Dexcom's guidance is absolute here: if a sensor comes off, it must be replaced by a new one. It cannot be stuck back on, reinserted, or restarted. End the session and start a fresh sensor.
  2. Pod off: insulin delivery has stopped, replace it. A pod that is off your skin is not delivering insulin. Put on a fresh pod and follow your backup plan for the gap. The pod failure backup plan covers the full version of this moment, including the packing math that keeps it survivable.
  3. Use a meter while you are without CGM readings. Dexcom's safety guidance says that if CGM readings do not match your symptoms, use a blood glucose meter for treatment decisions. The same rule covers a missing sensor: no readings means meter readings until the new sensor is running.
  4. Treat a low with fast sugar you can reach. My own low treatment is sugar packets, and sugar packets travel well: light, sealed, and easy to keep in a pocket rather than in the bag that went missing with the device. The low snacks ranking compares the travel options.
  5. Contact the device maker about a replacement. Dexcom support and Insulet support both handle failed-device reports, and replacement policies vary, so ask rather than assuming a replacement is automatic. Keep the failed device and note when it came off, because support will ask.

One timing trick softens the whole problem. A G7 session lasts up to 10 days plus a 12-hour grace period, so a sensor change that lands mid-trip can often be moved: if the session would end on a beach day, end it the evening before instead, when you are cool, dry, and indoors. The G7 airport security guide covers the related question of how many sensors to pack, and the Omnipod 5 airport guide covers the pod count the same way. Both guides use the same packing rule this site runs on every trip: count what the trip needs, then add 20% extra supplies.

6. The adhesion packing kit and the pre-trip checklist

Everything in this guide fits in a small kit that rides in the bag that stays with you:

  • Spare sensors and pods, counted for the trip plus the 20% extra buffer.
  • Overpatches for every sensor, plus a few extras, ordered free through a My Dexcom Account before departure.
  • Medical tape for the peeling-edge rescue.
  • Alcohol swabs for site prep, and a skin-safe adhesive remover for the fresh start between devices.
  • Unscented solid or spray antiperspirant for the pod site on sweaty days, applied 10 to 15 minutes before the pod goes on.
  • A blood glucose meter and strips, for the gap while a new sensor starts up.
  • Sugar packets in a pocket, not only in the bag, for a low at the moment a device fails.

Run this list before the trip, not at the airport:

  1. Order extra G7 overpatches through a My Dexcom Account, and confirm you have an overpatch for every sensor you are packing.
  2. Time your device changes so no fresh sensor or pod goes straight into a pool, a shower, or a sweaty travel day. The evening before travel is the sweet spot.
  3. Pack the adhesion kit in the bag that stays with you, and split spares with a travel companion if the trip allows it.
  4. Check the foreign and domestic rules that touch the rest of the trip: the pre-trip appointment guide covers the care-team conversation, and the G7 temperature limits guide covers the heat and cold numbers that sit next to adhesion on a winter trip.
  5. On water days, check the pod adhesive and the sensor edges after every swim, pat dry gently, and tape a lifting edge before the next swim.
  6. Keep sunscreen, lotion, and bug spray off the adhesive and off the device, and spray them away from the site, never toward it.
  7. If a device comes off, replace it, use a meter for treatment decisions until readings return, and contact the device maker about a replacement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Why do a CGM sensor and a pump pod peel off more on holiday trips?

A: Trips stack every adhesion enemy at once: sweat, pool and ocean water, sunscreen and body lotion near the adhesive, friction from new clothes and bag straps, and dry winter skin. Dexcom and Insulet both say the same thing in different words: adhesive sticks to clean, dry, oil-free skin, and anything that puts moisture, oil, or friction between the adhesive and the skin shortens the wear.

Q: What is the Dexcom G7 overpatch, and do I need it on a trip?

A: The overpatch is the extra adhesive patch that comes in the G7 box and goes over the sensor to hold it in place for the full session. Dexcom's adhesion guidance says to apply it smoothly and evenly, without wrinkles, and says G7 and G7 15 Day users can order extra overpatches free of charge through a My Dexcom Account. On a trip, pack the overpatches with the sensors, not in a separate bag.

Q: Can I swim with a Dexcom G7 and an Omnipod 5 on?

A: Yes, both are made for water, with limits. Dexcom says the G7 and G7 15 Day sensors are waterproof and may be submerged under eight feet of water for up to 24 hours without failure when properly installed. Insulet says the pod is rated for up to 7.6 meters (25 feet) for 60 minutes, and says to check the pod adhesive regularly, especially when swimming. The controller is not waterproof, so it stays out of the water.

Q: What should I do if a sensor or pod comes off during a trip?

A: A sensor that comes off must be replaced with a new one; it cannot be reinserted or reused. A pod that comes off stops delivering insulin, so replace it and follow your backup plan. While you are without CGM readings, use a blood glucose meter for treatment decisions, and check with Dexcom or Insulet support about a replacement. This is why the packing rule matters: spares plus the 20% extra buffer, in the bag that stays with you.

Q: What should I avoid putting near a sensor or pod on a trip?

A: Lotions, oils, and sunscreen on the adhesive itself. Dexcom says the sensor site must be clean, dry, and free of lotions or oils before insertion, and to skip moisturizer on insertion day. Insulet says to avoid spray sunscreen, body oils, lotions, personal care sprays, and bug sprays on or near the pod, because they can loosen the adhesive and can irritate the site or damage the pod's plastic. Sunscreen goes on the skin around the device, never over the adhesive.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your device setup, your backup plan, and your treatment decisions are conversations with your care team, ideally at an appointment four to six weeks before your next trip. Manufacturer guidance, device ratings, and support policies change, so confirm the current details for your own devices before you travel. If a device comes off and you are unsure what to do, use a blood glucose meter for treatment decisions and get medical help promptly if you have symptoms you cannot explain.
