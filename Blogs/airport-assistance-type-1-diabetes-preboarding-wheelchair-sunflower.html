Airport Travel

Airport Assistance with Type 1 Diabetes: Preboarding, Wheelchairs, and the Sunflower Lanyard (2026)

Updated October 2026 · 9 min read

Picture the holiday airport: a terminal walk that takes twenty minutes on a quiet Tuesday, a connection that leaves from the far end of another terminal, and a boarding crowd that fills the aisle before you have stowed anything. For most travelers that is an annoyance. For a traveler with type 1 diabetes (T1D), it is also a diabetes problem: the medical bag that must stay with you, the low that can arrive mid-walk, and the seat you need to reach while the aisle is already full.

This guide is about the help airports and airlines already owe you, and how to ask for it. It covers the US rules (preboarding and terminal help under the Air Carrier Access Act), the European rules (free assistance under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2006, with its 48 hour notification rule), and the sunflower lanyard for hidden disabilities. Every rule and number below was verified against the current US Department of Transportation (DOT) Bill of Rights, the federal regulation text, the European Commission interpretative guidelines, or an airport program page in October 2026. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an insulin pump and a continuous glucose monitor (CGM), so the lived-experience lines in here are mine and the legal lines are sourced.

The rule that organizes everything else. Assistance is yours to ask for and yours to shape. An airline cannot force you into a form of help you did not request, and asking for help with the airport is not asking for help with your diabetes. Medical decisions, dosing decisions, and device settings belong in a conversation with your care team before the trip, not with gate staff. This guide is general information, not medical advice, and nothing here replaces your endocrinologist or diabetes educator.

Assistance is not only a wheelchair

When travelers hear airport assistance, they picture a wheelchair. That is one form of it, and for many people it is exactly the right form. But the rules cover a much wider menu, and the menu is the point for diabetes travel:

  • An escort through the terminal. Someone who walks you from check-in to the gate, between gates on a connection, or from the arriving flight to baggage claim and the curb.
  • Help with bags. Help carrying the bags you cannot carry, including a medical bag, when the reason is your disability.
  • Extra time to board. Boarding before the crowd so you can stow medical equipment and be seated without an aisle queue behind you.
  • Help on the aircraft. Help moving to and from your seat, reaching the lavatory with the on-board chair, and stowing and retrieving carry-on items.
  • A discreet signal. The sunflower lanyard, which tells trained staff you may need more time or patience, without a conversation about your diagnosis.

You do not need to use all of it. Ask for the piece that solves your actual problem: a long terminal walk, a tight connection, a crowded boarding scrum, or a medical bag that must not leave your side. Staff are trained to provide the help requested, not to upgrade you to a wheelchair you did not ask for.

The US rules in plain language

US air travel for passengers with disabilities runs under the Air Carrier Access Act (ACAA) and its regulation, 14 CFR Part 382. The DOT publishes a plain-language Bill of Rights summarizing it, and the Bill of Rights states its own scope clearly: it applies to individuals with a disability, defined in Part 382 as persons with a physical or mental impairment that permanently or temporarily impacts a major life activity such as walking, hearing, or breathing, and it applies to all flights of US airlines and to flights to or from the United States by foreign airlines.

Three US principles carry the whole guide:

  • No discrimination, and no charge for required help. An airline may not refuse transportation or services because of a disability, and under 14 CFR 382.57 a carrier shall not impose charges for providing facilities, equipment, or services that Part 382 requires for qualified individuals with a disability. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) states the diabetes point directly: under the Air Carrier Access Act, airlines may not discriminate against people with physical impairments, which includes diabetes.
  • Help on request, by trained staff, promptly. Terminal and boarding help must be provided promptly and in a timely way on request, by properly trained airline personnel. If you requested help in advance, you still need to identify yourself to airline personnel once you arrive at the airport or the gate to receive it.
  • Your choice of help. The airline cannot require you to accept a specific form of assistance you did not request, such as requiring a wheelchair when you asked for a sight guide. If you are in a wheelchair or another device in which you are not independently mobile, the airline cannot leave you unattended for more than 30 minutes.

One more US rule matters for diabetes travelers specifically: assistive devices, which include medical devices and a personal amount of medication, travel as carry-ons in the cabin free of charge under the safety rules, and they must not count against your carry-on limit. The separate medical bag version of that rule, and what qualifies as medical only, is in the medical baggage guide.

Preboarding: the sentence that gets you on first

Preboarding is the single most useful US right for a diabetes traveler who walks fine but needs a calm boarding. The federal rule, 14 CFR 382.93, is one sentence long: a carrier must offer preboarding to passengers with a disability who self-identify at the gate as needing additional time or assistance to board, stow accessibility equipment, or be seated.

Say exactly that at the gate desk, before boarding starts:

"I need additional time to board, stow my medical equipment, and be seated."

That self-identification at the gate is the trigger. The DOT Bill of Rights states the result: the airline must allow you the opportunity to board before all other passengers. The one approved exception is an airline with an open seating policy that DOT has approved to board extra-time passengers after an initial group has boarded, but still early in the boarding process. Either way, you board with time and space, not in the scrum.

Once on board, the help continues on request: assistance moving to or from your seat, moving to or from the lavatory including using an on-board chair if requested, and stowing and retrieving carry-on items including assistive devices. Know the boundary too, so you plan around it: under 14 CFR 382.113, carriers are not required to provide extensive special assistance on board, defined as assistance in actual eating, assistance within the restroom or at your seat with elimination functions, and provision of medical services. Crew can help you get settled and get to the lavatory door; they are not your medical team. And crew must never hand-carry a passenger on or off an aircraft except in an emergency.

Terminal help in the US: curb, connections, baggage claim

Preboarding solves the last ten minutes. Terminal help solves the hour before it. Under the DOT Bill of Rights, the help on request includes the services of personnel and the use of ground wheelchairs, accessible motorized carts, boarding wheelchairs, on-board wheelchairs, and ramps or mechanical lifts as needed, plus:

  • Assistance moving from the curb to the departing flight.
  • Assistance with transportation between gates to make connections.
  • Assistance moving from the arriving flight to the curb for pick-up.
  • Assistance accessing key functional areas of the terminal such as the ticket counter or baggage claim, or to a restroom entrance if time allows.
  • Help transporting gate-checked or carry-on luggage for a passenger who is unable to carry the luggage because of a disability. The airline may ask for credible verbal assurance that you cannot carry it, and only if you cannot provide that assurance may it ask for documentation as a condition of this service.

For a diabetes traveler, the connection case is the one to request explicitly. A tight connection across a holiday terminal is where a low finds you far from your bag and where a gate change strands you. When you book, add the connection help to the same request as the boarding help, and reconfirm it at check-in and again at the gate. The long layovers playbook covers the rest of the delay day: meals, power, insulin temperature, and rebooking without losing supplies.

Seating is a separate request, worth making at booking rather than at the gate. The DOT Bill of Rights lists specific seat cases (movable aisle armrest, bulkhead for a service animal, greater leg room for a fused or immobilized leg, adjoining seat for a companion providing assistance). For passengers not in those listed cases, the rule is still helpful: the airline must provide a seat assignment that best accommodates the disability if the passenger meets the airline procedures. Ask under those procedures, name the need (an aisle seat near the lavatory, room to stow a medical bag), and accept that the airline works within the same class of service you bought.

How to ask in the US

There are four moments to ask, and using more than one is the reliable method:

  1. When you book. Add special assistance to the booking. Describe the help in plain words: escort between gates, help with a medical bag, preboarding for extra time to stow equipment.
  2. After booking, in Manage Trips. Most airlines let you add or change the request on the reservation before travel day. Do it as early as you can so the request travels with the booking.
  3. At the ticket counter. Reconfirm the request, and ask for wheelchair or escort help from check-in onward if you need it from the start.
  4. At the gate. Self-identify for preboarding with the sentence above, and reconfirm connection help for the next leg before you board.

Keep the request specific every time. "I need assistance" gets you the default. "I need an escort from this gate to my connecting gate, and preboarding to stow my medical equipment" gets you the help you actually need, and it gives staff something they can staff for. Assistance does not replace the security checkpoint: screening still happens, and the checkpoint rights for pumps and CGMs are in the TSA pat-down guide.

Europe: the 48 hour rule

Europe runs a different system with a different owner. Under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2006, assistance at EU airports is the responsibility of the airport managing body, and assistance on board is the carrier's, and both are free of charge. The UK kept the same regulation in domestic law after leaving the EU, so the shape below applies to UK airports too, with the UK version as the legal source there.

The rule to build your trip around is notification. To be entitled to the full assistance, notify the air carrier, its agent, or the tour operator of your particular needs at least 48 hours before the published time of departure. That notification also covers a return flight if the outward and return flights were contracted with the same carrier. Then present yourself at the designated assistance point and at check-in at the time stipulated in advance, or if no time was stipulated, not later than one hour before the published departure time.

Miss the 48 hour window and you are not out of options. The European Commission interpretative guidelines state it plainly: if you do not pre-notify, or you notify less than 48 hours before departure, the airport managing body is nevertheless required by Article 7(3) of the regulation to make all reasonable efforts to provide the assistance so you can take your flight. Ask anyway, arrive early, and go to the designated point.

What the European assistance actually covers, from Annex I to the regulation, is a door to door list:

  • Communicating your arrival and request for assistance at designated points inside and outside the terminal.
  • Moving from a designated point to check-in, checking in and registering baggage.
  • Proceeding from check-in to the aircraft, completing emigration, customs, and security procedures.
  • Boarding with lifts, wheelchairs, or other assistance as needed, proceeding to your seat, and stowing and retrieving baggage on the aircraft.
  • Disembarking, proceeding to the baggage hall and retrieving baggage with immigration and customs, and proceeding to a designated point.
  • Reaching connecting flights when in transit, with assistance on the air and land sides and within and between terminals as needed, and moving to toilet facilities if required.

Two European proof rules are worth knowing because they are the opposite of what travelers expect. First, the regulation does not require you to provide evidence of your condition as a precondition for assistance, and carriers may not ask for such evidence as a precondition; a deeper enquiry is allowed only on reasonable grounds to suspect abuse, handled with care for dignity and privacy. Second, medical clearance may be requested only if your condition threatens safe transportation or the health of other passengers and crew. And on the equipment side, carriers must transport medical equipment free of charge, with no defined quantity limit in the guidelines, unlike mobility equipment which is limited to two pieces per person. The checkpoint side of European travel, including the UK card and liquid rules, is in the airport security abroad guide.

The sunflower lanyard: what it does and does not do

The Hidden Disabilities Sunflower is a green lanyard with sunflowers, worn visibly around the neck, that signals to trained staff that the wearer has a disability or condition that is not immediately apparent and may need additional support or more time. Munich Airport lists diabetes explicitly among the respiratory or chronic health conditions the program covers, and Philadelphia International Airport (PHL) lists diabetes among the invisible disabilities in its program announcement.

How it works in practice, at participating airports:

  • Free, and no proof at the airports that publish the rule. Munich Airport states the lanyard is available free of charge and without proof at its Terminal 1 Main Information Desk and Mobility Service Point, its Terminal 2 level 3 Information Desk and Mobility Service Point, and the Airport Shop at Visitors Park. PHL states guests do not have to discuss the details of their disability to participate, and the lanyard is free of charge from an airport security counter just past the security checkpoints.
  • Staff are trained to notice and ask. At PHL, employees are trained to recognize the lanyard and engage, with examples including additional time, accompanying another person to the gate, or help reading a sign. At Munich, trained staff assist with extra time, patience, or individual help.
  • It is voluntary and discreet. You choose when to wear it. Nobody at a participating airport should demand an explanation of your condition because you are wearing one.

What it does not do matters as much. Quito Airport, announcing its own program, states it plainly: the lanyard does not grant benefits such as access to priority lines, and it does not exempt anyone from mandatory controls. It helps staff offer service adapted to your needs. So do not plan a trip around the lanyard replacing a formal assistance request. Use both: the formal request books the escort or the wheelchair, and the lanyard smooths the hundred small interactions in between. And check before you travel whether your airport runs the program at all, because participation is airport by airport, not universal.

The diabetes day at the airport, with assistance booked

Assistance changes the airport day, but it does not change the diabetes day. Run both plans together:

  • Keep fast sugar on your body, not in a bag someone else carries. If an escort or a wheelchair pusher takes your bag ahead, your pocket is still with you. Sugar packets are my low treatment everywhere: one packet at 60 to 70, two below 60. The same pocket rule applies to a fingerstick meter when a sensor reading does not match how you feel.
  • Tell your assistant where the lows kit is, in one sentence. "My fast sugar is in my jacket pocket, my glucagon is in my personal item." If a low hits mid-transfer, nobody has to search. The companion briefing version of that sentence, and the three modern glucagon options, are in the glucagon guide.
  • Pre-bolus the way you do at home. I pre-bolus without a timer and it works fine; a phone reminder is an optional crutch if you are someone who wants one, never the plan. Assistance staff are not there to time your meals, and a delay day is not the day to experiment with new timing.
  • Pack 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says. Assistance does not replace the buffer. A missed connection that becomes an overnight stay is paid for out of that buffer, not out of a pharmacy run in a strange city.
  • Keep the CGM safety net independent of the airport. If you travel solo, the follower plan matters more than the escort plan: someone at home who can see your numbers and call for help if you stop responding. The solo travel playbook builds that system before the trip.

The airport assistance checklist

Here is the full routine, in trip order:

  1. Decide the exact help you need: escort, wheelchair, connection help, preboarding, or a combination. Name it in plain words.
  2. For a European flight, notify the carrier, agent, or tour operator at least 48 hours before departure. For a US flight, add special assistance when you book or in Manage Trips.
  3. Reconfirm the request at check-in and again at the gate. If you pre-requested help, identify yourself to airline personnel on arrival.
  4. At a US gate, self-identify for preboarding: "I need additional time to board, stow my medical equipment, and be seated."
  5. At a European airport, go to the designated assistance point and arrive for check-in at the stipulated time, or no later than one hour before departure if none was stipulated.
  6. If your airport runs the sunflower program, pick up a lanyard at the information desk or mobility service point. Wear it visibly. Remember it does not replace a formal request and does not grant priority lines.
  7. Sugar packets in your jacket pocket, not in a bag someone else carries: one packet at 60 to 70, two below 60. Tell your assistant where the lows kit and glucagon are.
  8. Pre-bolus the way you do at home, without a timer. Pack 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says.
  9. If assistance never shows up in the US, ask at once for the Complaint Resolution Official. In Europe, complain first to the airport managing body or carrier, then to the national enforcement body.
  10. Save the DOT Disability Hotline before a US trip: 1-800-778-4838, Monday to Friday 8:30am to 5:00pm.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do I qualify for airport assistance if I have type 1 diabetes?

A: In the United States, yes in the sense that matters: the American Diabetes Association states that diabetes is a physical impairment covered by the Air Carrier Access Act, and the US Department of Transportation defines disability in its air travel rules as a physical or mental impairment that permanently or temporarily impacts a major life activity. You qualify for the help you actually need, so describe that help, not just the diagnosis. In Europe, the regulation covers people whose mobility when using transport is reduced by a physical disability or other cause, and the European Commission guidelines say each request is judged on its merits and assistance must be proportionate to the actual need, so again describe the help you need: long terminal walks, carrying medical bags, or extra time to board.

Q: How do I ask for preboarding in the United States?

A: Go to the gate agent and self-identify as needing additional time or assistance to board, stow accessibility equipment, or be seated. That self-identification at the gate is the trigger in the federal rule, and the airline must then let you board before all other passengers, except on an airline with an approved open-seating alternative that boards extra-time passengers after an initial group but early in the process. Say it plainly: I need additional time to board, stow my medical equipment, and be seated. No advance request is needed for preboarding itself, though requesting assistance when you book still helps the airline staff the rest of the trip.

Q: Do I need a doctor note or proof of diabetes to get airport assistance?

A: For assistance itself, no. The American Diabetes Association states airlines generally may not ask for a prescription or a doctor note, with a narrow exception for a medical certificate when the disability requires extraordinary medical assistance in flight, such as oxygen or a stretcher. In Europe, the Commission guidelines state the regulation does not require evidence of the condition as a precondition for assistance, and carriers may not ask for such evidence as a precondition; a deeper enquiry is allowed only on reasonable grounds to suspect abuse, and medical clearance may be requested only if the condition threatens safe transportation or the health of others. Proof rules for security screening and for medical bags are separate questions, covered in the site guides on those topics.

Q: What if I book a European flight less than 48 hours before departure?

A: Ask anyway. The 48 hour notification to the carrier, agent, or tour operator is the condition for guaranteed assistance under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2006, but if you notify late or not at all, the airport managing body must still make all reasonable efforts to provide the assistance so you can take your flight. Your chances are best if you ask at booking, show up at the designated assistance point, and arrive for check-in at the time the carrier stipulated, or if none was stipulated, no later than one hour before the published departure time.

Q: Does a sunflower lanyard get me priority boarding or let me skip security?

A: No. The sunflower lanyard is a discreet signal to trained staff that you have a hidden disability and may need more time, patience, or individual help. Participating airports are explicit that it does not grant priority lines and does not exempt anyone from mandatory controls. It helps the conversation, it does not replace the rules. It is also not universal, so check whether your airport runs the program before you count on it: Munich Airport hands the lanyard out free of charge and without proof at its information desks and mobility service points, and Philadelphia International Airport hands it out free of charge at an airport security counter just past the security checkpoints.

Q: What do I do if the assistance I requested never shows up?

A: In the United States, ask at once for the airline Complaint Resolution Official, the airline expert in resolving disability-related issues, who must be made available in a timely manner and may be reached by phone. If the trip is already over, complain to the airline and to the US Department of Transportation: the airline must respond in writing within 30 days, and the Department refers disability complaints it receives within 6 months of the incident to the carrier for a response. The Department Disability Hotline is 1-800-778-4838, Monday to Friday 8:30am to 5:00pm. In Europe, complain first to the airport managing body or the carrier, then to the national enforcement body designated by the member state where the airport sits, or for carrier assistance, the state that issued the operating licence.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your insulin plan, device settings, and any decision about traveling with a medical condition are conversations with your care team, ideally before you book anything nonrefundable. Airline and airport assistance rules change, so confirm the current rules for your airline and airports before you travel. If assistance fails and you miss a flight or a connection, keep the names, times, and reference numbers: that record is what a complaint runs on.
