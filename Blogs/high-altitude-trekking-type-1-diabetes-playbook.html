High-Altitude Trekking with Type 1 Diabetes: The Mountain Playbook (2026)

Updated October 2026 · 9 min read

Picture the route: four days on the Inca Trail, sleeping in a tent at altitude, no heated lodge, no pharmacy, and your insulin riding in your jacket. Most people picture Machu Picchu as the high-altitude challenge of the trip. The numbers say otherwise. The citadel sits at about 7,970 feet, but Cusco, where most itineraries begin, is at about 11,200 feet, and the trail's highest point, Dead Woman's Pass, tops out at 13,828 feet. That is nearly 4,000 feet above the rated ceiling of an Omnipod pod.

This guide is for mountain trekking and backpacking with type 1 diabetes (T1D), the multi-day kind where altitude, cold, and long walking days stack on top of each other far from medical help. It covers what altitude does to blood sugar, your pump's hard ceiling, why your blood glucose meter cannot be fully trusted up high, how altitude sickness and DKA (diabetic ketoacidosis) disguise each other, and keeping insulin liquid when the tent drops below freezing. Every product and policy fact below was verified against the current manufacturer user guide, ADA (American Diabetes Association) guidance, or the CDC's travel medicine guidance in October 2026. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I fly with an insulin pump, so the lived-experience lines in here are mine and the medical lines are sourced. The skiing playbook covers the same altitude and cold problems for resort days; this is the version for the trail.

The rule that organizes everything else. A high-altitude trek with type 1 diabetes is a pre-trip appointment with your care team, not a figure-it-out-on-the-trail situation. Dosing decisions, a pump backup plan for elevations above your device's rating, and any altitude-sickness medication all belong in that appointment. This guide is general information, not medical advice, and nothing here replaces your endocrinologist or diabetes educator.

The numbers on the trail

Getting the elevation sequence right changes the whole plan. Cusco is the altitude event, not the ruins.

  • Cusco: about 11,200 feet (3,400 m). Most Peru itineraries start here. This is where the thin air hits, and it is already above the 10,000-foot ceiling in pump safety information.
  • Sacred Valley: roughly 9,160 feet (2,792 m). Lower than Cusco; a common first stop for gradual acclimatization.
  • Machu Picchu citadel: about 7,970 feet (2,430 m). Meaningfully lower than Cusco. By the time most visitors arrive, they have descended.
  • Dead Woman's Pass, classic Inca Trail high point: 13,828 feet (4,215 m). The top of the trail, and almost 4,000 feet above the altitude warning in Omnipod's safety information.

The general pattern holds on other treks too: the town or trailhead you acclimatize in can be the high-altitude problem, while the famous destination sits lower. Plan around the highest sleeping altitude, not the most photographed one.

What altitude does to blood sugar

Altitude does not push glucose in one direction. It pushes in two, at the same time, for different reasons. The lower oxygen level triggers a stress-hormone response, cortisol and adrenaline, which tends to raise blood sugar; researchers tracking trekkers with diabetes on a high Himalayan circuit found significantly higher overnight glucose once they passed about 3,600 meters. At the same time, hours of walking at altitude burn glucose the way any hard trekking day does, which pulls it down. The net effect for a given trekker on a given day is genuinely unpredictable.

Treat day one at altitude as a watch-and-learn day. Check more often than usual, watch the CGM (continuous glucose monitor) trend arrows instead of single numbers, and expect your sea-level correction habits to be unreliable up high. Hydrate aggressively: high altitude dehydrates everyone through faster breathing and dry air, and dehydration makes glucose harder to read and lows harder to feel. This is also the section where the pre-trip appointment matters most, because the insulin plan for altitude is your care team's call.

Your pump has a ceiling

This is the one with hard numbers from the manufacturers, and it is the one most trekkers never think about. Insulet's Omnipod 5 Important Safety Information is explicit: do not use the system at low atmospheric pressure (below 700 hPA). Those pressures occur at high elevations, such as when mountain climbing or living at elevations above 10,000 feet (3,000 meters). The mechanism is physical: tiny air bubbles inside the Pod expand as pressure drops, and unintended insulin delivery can occur, which can cause low blood sugar. The safety information says to check glucose frequently when flying for the same reason.

The practical answer is a backup plan you have actually rehearsed, not one you read about. Pens or syringes plus long-acting insulin, packed and ready, with a pod-change kit in the daypack. Talk through the plan with your care team before the trip, including at what elevation you switch from pump to backup, because the switch decision is not one to improvise on a pass at 13,000 feet. The pod-failure backup plan covers the rehearsal drill, and the skiing playbook rounds up altitude ratings for other pumps, including Medtronic and Tandem.

Your CGM works up high; the cold is the limit

Here is the good news first. Dexcom's G7 user guide rates the sensor, transmitter, and receiver for operating and storage altitudes from 1,253 feet below sea level to 16,406 feet. No trekking route you can book goes higher than that. Altitude, by itself, is not a G7 problem.

Cold is the real limit, and the numbers are tighter than people expect. The sensor is rated for operation between 50F and 107.6F; below that, readings can stop being trustworthy. On the trail that means the sensor lives under a layer, close to your body, not exposed on an arm under a short sleeve on a windy ridge. The receiver is rated down to 32F, and spare sensors store between 36F and 86F, never in the freezer, so the spares sleep in the sleeping bag too. Keep the phone warm for the same reason; a dead phone ends CGM sharing and your alarm net in one stroke. The full heat-and-cold breakdown is in the G7 temperature limits guide.

The meter problem at altitude

This is the section that surprises people, and it is the one backed by the most research. Most blood glucose test strips use chemistry that depends on oxygen, and thinner air throws it off. A Diabetes Care review of adventure travel and type 1 diabetes pulled together studies done at altitudes up to 5,800 meters and found meter reliability problems nearly everywhere: some meters read falsely high, others falsely low, off by up to 37% on control solutions. Cold compounds it, with meters failing outright or needing to be warmed by hand before they would give any reading at all.

A later field study on Kilimanjaro put numbers on it: out of 228 paired measurements, 21% fell outside accuracy criteria, mostly in the normal-to-low range and mostly by overestimation, with most errors happening between 3,000 and 3,900 meters. Read that range again: Cusco sits at 3,400 meters, square in the middle of the band where meters were wrong most often. In head-to-head testing, glucose-dehydrogenase (GDH) meters held up better than glucose-oxidase (GOX) ones, but no meter class was perfect. The CDC's travel medicine guidance puts it plainly: not all glucometers read accurately at high altitude.

What to do with that:

  • Carry the meter next to your skin. Body heat is the fix for both the cold problem and part of the accuracy problem; the Diabetes Care review specifically recommends carrying monitoring equipment close to the body.
  • Pack control solutions and use them. Testing the meter against its control solution is how you spot a meter that has drifted at altitude, and carrying a second meter adds confidence when two readings disagree.
  • Trust symptoms over the number when they disagree. If you feel low and the meter says you are fine, treat the low. If the CGM and the meter disagree at altitude, the meter reading that matches how you feel wins, and a second meter breaks the tie.
  • Confirm lows, not just highs. Overestimation is the dangerous direction at altitude, because a meter reading high can mask a real low.

Altitude sickness and DKA look alike

Acute mountain sickness (AMS) brings headache, nausea, and fatigue. So does diabetic ketoacidosis, the dangerous buildup of blood acids called ketones that happens when the body burns fat for fuel without enough insulin. On a trail at 11,000 feet, you cannot tell them apart by how you feel.

The CDC's guidance connects the two directly: altitude illness can trigger DKA. The rule on the trail is therefore simple. When you feel sick at altitude, check glucose and ketones, not just glucose. High ketones with high glucose is a DKA warning, not just altitude, and the answer is descending and getting medical care, not waiting it out in the tent. Pack ketone strips and keep them with the meter, and make sure your trekking partner knows where your glucagon is. If a travel day goes sideways medically, the sick-day travel playbook is the fallback plan.

One more conversation for the pre-trip appointment: altitude-sickness medication. A Diabetes Care review on diabetes and physical activity at altitude advises caution with acetazolamide (Diamox) in type 1 diabetes. It increases fluid loss and blood acidity, which can theoretically push toward DKA, and the International Climbing and Mountaineering Federation advises against its use in T1D. The alternative medication, dexamethasone, raises blood sugar. Neither is a do-it-yourself decision; both belong in the endocrinologist visit, with a plan for more frequent ketone checks if either gets approved.

Insulin sleeps in your sleeping bag

On a trek there is no heated hotel room, so your body heat becomes the storage system. Insulin freezes at 32F (0C), and frozen insulin must be discarded because it does not recover when it thaws. A tent at 12,000 feet drops below freezing on an ordinary night, so the insulin goes in the sleeping bag with you, every night, no exceptions. During the day it rides in an inside pocket close to your body, never in an outer pocket, never in a bag left outside the tent.

The manufacturer numbers still apply on the trail. Unopened insulin stays good between 36F and 46F until its expiry date. Once a pen or vial is opened, most insulins keep at room temperature, below 86F, for up to 28 days, which covers most treks without any refrigeration at all. Never try to keep insulin cold on a mountain; the enemy up there is freezing, not heat. The full storage rules, labels included, are in the hotel insulin storage guide, and the cold-weather side of the problem is in the skiing playbook.

Trail logistics: power, water, food

Power. Cold drains batteries faster, and there are no outlets on the trail. The phone and power bank sleep in the sleeping bag with the insulin; the power bank rides in the daypack during the day, never in checked-style duffels the porters carry ahead. Charge the CGM receiver or phone from the power bank each night. The full device-power routine is in the devices charging guide.

Water. Drink more than feels necessary. Thin, dry mountain air pulls moisture out with every breath, and dehydration muddies every glucose reading and makes lows harder to feel. Set a bottle target for the day and finish it.

Food. Eat real meals, protein-forward the way you eat at home, and pre-bolus for them the way you do at home. I pre-bolus without a timer and it works fine; a phone reminder is an optional crutch if you are someone who wants one, never the plan. Keep fast sugar on your person, not in the pack: sugar packets are my low treatment everywhere, and the trail is no exception, one packet at 60 to 70, two below 60. They are flat, they weigh nothing, and they do not freeze shut. A hip-belt pocket or chest pocket beats the main pack compartment, because the pack can be ten minutes behind you on a steep section.

The mountain trek checklist

Here is the full kit, in packing order:

  1. The pre-trip care-team appointment: altitude plan, pump backup decision, altitude-medication conversation, and the elevation where you switch to backup insulin.
  2. Your pump's altitude rating confirmed from its user guide; the rehearsed pen or syringe backup (plus long-acting insulin) packed and ready.
  3. 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says, with the essentials split so a lost bag cannot take everything: the how-much-insulin guide is the starting point, and the lost-or-stolen recovery playbook covers the worst case.
  4. The CGM sensor worn under a layer; spare sensors stored 36F to 86F, never frozen, sleeping in the sleeping bag.
  5. A blood glucose meter in an inside pocket, control solutions, and ideally a second meter for tie-breaking at altitude.
  6. Ketone strips with the meter, and a trekking partner who knows where your glucagon is.
  7. In-use insulin in an inside pocket by day and in the sleeping bag by night; unopened insulin kept between 36F and 46F until opened.
  8. Sugar packets on your person, not in the pack: one packet at 60 to 70, two below 60.
  9. The power plan: phone and power bank in the sleeping bag overnight, power bank in the daypack by day.
  10. Travel insurance confirmed to cover the trip you are actually taking, including medical evacuation from remote areas: the travel insurance guide covers what to check.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I wear my insulin pump above 10,000 feet?

A: It depends on your pump, and the answer is in your user guide. Insulet's Omnipod 5 safety information says not to use the system at low atmospheric pressure (below 700 hPA), which occurs at high elevations such as above 10,000 feet (3,000 meters): expanding air bubbles inside the Pod can cause unintended insulin delivery and low blood sugar. If your trek crosses that line, go in with a backup plan you have rehearsed (pens or syringes plus long-acting insulin) and talk it through with your care team before the trip, not on the mountain.

Q: Why is my blood glucose meter wrong at altitude?

A: Most test strips use chemistry that depends on oxygen, and thinner air throws it off. Studies reviewed in Diabetes Care found meters over- and under-reading by up to 37% at altitude, with glucose-dehydrogenase (GDH) meters holding up better than glucose-oxidase (GOX) ones. A field study on Kilimanjaro found 21% of readings outside accuracy criteria, with most errors between 3,000 and 3,900 meters. Keep the meter warm next to your skin, pack control solutions to spot-check it, confirm with a second meter when a reading does not match symptoms, and trust your body over the number.

Q: Will my Dexcom G7 work on a high-altitude trek?

A: The altitude itself is not the problem: Dexcom's user guide rates the G7 sensor and receiver for operating and storage altitudes from 1,253 feet below sea level up to 16,406 feet, which covers every major trekking route. The cold is the real limit. The sensor is rated for operation between 50F and 107.6F, so wear it under a layer and keep it close to your body; the receiver is rated down to 32F. Store spare sensors between 36F and 86F, never in the freezer.

Q: Can I take altitude-sickness medication with type 1 diabetes?

A: Only after a detailed conversation with your endocrinologist before the trip. A Diabetes Care review on diabetes at altitude advises caution with acetazolamide (Diamox) in type 1 diabetes: it increases fluid loss and blood acidity, which can theoretically push toward diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), and the International Climbing and Mountaineering Federation advises against it in T1D. Dexamethasone, the alternative altitude medication, raises blood sugar. If your doctor approves either one, plan on checking ketones far more often.

Q: How do I tell altitude sickness apart from DKA on the trail?

A: You cannot tell by symptoms alone: nausea, headache, and fatigue belong to both altitude sickness and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous buildup of blood acids called ketones that happens when the body burns fat for fuel without enough insulin. The CDC notes that altitude illness can trigger DKA. So the rule is simple: when you feel sick at altitude, check glucose and ketones. High ketones plus high glucose is a DKA warning, not just altitude, and it means descending and getting medical care, not waiting it out in the tent.

Q: How do I keep insulin from freezing on a multi-day trek?

A: Insulin freezes at 32F (0C), and frozen insulin must be discarded because it does not recover when it thaws. On a trek there is no heated hotel room, so your body heat is the storage system: carry in-use insulin in an inside pocket close to your body during the day, and sleep with your insulin in your sleeping bag at night. Unopened insulin stays good between 36F and 46F until expiry, and once opened most insulins keep at room temperature (below 86F) for up to 28 days.
