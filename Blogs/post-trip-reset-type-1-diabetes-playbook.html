Back Home with Type 1 Diabetes: The Post-Trip Reset Playbook (2026)

Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

The plane landed, the bags are by the door, and the trip is over. But your diabetes routine is not home yet. It is still running on trip time: a pump clock set to another city, a sensor counting down its last day, a sleep schedule five hours off, and a supply drawer emptier than you remember. The trip ends at the airport. The reset takes a few days, and it is worth doing properly, because the next trip is already coming: Thanksgiving flights are a month away, and the holiday season is the worst time to discover you never restocked.

This guide is the reset itself, in the order that works: clocks first, then sleep, then the device cycle, then the supply drawer, then the trip data. Every rule and number below was verified against the current CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention), Dexcom, Insulet, or NovoLog label source in October 2026. I have had type 1 diabetes (T1D) for 20 years and I fly with an Omnipod 5 and a Dexcom G7 (a continuous glucose monitor, or CGM), so the routine lines in here are mine and the manufacturer lines are sourced.

The rule that organizes everything else. The reset is a return to your normal routine, not a new plan. Dose amounts, pump settings, and any change to your usual schedule belong in a conversation with your care team, not in a jet lagged evening at home. This guide is general information, not medical advice, and nothing here replaces your endocrinologist or diabetes educator.

1. Put the clocks back first

Your phone fixes itself. Your diabetes devices may not, and the clock is the one thing every other part of the routine depends on. Do this on landing day, before the first home-cooked meal.

On Omnipod 5, there are two different clocks, and confusing them is the classic post-trip mistake. The device time zone is the time shown on your Controller or phone, and it updates automatically when you travel if the Automatic Time Zone setting is on. The insulin delivery time zone is the time your insulin delivery is based on, and it only changes when you change it yourself. When the two do not match, the Omnipod 5 app notifies you and asks if you want to update the insulin delivery time zone to your new local time. Accept that prompt when you get home, in Manual Mode, following your care team's guidance, and confirm the change took. The time zones guide covers the trip-direction version of this same step.

If you take multiple daily injections (MDI, insulin by pen or syringe rather than a pump), the home version is simpler: your long-acting background insulin goes back to its usual home clock time, and your meal insulin goes with your meals, whenever you eat. If the trip was long enough that your long-acting timing drifted, that is a care-team question, not a guess to make while tired.

One more clock to check: your CGM receiver, if you use one. A receiver that still thinks it is in another time zone will timestamp your readings wrong, and wrong timestamps make the trip data in section 5 harder to read.

2. Sleep: the jet lag math, and why it matters for glucose

Jet lag is not just tiredness. The CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) describes it as a temporary sleep problem caused by a mismatch between your normal daily rhythms and a new time zone. It usually occurs when you cross more than three time zones, and it affects your mood, your ability to concentrate, and your physical and mental performance. A tired brain reads glucose trends worse, forgets the pre-bolus, and reaches for the wrong snack. Sleep is a diabetes tool, not a luxury, in the first week home.

The CDC Yellow Book gives the recovery math, and it is direction dependent. Westward travel is easier to adapt to than eastward travel, with an average adaptation rate of 1.5 hours per day for westward travel and 1 hour per day for eastward travel. A 6 hour shift east takes roughly 6 days to absorb. The same shift west takes roughly 4 days. So the first thing to do is set expectations: if you flew east, you are not "bad at recovering." You are on the slower schedule, and the numbers will look messier for longer.

The CDC's own homecoming moves are simple, and they work in reverse just as well as they work on arrival:

  • Follow home time from day one. When the shift is more than 3 hours, follow the sleep and waking routines of your home time zone as soon as you arrive, even if your body disagrees.
  • Get daylight during the day. Stay in well-lit areas during the day. Morning light is the strongest reset signal you have, and it is free.
  • Eat small, regular meals. Small meals help with the stomach problems jet lag brings, and regular mealtimes give your insulin routine its anchors back.
  • Use caffeine and exercise strategically. Both can help you stay alert during the day, but avoid them in the evening, or they will steal the night's sleep too.
  • Keep naps short. If you are sleepy during the day, a nap of 15 to 20 minutes helps without wrecking the night's sleep.
  • Skip the alcohol. Alcohol disrupts sleep, and a disrupted night makes the next day's glucose harder to read.

Two diabetes-specific cautions for the jet lag week. First, do not redesign your insulin plan to chase tired-day numbers. Travel stacks stress, strange food, broken sleep, and time zone shifts, and those effects fade as the routine returns. Go back to the home routine, watch a few normal days, and bring any pattern that survives the reset to your care team. Second, keep the low routine exactly as it is at home: sugar packets on the nightstand, one packet at 60 to 70, two below 60. Jet lag nights are the nights you do not want to improvise.

3. Reset the device cycle: sensor and pod on home schedule

Trips bend device schedules. A sensor started mid-trip expires mid-week at home. A pod changed at a hotel expires on a workday morning. The reset is one deliberate decision: put both devices back on a home schedule you can live with, and stop letting trip timing dictate your weeks.

The sensor. Dexcom's numbers are exact. The G7 sensor runs up to 10 days with a 12 hour grace period at the end, and the G7 15 Day sensor runs up to 15 days with the same grace period. The grace period is extra time Dexcom gives you to replace the sensor at a convenient moment, and the sensor keeps working normally during it. Two rules matter for the reset. First, you cannot restart a sensor after its session ends, so a sensor expiring the day after landing is a planned change, not a failure. Second, always use the overpatch included with the sensor, because a sensor that stays put for its full session is one less thing to think about during the jet lag week. If your sensor expires within a day or two of landing, change it at home, calmly, and let the new session anchor your home schedule. The Dexcom G7 guide covers the packing math for the next trip.

The pod. Each Omnipod 5 Pod provides up to 3 days, or 72 hours, of continuous insulin delivery. If your pod is mid-cycle when you land, let it finish on its normal schedule, then change the next one at a home-friendly hour, morning rather than midnight, so the cycle lands on convenient days going forward. A pod change timed for the trip home is a pod change timed for nobody. The pod-failure backup plan covers the spare-pod habit that makes any change a 10 minute job instead of a crisis.

The charger and the receiver. Charge everything that ran low on the trip: the CGM receiver, the spare phone charger, the power bank. A dead receiver on the first home morning is a small problem that feels large when you are jet lagged, and it is entirely preventable the night you land.

4. Restock before you forget: the supply drawer audit

This is the step most people skip, and it is the one the holiday season punishes. The rule is simple: count what is left the week you get home, not the week before the next trip. Restock in this order: insulin first, then pods or infusion sets, then sensors, then the small things that run out quietly, overpatches, test strips, sugar packets.

Two label checks belong in the audit. First, the temperature check. Unopened NovoLog stays refrigerated between 36F and 46F until expiry, and frozen insulin must be discarded because it does not recover when it thaws. In-use vials and pens stay good below 86F for up to 28 days, and an in-use pen must not go back in the fridge. If a vial or pen spent the trip somewhere suspect, a hot car, a freezing hotel fridge, a checked bag, replace it from your spare supply rather than testing it on your body. The full storage rules are in the hotel insulin storage guide.

Second, the expiry check. Check the expiration date on every pen, vial, pod box, and sensor box you brought home, and throw out anything past its date. Trip bags accumulate half-used boxes, and a half-used box with an expired sensor is not a spare, it is clutter that looks like a spare.

Then apply the standing rule to the next trip, while the last trip is still fresh. The how-much-insulin guide sets it: calculate what the trip needs, add 20%, and pack that. If the last trip ate into the buffer, the buffer is what you are restocking. A delay that stretches a 7 day trip into 9 or 10 days, the scenario in the long-layovers playbook, is paid for out of that buffer, not out of a pharmacy run in a strange city.

Finally, unpack the diabetes bag completely. Do not leave a half-unpacked kit by the door "for next time." Next time starts with a clean, counted kit, and a bag that stayed half-packed through three trips is how a missing charger gets discovered at the airport.

5. Read the trip in your data, then let it go

Your CGM data holds the trip, and the trip holds lessons, but only if you look at it once, on purpose, instead of doomscrolling it all week. Pick one evening in the first week home, open the data, and ask three questions: where did the lows cluster, where did the highs cluster, and what was different about those hours from home hours? A low cluster around long walking days is a pattern. A high cluster around restaurant dinners is a pattern. A single bad day is not.

Dexcom Clarity is the tool for this. Clarity shows glucose patterns, trends, and statistics from your CGM data, and app users send data to Clarity automatically once sharing is authorized. If you use a receiver, upload it: the receiver holds about 30 days of data, so uploading once a month saves everything, and the Clarity Uploader software handles the transfer. To share the trip with your clinic, your clinic provides a sharing code, which you enter in the Clarity app or website. Bring the pattern, not the whole trip, to your next appointment: "lows on walking days, highs at big dinners" is a useful sentence, and it is the sentence your care team can actually act on.

One caution: do not turn the trip review into a new plan. If a pattern survives the reset week, it belongs in a care-team conversation. If it fades with sleep and routine, it was travel, and travel is over. The CGM sharing playbook covers the other half of the data story: telling your followers you are home, so a frozen number on their screen reads as "trip over" instead of "something wrong."

And if the trip ended with illness rather than just tiredness, the reset changes: the sick-day playbook covers the testing rules and red flags, and a trip that ends sick is a trip to call your care team about, not to reset alone.

The post-trip reset checklist

Here is the full reset, in order:

  1. Put the clocks back on landing day: Omnipod 5 insulin delivery time zone to home time, receiver clock checked, MDI long-acting insulin back to its usual home clock time.
  2. Follow home time from day one: daylight during the day, small regular meals, caffeine and exercise early not late, naps of 15 to 20 minutes, no alcohol while resetting.
  3. Expect the jet lag math: roughly 1 hour per day of recovery after flying east, roughly 1.5 hours per day after flying west, and a longer window after an eastbound trip.
  4. Reset the device cycle: G7 sensor up to 10 days plus a 12 hour grace period (15 Day: 15 days plus grace), no restarts, overpatch on; Omnipod Pod up to 72 hours, next change at a home-friendly hour.
  5. Keep the low routine unchanged through the jet lag week: sugar packets on the nightstand, one packet at 60 to 70, two below 60.
  6. Do not redesign your insulin plan for tired-day numbers; watch a few normal days, and bring any surviving pattern to your care team.
  7. Audit the supply drawer the week you get home: insulin first, then pods or sets, then sensors, then overpatches, strips, and sugar packets.
  8. Check temperature and expiry: unopened insulin refrigerated 36F to 46F, frozen insulin discarded, in-use pens and vials below 86F for up to 28 days, in-use pens never back in the fridge.
  9. Restock to the standing rule: calculate what the next trip needs, add 20%, and pack that; unpack the diabetes bag completely so the next trip starts from a counted kit.
  10. Read the trip once in Dexcom Clarity: three questions (low clusters, high clusters, what was different), upload receiver data, share with your clinic via its sharing code, and bring the pattern to your next appointment.
  11. Tell your followers you are home so sharing reads correctly, and if the trip ended with illness, use the sick-day playbook and call your care team.

Home again? The time zones guide is the companion piece for the next departure, and the how-much-insulin guide is the math for the next packing list. A trip ends twice: once at the airport, and once when the routine is home. Finish the second one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How long does jet lag last after flying home?

A: The CDC Yellow Book gives average adaptation rates of about 1 hour per day after flying east and about 1.5 hours per day after flying west. A 6 hour eastbound shift takes roughly 6 days to absorb, while a 6 hour westbound shift takes roughly 4 days. Jet lag is a temporary sleep problem that usually occurs when you cross more than three time zones, and it affects mood, concentration, and physical and mental performance. If sleep problems persist well past that window, talk to your care team.

Q: Do I need to change my pump time zone when I get home?

A: Yes, your insulin delivery needs to run on home time again. On Omnipod 5, the device time zone and the insulin delivery time zone are separate: the device clock updates automatically when Automatic Time Zone is on, but the insulin delivery time zone only changes when you change it, and the app notifies you when the two do not match. Change it in Manual Mode, following your care team's guidance, and confirm the change took before your next meal. The timing plan for the trip itself is in the time zones guide.

Q: My Dexcom G7 sensor expires the day after I land. What should I do?

A: Change it at home, on your normal schedule, not in a rush at the airport. The G7 sensor runs up to 10 days with a 12 hour grace period at the end, and the G7 15 Day runs up to 15 days with the same grace period. The grace period is extra time Dexcom gives you to replace the sensor at a convenient moment, and the sensor keeps working normally during it. You cannot restart a sensor after its session ends, so a sensor that expires right after landing is a planned change, not an emergency. Pack a spare sensor for the trip home so the change is ready when you are.

Q: How do I know if my insulin survived the trip?

A: Check the two things the label actually rules on: temperature and time. Unopened NovoLog stays refrigerated between 36F and 46F until expiry, and frozen insulin must be discarded because it does not recover when it thaws. In-use vials and pens stay good below 86F for up to 28 days, and an in-use pen must not go back in the fridge. If a vial or pen spent time frozen, or past its 28 day in-use window, replace it from your spare supply and talk to your care team if your numbers run unexpectedly high afterward.

Q: Should I change my insulin plan because my numbers were off on the trip?

A: No, not on your own. Travel days stack stress, strange food, broken sleep, and time zone shifts, and those effects fade as your routine returns. Go back to your home routine first, watch a few normal days of data, and bring the trip pattern to your care team if it is still there after the reset. Dose amounts and pump settings are a care-team conversation, not a post-trip adjustment you make while jet lagged.

Q: What should I restock first after a trip?

A: Count what is left before you unpack anything else, and restock in this order: insulin, then pods or infusion sets, then sensors, then the small things that run out quietly (overpatches, test strips, sugar packets). The standing rule is the same as before the trip: calculate what the next trip needs, add 20%, and pack that. Restocking the week you get home, not the week before the next trip, is what keeps the holiday season from starting with a pharmacy run.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your insulin plan after a trip, your pump settings, CGM alert settings, and any change to your usual meal timing are conversations with your care team, ideally before your next trip. Manufacturer specifications and travel health guidance change, so confirm the current numbers for your devices before you travel. If you have a severe low or suspected DKA (diabetic ketoacidosis, the emergency where ketones build up because the body cannot use glucose) on a trip, get medical help and tell your care team afterward.
