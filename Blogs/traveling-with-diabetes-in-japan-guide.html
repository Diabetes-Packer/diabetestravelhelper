Traveling with Diabetes in Japan: The Import Rules, the Pharmacy, and the Trip (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Winter is the season this guide is written for: ski trips in Hokkaido and the Japan Alps, and New Year trips to Tokyo, Kyoto, and Osaka. It is also the first country on this site to get its own destination guide, and that is not an accident. Japan is one of the few places where the diabetes rules are genuinely different from the ones you already know: a written import certificate for your insulin, a pharmacy system that will not accept your prescription from home, and a food label system that actually makes carb counting easier once you learn where to look. None of it is hard. All of it is specific. This guide covers the import rules, the pharmacy, and the trip itself, in that order, so you can sort the paperwork at home and enjoy the trip once you land.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. I have not traveled to Japan with diabetes. This guide is not a trip report, and it does not pretend to be one. It is built from the published guidance of Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW), the ministry that regulates medicine in Japan, the United States Embassy in Tokyo, Japan's Narcotics Control Department, and the Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO), the national tourism body. Where a rule comes from one of those sources, this guide says so. Where something is my own travel habit, it is labeled as mine, and it is kept separate from the official rules. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own travel plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not going, tell your care team.

1. The import rule in one paragraph, then the details

Japan controls medicines at the border under its Pharmaceutical Affairs Law, and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) publishes the traveler rules in a short question and answer document. The rules turn on two questions: what the medicine is, and how much of it you carry. For ordinary prescription medicine taken by mouth, the line is one month. Bring up to a one-month supply for your own use, and no special procedure is needed. Bring more than a one-month supply, and you need a certificate called a Yunyu Kakunin-sho, which means Import Confirmation. Older guides call it a Yakkan Shoumei, which was the previous name for the same certificate, and searching either term will find it. You apply before you leave home, you receive it before you leave home, and you show it to customs if asked when you arrive. Our customs and border rules guide covers the general version of this system for any country, and our international travel guide covers the wide version of traveling abroad with diabetes. Japan is where the general version gets specific.

Three points about the certificate matter before anything else. First, the application runs online through MHLW's Application for Import Confirmation system, which has an English option. You create a personal account, fill in the medicine and the quantity, and attach a copy of your prescription. Second, the United States Embassy in Tokyo says certificate approval may take several weeks, so do not leave the application to the week of the flight. Apply as soon as the trip is booked. Third, the certificate covers the medicine listed on it and nothing else. It is valid only when your luggage contents match what is written on it, so list every insulin, every pen, and every box of syringes you plan to carry, and do not add items after the certificate is issued. If the trip runs longer than a month, our long-stay abroad playbook covers the planning for a trip that outlasts the supply you can carry, and the certificate is the piece that makes a long Japan stay work.

2. Insulin at the border: pens, syringes, and what to carry

Insulin is an injection, and MHLW treats injections separately from oral medicine. Its question and answer document says you can bring an injection into Japan only as a pre-filled syringe or a self-injection kit used under the direction of a doctor, and only if you apply for a Yunyu Kakunin-sho and receive it before you leave home. An insulin pen is a self-injection kit. A vial with separate syringes is not a pre-filled syringe, so if you travel with vials and syringes, list them on the same application and bring the certificate too. Do not plan to bring a loose syringe on its own. Pack the syringes with the insulin they belong to, and list them on the same application, so the certificate and the bag agree with each other.

What to carry, in the bag that stays with you. Keep every insulin in its original box with the pharmacy label on it, so an officer can match the medicine to the paper without a conversation. Carry a copy of your prescription, and ask your doctor for a letter that states your name, your diagnosis, the medicines by their generic name, and that they are for your personal use. Japan does not require the letter. The United States Embassy in Tokyo says travelers should consider bringing a copy of the prescription as well as a letter stating the purpose of the drug, and that is the right way to think about it: not a legal requirement, but the document that turns a customs question into a thirty-second check. Our travel letter guide covers what the letter should say, and our pre-trip appointment guide covers the full appointment, including asking for generic-name prescriptions before you leave. A prescription that lists the generic name is the one that travels. A brand name that only exists in your home country does not.

Two habits from my own travel routine, kept separate from the border rules. Everything diabetes-related rides in my carry-on, never in checked luggage, and I pack 20 percent extra of everything: pods, sensors, insulin, test strips, lancets, ketone strips, and the low kit. Our packing list does the counting for the trip length. The 20 percent buffer covers the pod that fails on a ski slope and the sensor that peels off in a hot bath. Bring backup insulin for injections in case a pod fails mid-trip, and keep your insulin plan written down somewhere that is not the pump. The flight there follows the same airport rules as any trip, covered in our TSA rules guide for the United States departure and our airport security abroad guide for the arrival side.

3. What is banned, and what needs a separate permission

Some medicines cannot come to Japan at all, and no certificate fixes that. MHLW's question and answer document is blunt: nobody can bring any medicine containing methamphetamine or amphetamine into Japan, and being found with one can mean arrest on the spot. That includes Adderall and similar ADHD medicines. A valid prescription from home does not change it. The United States Embassy says the same thing in plainer terms: it does not matter if you have a valid United States prescription for a medicine which is illegal in Japan. If you bring it with you, you risk arrest and detention by the Japanese authorities. If you take a stimulant medicine, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about what to do instead, because the answer cannot be found at the border.

A second group needs a separate permission from a different office. Narcotics such as morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone, and codeine, and stimulant raw materials such as lisdexamfetamine, the active ingredient in Vyvanse, need advance permission from Japan's Narcotics Control Department before you travel, through a process that is separate from the Yunyu Kakunin-sho. The department asks you to apply at least 14 days before travel, and its medical certificate must list your name, your address, the specific reason you take the medicine, the medicine list with doses and strength, the doctor's signature, and a date of issue within three months. You must carry the medicine yourself: you cannot mail it to Japan, and you cannot ask someone else to carry it for you. Show the import certificate to a customs officer on arrival. Psychotropics such as methylphenidate and common sleep and anxiety medicines need no permission up to the amount in the department's table, and permission above it. Heroin, opium powder, methamphetamine, and methaqualone are prohibited outright, even with a prescription. Cannabis is a controlled substance in Japan too, so do not bring cannabis, THC, or CBD products without checking the Narcotics Control Department list first. Check every medicine by its active ingredient, not its brand name, against the department's controlled substances list before you pack. The active ingredient is what the law sees. The brand name is not.

Two practical notes for winter and New Year travel. The Narcotics Control Department's online application trial started on November 4, 2026, and its page warns that emails sometimes do not come through because of its security settings, and that it cannot take responsibility if a permit cannot be issued in time because an email was not delivered, so check the page for current processing notices before you apply and send the follow-up note the page asks for rather than assuming the email route works. Its offices are closed from December 29, 2026 to January 3, 2027, and for entry between December 21 and January 4 it asks for complete application documents by December 7. If you take a controlled medicine and you are traveling over the New Year, that deadline is the one that decides whether the permission arrives in time.

4. The pharmacy: your prescription from home does not work here

Japan separates prescribing from dispensing. A doctor writes the prescription, and a separate pharmacy fills it. That is the same shape as the system in many countries, but Japan adds a rule that catches travelers by surprise: a Japanese pharmacy can only dispense against a prescription written by a doctor licensed in Japan. Under Japan's Pharmacists Act, a pharmacist may only dispense against a prescription from a doctor, dentist, or veterinarian, and under the Medical Practitioners Act a doctor in Japan must hold a licence from the Minister of Health, Labour and Welfare. A prescription from an overseas doctor, translated or not, is information, not permission. It shows a Japanese doctor what you take and why. It does not buy you medicine.

So the pharmacy visit, if you need one, has a fixed sequence. First, see a Japanese-registered doctor at a clinic or a hospital. A small internal medicine clinic, called a naika, usually takes walk-ins and is the fastest route for a straightforward replacement. Bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product. Tell the doctor the generic name of your insulin, not just the brand: insulin aspart, insulin lispro, insulin glargine, insulin degludec. The pharmacist and the doctor both work from the generic name, and the generic name is what survives the brand change at the border. NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid almost everywhere else, and both are insulin aspart. Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine. Our finding insulin abroad playbook covers the name game in full, including the concentration check that matters more than the brand name.

Then take the Japanese prescription to a dispensing pharmacy, called a chozai yakkyoku, which is usually next door to the clinic or across the street. The Japanese prescription is called a shohosen, and it is perishable. Under MHLW rules it is valid for four days including the day it was issued, and weekends and holidays count toward those four days. A prescription written on a Friday is valid through Monday. Go to the pharmacy the same day, because after the four days the pharmacy cannot fill it and you have to see the doctor again. A doctor can write a longer period on the form for special circumstances such as a long trip, but do not count on it. The same-day habit is the safe one.

Two more things to know before you walk in. First, the familiar insulins are sold in Japan. Novo Nordisk's Tresiba, insulin degludec, was approved in Japan in September 2012, and Sanofi's Lantus XR, insulin glargine at 300 units per milliliter, was approved in July 2015. Japanese insulin pens use the same U-100 standard, 100 units in each milliliter, so a pen dial counts the same way it does at home. If you buy a vial instead of a pen, check the concentration printed on the box before you use it, because a syringe is calibrated for one concentration only. Second, visitors pay the full amount. Japan's national health insurance does not cover a visitor, and JNTO's guide for visitors who fall ill says plainly that without overseas travel insurance you may be charged the full amount for medical care. Keep every receipt for your travel insurance claim, and check your policy before the trip so the clinic visit is not the first time you read it. Our travel insurance guide covers what to declare and what to look for.

5. Your devices in Japan: the app, the sensors, and the hot bath

Start with the phone, because it holds the one trap that ruins more international trips than any other. The Dexcom G7 app is region-locked at install: Dexcom's own user guide states the app must be installed in your country of residence, and the app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when you first install it. The check happens at install time. An app that is already installed and working keeps working abroad, and travelers report normal operation on international trips as long as nobody deletes the app mid-trip. So install the G7 app and update it to the latest version before you leave, turn off auto-updates before the trip so nothing about your diabetes setup changes mid-trip without your say-so, and do not delete the app while you are in Japan. A deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country, and that is a long trip without CGM data. Our G7 app region-lock guide covers the full prep list, and nothing in it is specific to Japan: the same rule applies everywhere you land.

The receiver is the backup that makes the app problem a non-issue. Dexcom's own international travel guidance says that if you have a receiver, it makes a great backup to your phone app. The receiver has no app store, no region check, and no install step. It pairs with your sensor and shows readings anywhere on earth. Pack it with its USB cable and charger, plus a plug adapter for Japan. If you do not already own a receiver, ask Dexcom about getting one before a big international trip. Bring the fingerstick meter too, as the final fallback, and keep it in your day bag rather than buried in your hotel room.

Buying replacement sensors in Japan is a pharmacy problem, not a shop problem. Dexcom sells the G7 in Japan, and Abbott sells FreeStyle Libre there with a Japan version of the LibreLink app, but a replacement sensor bought in Japan means navigating a foreign pharmacy, a local prescription, and regional compatibility questions you do not want to solve on vacation. Pack more sensors than the trip math says you need, with the spares in your carry-on, and do not plan to shop. The same applies to pump supplies. Carry spare Pods and a backup injection plan with syringes and a vial, and keep your basal rates written down somewhere that is not the pump. Our pod failure backup plan covers the sequence when a Pod fails away from home.

Then there is the onsen, the hot spring bath, which is one of the best parts of a winter trip to Japan and the one place where your devices meet real heat. Dexcom publishes exact temperature numbers for the G7. The sensor's operating range is 50F to 107.6F, and its storage range is 36F to 86F. A worn sensor sits against warm skin, so normal winter days do not push it out of range, but a long soak in a hot bath can push a worn sensor past its operating ceiling. Keep soaks short, keep the sensor out of the water where you can, and check the readings against your fingerstick meter after a bath. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. The same habit applies to the Pod. The Pod is rated waterproof to 25 feet for 60 minutes, which covers a swim, but a hot bath is a heat problem, not a depth problem, and heat loosens adhesive. Press the sensor and pod edges down firmly after every bath, and pack extra overpatches. Our G7 heat and cold guide has the full temperature table, and our adhesion playbook covers keeping a sensor and a pump stuck through holiday trips.

6. Time zones: Japan is UTC+9, and it never moves

Japan runs on one time zone for the whole country: Japan Standard Time, or JST, which is 9 hours ahead of Coordinated Universal Time, written as UTC+9. Japan does not observe daylight saving time, so the offset never changes through the year. The only thing that changes is the gap between Japan and home, and that changes because home moves its clocks, not because Japan does. From the United States West Coast, the gap is 17 hours in winter and 16 hours in summer. From London it is 9 hours in winter and 8 in summer. Either way, it is one of the biggest jumps a traveler can make, and the insulin timing needs a plan before the flight, not after landing.

The plan follows the rule in our time zone insulin guide. If you use a pump, change the pump clock to local time when you land, so your basal rates, the background insulin the pump drips all day, run on local time. Most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, so this is one manual step at the gate or the hotel. If you take multiple daily injections, keep your long-acting insulin on home time on the travel day, then switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone when you arrive, for example bedtime. Meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch: take it when you eat, wherever you are. The guide covers the eastbound and westbound cases in full, and the timing shift for a Japan trip is large enough that the careful planning earns its keep. Run your route through our time zone planner before you fly, and confirm the plan with your care team. The planner adjusts timing only, never dose amounts. Dose changes are a conversation with your care team, not something a tool or a blog post should decide.

7. Eating in Japan: the label that does the math for you

Japan is one of the easiest countries in the world to count carbs in, once you know where to look, and one of the hardest if you do not. The split is between packaged food and restaurant food, and the konbini, the convenience store on every corner, sits squarely on the easy side. Under Japan's Food Labeling Act, nutritional components labeling is mandatory on processed foods, and the label must list five items in the same order every time: calories, protein, fat, carbohydrate, and sodium shown as its salt equivalent. The carbohydrate number is in grams. The label is in Japanese, so learn the characters for the nutrition panel, or point your phone's camera at it with a translation app. The heading reads like nutrition facts, and the carbohydrate line is the fourth number in the list. Packaged food from a konbini, a supermarket, or a train station kiosk all carries the same panel, which makes a rice ball, a sandwich, or a bento box a known quantity instead of a guess. This guide gives no carb counts for specific foods, because the number on the package in front of you is the one that counts, and it is printed right there.

Restaurant food has no label, so it works the way eating out works everywhere. Ask what is in the dish, estimate the carbohydrate from the rice or the noodles, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. I pre-bolus in my usual way before meals. That habit travels with me, and Japan does not change it. A bowl of ramen is mostly noodles and broth, a plate of sushi is mostly rice, and a set meal with rice, fish, and soup is mostly the rice. Our eating out playbook covers the ordering and estimating routine in full, and our pre-bolus guide covers the timing habit. For lows, keep sugar packets in your day bag. I treat lows with sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, and that is the low kit that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day in any country. Do not let the low kit be something you buy after you need it. Pack it before you leave the hotel.

8. Winter in Japan: cold, snow, and the hotel fridge

Winter is the season this guide is written for, and cold is the thing to plan around. The good news is that a worn sensor sits against warm skin under your clothes, so normal winter days and even ski trips do not push it out of its operating range. Body heat does the work for you. The weak links are the spares and the phone. Spare sensors must be stored between 36F and 86F, so do not leave them in a freezing car overnight or in an unheated bag on a cold tarmac. Keep them insulated in your carry-on, inside your jacket or bag rather than an outer pocket, and let a cold spare warm up indoors before you put it on. Phones give up in extreme cold long before the sensor does, so keep your phone in an inside pocket on bitter days and bring a portable charger, since cold drains batteries faster. Our skiing playbook covers the cold and altitude routine for a ski trip, and the same habits apply to a cold day walking around Sapporo or Kyoto.

Insulin follows the same cold rule it follows everywhere. Unopened insulin belongs in the fridge at 36 to 46F, and in-use insulin is fine at room temperature below 77F. Insulin freezes at 32F, and frozen insulin must be discarded. On a winter trip that means a FRIO cooling wallet or an insulated case with a gel pack in the day bag for the in-use insulin, and care with the hotel fridge for the unopened supply. Hotel minibar fridges can freeze items at the back, so keep unopened insulin away from the cooling element and check that it has not frozen. Our insulin cooling guide covers the methods, and our hotel insulin storage guide covers the minibar trap in detail. The one winter habit that matters most is the simplest: keep the supplies on your body or in the bag that stays with you, not in a cold car, a cold room, or a checked bag.

9. If something goes wrong: numbers, clinics, and the insurance call

Save three numbers in your phone before you land. Dial 119 for fire and ambulance, and 110 for police. Both are free and work nationwide. If you need help in English, call the Japan Visitor Hotline run by the Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) at 050-3816-2787. It answers 24 hours a day, every day of the year, in English, Chinese, and Korean, and it can help with illness, accidents, disasters, and finding a medical institution that can see a foreign visitor. JNTO also runs an online search for medical institutions by area, language, and department, so you can find an English-speaking clinic before you need one. If you are with someone who speaks Japanese, the sentence to show them is to ask them to call an ambulance, and JNTO's guide prints it for exactly that moment.

If you develop symptoms of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous complication where the blood turns acidic when insulin runs too low, get urgent help right away. Vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation, and that is a hospital visit, not a wait-and-see day. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the ketone checks and the plan for that day. Carry glucagon in your day bag, and make sure your travel companion knows where it is and how to use it. Our glucagon emergency kit guide covers what is in the kit and how to keep it travel-ready. If the trip goes wrong in a bigger way, a bag lost or stolen, our lost or stolen supplies playbook covers the exact call order, and the travel insurance emergency line is the call that arranges care, including a local doctor visit. Check your policy before the trip, so the call is not the first time you read it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do I need a Yunyu Kakunin-sho to bring insulin into Japan?

A: For insulin, apply for one. Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) says injections may be brought in only as pre-filled syringes or self-injection kits, and its Q&A says to apply for a Yunyu Kakunin-sho, an import confirmation certificate, and receive it before you leave home. The application is online with an English option. For ordinary prescription tablets the line is different: up to a one-month supply needs no special procedure, and more than that needs the same certificate.

Q: How much insulin can I bring into Japan without paperwork?

A: Do not count on a paperwork-free allowance for insulin, because insulin is an injection and MHLW's Q&A treats injections separately from oral medicine. Apply for the Yunyu Kakunin-sho for your insulin, pens, and syringes before you travel, and show it to customs if asked. The one-month rule you will see quoted everywhere applies to ordinary prescription medicine taken by mouth: up to a one-month supply needs no special procedure, and anything over that needs the certificate.

Q: Can a Japanese pharmacy fill my prescription from home?

A: No. Japanese pharmacies dispense only against a prescription written by a doctor licensed in Japan, so a prescription from your home country cannot be filled there. If you run out of insulin in Japan, see a Japanese-registered doctor at a clinic or hospital, show your prescription and doctor's letter so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the Japanese prescription, called a shohosen, to a dispensing pharmacy the same day: it is valid for four days including the day it was issued, weekends and holidays included.

Q: What medicines are banned in Japan?

A: Medicines containing methamphetamine or amphetamine are prohibited outright, including Adderall, and no certificate or prescription makes them legal to carry. Narcotics such as morphine, fentanyl, and oxycodone, and stimulant raw materials such as lisdexamfetamine, need advance permission from Japan's Narcotics Control Department before you travel. Cannabis is controlled in Japan too, so do not bring cannabis, THC, or CBD products without checking the Narcotics Control Department list first. Check every medicine by its active ingredient, not its brand name, against the Narcotics Control Department list before you pack.

Q: Will my Dexcom G7 app work in Japan?

A: Yes, if it was already installed and set up at home. The Dexcom G7 app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when it is first installed, so install and update it before you leave and do not delete it while you are abroad: a deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country. Bring the Dexcom receiver as backup. It has no app store and no install check, and it pairs with your sensor anywhere.

Q: What are the emergency numbers in Japan?

A: Dial 119 for fire and ambulance and 110 for police. Both are free and work nationwide. If you need help in English, call the Japan Visitor Hotline run by the Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) at 050-3816-2787. It answers 24 hours a day, every day of the year, in English, Chinese, and Korean, and can help with illness, accidents, and finding a clinic.
