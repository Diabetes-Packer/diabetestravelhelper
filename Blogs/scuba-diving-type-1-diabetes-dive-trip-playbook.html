Scuba Diving with Type 1 Diabetes: The DAN Rules, the Pre-Dive Checks, and the Trip (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 10 min read

If a winter dive trip is on your calendar, a reef in the Caribbean, a liveaboard, or a first certification course, the first question is not which resort to pick. It is whether you can dive at all, and what the rules ask of you before you get in the water. For a long time the answer was simple and final: no. Diabetes was treated as an automatic bar to scuba diving, and plenty of people who wanted to dive either gave up on it or hid the condition from the dive center, which is the worst of both worlds. That changed in 2005, when Divers Alert Network (DAN), the dive safety organization, and the Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society (UHMS), the international society for diving and hyperbaric medicine, ran a workshop and published consensus guidelines that opened recreational diving to people with diabetes who meet clear criteria and follow a clear protocol. This guide walks through those rules as they are published, so you can find out where you stand before you book anything.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. This guide describes the rules for diving with diabetes. It is not a story about a dive trip. It is built from the published guidelines of DAN and UHMS, the Professional Association of Diving Instructors (PADI), a recreational diver training agency, and a 2020 review in Diving and Hyperbaric Medicine. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own travel plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not diving, tell your care team.

1. The old rule, and the one that replaced it

For decades, the diving medicine community held that insulin-treated diabetes was an absolute bar to scuba diving. The worry was straightforward: low blood sugar can change consciousness fast, and a fast change in consciousness underwater can end in drowning. The ban was not universal and it did not hold everywhere: the United Kingdom reversed its ban in 1991, and divers with diabetes kept diving anyway, openly or quietly, which meant dive centers often did not know and could not plan for it. The 2005 workshop was the response to that reality. On June 19, 2005, in Las Vegas, more than 50 clinicians and researchers from seven nations met under UHMS and DAN, reviewed the data, and wrote consensus guidelines in three parts: who can dive, how deep and how long, and what to do with glucose on the day of the dive.

Two things about those guidelines matter before anything else. First, the workshop said plainly that they are guidelines, not rules, and that different groups may apply them differently. Second, they are a floor, not a promise. Meeting every criterion means you may reasonably dive, not that any doctor must clear you or any dive center must take you. PADI, the training agency most new divers meet first, puts it the same way in its own guidance for instructors: historically the condition was an absolute diving disqualifier, but many people with diabetes can now dive if they comply with the guidelines, and unless you are a doctor, do not give medical advice to diabetic divers. That is the shape of this whole guide: the published rules, and the doctor at the end of them.

2. Who can dive: the clearance checklist

The guidelines start with the same medical fitness evaluation every other diver candidate gets, to make sure no other condition bars you from diving, such as epilepsy, lung disease, or heart disease. Then they add the diabetes criteria. All of them must be met, and they are checked again every year:

Age 18 or older. The limit may be lowered to 16 if a special training program is available, one designed for young people with diabetes and ideally involving parents or guardians.

Time on your current treatment. At least three months must have passed since starting or changing oral diabetes medicine, or one year since starting insulin. An established diver who is started on insulin should wait at least six months before resuming diving.

No severe episode in the past year. There should have been no low or high blood sugar needing help from another person for at least one year, and no history of hypoglycemia unawareness, the loss of the warning signs that blood sugar is dropping.

HbA1c 9% or lower. HbA1c is a blood test that shows average blood sugar over the past two to three months. It should be 9% or lower when measured no more than one month before the first assessment, and again at each annual review. Above 9%, the guidelines say to contact your diabetes specialist for further evaluation.

No significant secondary complications. That means no eye damage worse than early background retinopathy, no significant nerve damage, no kidney damage causing protein in the urine, and no coronary artery disease or significant peripheral vascular disease. The guidelines explain why: eye damage raises the risk of a retinal bleed from small mask pressure changes, nerve damage can cause a sharp blood pressure drop when leaving the water, and vascular disease can change how gas leaves the body and raise the risk of decompression sickness.

An annual review, every year. Within two months of the first diving medical assessment and at each annual review, your primary care physician, who knows your diabetes, must confirm that the criteria above are met, that you use your glucose meter accurately, and that you understand how food, exercise, stress, temperature, and blood sugar relate to each other. The diving medical examination itself is done by, or in consultation with, a doctor trained in diving medicine. If you are over 40, the guidelines also call for an evaluation for silent ischemia, heart disease that causes no symptoms, because immersion adds stress to the heart and diabetes can hide the warning signs.

If any one of those fails, the answer is not to argue with the list. It is to work on the item with your care team and come back when it is met. Our pre-trip appointment guide covers the questions to ask your care team before any big trip, and a dive trip is the one where that appointment matters most.

3. The medical form: question 8 and Box F

Before any training or diving, the dive center hands you the Diver Medical Participant Questionnaire, the standard medical screen used by PADI and most other recreational training agencies. The current version is dated 2026-01-01, and it was created by the Diver Medical Screen Committee with UHMS, DAN in the United States, DAN Europe, and the hyperbaric medicine division at the University of California, San Diego. The form has ten yes or no questions on the first page. Question 8 reads: "I have had back problems, hernia, ulcers, or diabetes." Answering yes sends you to Box F on the second page, which lists "Diabetes, either drug or diet controlled, or gestational diabetes within the last 12 months."

A yes in Box F means a physician's medical evaluation is required before any in-water session, including the pool. You take all three pages of the form, the questionnaire and the physician's evaluation form, to your doctor, and participation in a diving course requires your physician's approval. Answering no to all ten questions means no medical evaluation is required, but that is not an option for a diver with diabetes, and it should not be gamed: the form says to answer all questions honestly, and a yes does not disqualify you, it routes you to the doctor who can clear you. Bring the form to the appointment in section 2, not to the dive shop on the morning of the course. A doctor's signature takes time, and a dive center that finds an unsigned yes on the morning of the dive will not put you in the water.

4. The dive itself: depth, time, and the buddy

Once cleared, the guidelines set the shape of the dive. All of these are recommendations, and all of them are there so you can get to the surface fast if something goes wrong:

No deeper than 30 meters (100 feet). The depth limit exists because nitrogen narcosis, the confused and foggy feeling that comes with depth, can look like low blood sugar, and the two need different responses. Staying above 30 meters keeps that confusion out of the picture.

No longer than 60 minutes. The time limit exists to moderate how long your glucose goes unmonitored. You cannot check a meter at depth, so the dive stays short enough that the pre-dive checks still mean something.

No compulsory decompression stops, and no overhead environments. No caves, no wreck penetration, nothing that puts a ceiling between you and the surface. If a low hits, the way up must be open.

Avoid prolonged cold and arduous dives. Cold water, strong currents, and dives that demand strenuous activity all raise the risk of a low, so the guidelines say to avoid combinations of those conditions. DAN's own Alert Diver summary says the same thing in plain words: do not dive in cold water, strong currents, or conditions that demand strenuous activity.

Dive with an informed buddy, who should not have diabetes. Your buddy and the dive leader must know your condition and know the right response if a low happens. The guidelines recommend that the buddy does not have diabetes, so that if you go low, the person next to you is not going low at the same time.

The 2020 review in Diving and Hyperbaric Medicine adds one more idea the 2005 guidelines did not include: graduated clearance. Start at the shallowest depths with experienced partners, prove your glucose control at each step, and go deeper only after that. Depth stays limited to 30 meters until your diving competence, your diabetes control, and your emergency management are well established, and any increase after that is thoughtful and gradual. For a first dive trip, that means shallow reef dives with an experienced buddy, not the deep wall on day one.

5. The pre-dive glucose routine: three checks, one floor

The glucose routine on the day of a dive is the most specific part of the whole protocol, and it is the part the dive center can see you follow. Here it is, exactly as DAN publishes it:

Three checks before the dive. Check your blood glucose at 60 minutes, at 30 minutes, and immediately before diving. The three readings are compared as a trend, not taken one at a time.

The floor is 150 mg/dL (8.3 mmol/L), stable or rising. All three readings should be at or above 150, and the level should be stable or rising across the three checks. A 2020 review in Diving and Hyperbaric Medicine suggests a pre-dive target range of 7 to 12 mmol/L (126 to 216 mg/dL), which is a wider and slightly lower range than the 2005 floor. Your own target is set with your doctor, and the 150 floor is the number the dive center will know.

Delay the dive below 150 or above 300 mg/dL (16.7 mmol/L). If any reading is below 150 or above 300, the dive waits. Delay the dive too if the level falls between any two of the three checks, even if every reading is above 150. A falling trend before the dive is a warning, not a pass.

Check yourself first. On every day you plan to dive, assess how you feel. If you are uncomfortable, unduly anxious, or unwell in any way, including seasickness, or if your glucose control is not in its normal stable pattern, do not dive. Vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation, and ketones can mean diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous complication where the blood turns acidic when insulin runs too low. That is a sick day, not a dive day. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the ketone checks and the plan for that day.

Plan the food, not just the checks. The 2020 review recommends eating a meal at least 1.5 to 2 hours before the dive, preferably 3 hours, so that insulin levels are lower when the dive starts and the risk of a low during the dive is smaller. It also recommends at least two weeks of continuous glucose monitor (CGM) data before you start diving, so you and your doctor can see how your glucose moves across normal days before you add diving to the mix. Any change to insulin or tablet timing around a dive is a plan you work out with your care team before the trip, not something to improvise on the boat. This guide does not state any such change.

After the dive, keep checking. Check your blood glucose at the end of every dive, and the same floor applies to any second dive that day. Then keep checking frequently for 12 to 15 hours after diving, because glucose can fall late after a dive. Drink plenty of water on dive days: high blood sugar makes you pass more urine, and dehydration after a dive is a real risk. Log every dive with the glucose readings and anything you did about them, so the next dive is planned from your own data, not from memory.

6. What to carry for a low, and the L-signal

The rescue plan is short, and every diver on the trip should know it before the first dive. DAN's rule is that divers with diabetes carry oral glucose in a readily accessible and ingestible form at the surface and during all dives. That is not a suggestion, it is the rule, and it sits in the guidelines next to the glucose checks. A glucagon injection should be available at the surface, and the dive buddy or another person at the surface should know how to use it. Our glucagon emergency kit guide covers what is in the kit and how to keep it travel-ready.

Two habits from my own travel routine, kept separate from the dive rule. On land, I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. That is my land habit, and it is what lives in my pocket on a normal travel day. On a dive, the rule is DAN's: oral glucose goes on the dive, glucagon stays at the surface, and the buddy carries glucose too. Do not mix the two up, and do not leave the dive glucose in your bag on the boat.

If low blood sugar is suspected underwater, the DAN protocol is to surface with your buddy, establish positive buoyancy, take glucose, and leave the water. The hand signal for a suspected low is an L made with the thumb and index finger of either hand. Practice it with your buddy before the dive, along with the surface routine, so that nobody is learning it during a real low. A medical alert bracelet is also on DAN's recommendation list, so that a rescuer or a medic on the boat knows about the diabetes without having to ask.

7. Pump and CGM underwater: what the ratings actually cover

This is the section where the numbers most often get mixed up, so here they are, side by side. A water-resistance rating is a test result for a specific depth and time, and a scuba dive goes far beyond every one of them.

The pump comes off before the dive. The 2020 review is direct: pumps are generally described as water-resistant, but only to depths of 2.5 to 3.5 meters for short periods, and neither the structural integrity nor the delivery performance is tested beyond those near-surface conditions. Its recommendation is that all insulin pumps should be disconnected and removed before dives, and reconnected as soon as possible after diving, to reinstate insulin delivery, glucose monitoring, and control. The plan for pump wear during dives is a question for your care team and the dive doctor, not a setting you change on the boat.

The Omnipod Pod rating does not cover scuba depth. Insulet rates the Pod IP28, waterproof to 25 feet for 60 minutes, and the Omnipod 5 Controller is not waterproof. Twenty-five feet is about a third of the way to the 100-foot dive limit in the guidelines, and the rating is for 60 minutes at most. A Pod that stays on for a shallow snorkel is inside its rating. A Pod taken to 100 feet is not, and the manufacturer warns that going deeper or staying under longer can damage the Pod and cause it to deliver too much or too little insulin. The Controller stays dry on the boat either way.

The Dexcom G7 rating does not cover scuba depth either. Dexcom says the G7 sensor is waterproof and may be submerged under 8 feet of water for up to 24 hours without failure when properly installed. Eight feet is a swimming pool, not a dive. The sensor can stay on for the surface swim and the boat, and it keeps reading after the dive, but it is not rated for the dive itself. Salt water and long days in the water also loosen adhesive, so pack extra overpatches and press the sensor and pod edges down firmly after every swim, the same habit our cruising playbook recommends for pool and shore days.

What about a CGM underwater? DAN Europe ran research on exactly this. In one study, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) in a waterproof case was used during 26 dives in a hyperbaric chamber, with readings every five minutes before, during, and after the dives, and the researchers found no statistical differences among the readings. Their conclusion was that a real-time CGM used while diving can give immediate information on glucose values and trend, which could increase dive safety, because low blood sugar symptoms underwater are easily confused with other diving symptoms such as nitrogen narcosis. That is research with a cased device, not a clearance for a bare sensor at depth, and it does not change the decision rule: the three fingerstick checks decide whether you dive, and the sensor is a trend line on the surface, not a replacement for the meter.

8. Packing for a dive trip: insulin, insurance, and the boat bag

The dive rules are the hard part. The packing is the part you already know, with a few dive-specific twists.

Pack 20% extra of everything. Pods, sensors, insulin, test strips, lancets, ketone strips, and the low kit, all in your carry-on, never in checked luggage. Our packing list does the counting for the trip length, and the 20% buffer covers the pod that fails on a boat and the sensor that peels off in salt water. Bring backup insulin for injections in case a pod fails mid-trip, and keep your insulin plan written down somewhere that is not the pump.

Keep insulin cool, and out of the freeze. Unopened insulin belongs in the fridge at 36 to 46F, and in-use insulin is fine at room temperature below 77F. Insulin freezes at 32F, and frozen insulin must be discarded. On a dive trip that means a FRIO cooling wallet or an insulated case with a gel pack in the day bag, never insulin in a hot boat bag in the sun, and never insulin in the hotel minibar fridge, which can freeze items at the back. Our insulin cooling guide covers the methods, and our hotel insulin storage guide covers the minibar trap in detail.

Sort the insurance before you book. DAN membership is the standard safety net for a dive trip. Regular membership costs $40 a year and includes DAN TravelAssist emergency medical transportation up to $150,000 when you are more than 50 miles from home, plus a 24/7 emergency hotline staffed by doctors, nurses, and paramedics. Enhanced membership costs $75 a year and raises the transportation benefit to $500,000. The important distinction is that membership alone does not cover the medical costs of a dive accident: dive accident insurance must be purchased separately, with plans starting at $42 a year, and it covers hyperbaric chamber treatment, physician charges, and hospital charges. If an evacuation is ever needed, you must contact DAN TravelAssist through the emergency hotline, +1-919-684-9111, before making arrangements, or the costs may not be covered. Check your regular travel insurance too: our travel insurance guide covers what to declare and what to look for, and scuba diving is one of the activities that needs a specific check.

The trip around the diving. If the dive trip is based at a resort, our all-inclusive resort playbook covers the food and the heat. If the diving is from a liveaboard or a cruise ship, the cruising playbook covers the cabin fridge trap and the shore-excursion kit. The flight there follows the same airport rules as any trip, covered in our TSA rules guide, and if the trip crosses time zones, run the route through our time zone insulin guide before you fly. For an international dive destination, our international travel guide covers insulin at the border and the time zone reset on landing.

  • Pick the dives to the guidelines: no deeper than 30 meters (100 feet), no longer than 60 minutes, no decompression stops, no overhead environments, no prolonged cold or arduous dives

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I scuba dive with type 1 diabetes?

A: Yes, if you meet the clearance rules and follow the dive-day protocol. The 2005 guidelines from Divers Alert Network (DAN) and the Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society (UHMS) say recreational diving is open to people with diabetes who pass the medical evaluation, have no significant secondary complications, and follow the glucose checks and dive limits in the protocol. Diabetes used to be an automatic bar to diving, and the guidelines changed that, but they are guidelines, not a promise: the doctor who examines you makes the call, and a dive center can still ask for its own medical clearance.

Q: What does the doctor check before clearing me to dive?

A: The doctor checks that no other condition bars you from diving, that your diabetes is stable, and that you have no significant secondary complications. The published criteria are age 18 or older, at least three months since starting or changing oral diabetes medicine or one year since starting insulin, no low or high blood sugar needing help from another person in the past year, no history of hypoglycemia unawareness, an HbA1c of 9 percent or lower measured within a month of the first assessment and at each annual review, and an annual review with your physician or diabetes specialist. If you are over 40, the guidelines also call for an evaluation for silent heart disease.

Q: What blood sugar do I need before a dive?

A: At least 150 mg/dL (8.3 mmol/L), stable or rising, shown by three checks: 60 minutes, 30 minutes, and immediately before the dive. Delay the dive if any reading is below 150 or above 300 mg/dL (16.7 mmol/L), or if the level falls between any two of the three checks. A 2020 review in Diving and Hyperbaric Medicine suggests a pre-dive target range of 7 to 12 mmol/L (126 to 216 mg/dL), so your own target is set with your doctor. Check again at the end of every dive, and keep checking frequently for 12 to 15 hours after diving.

Q: Can I wear my insulin pump or CGM underwater?

A: Not at scuba depth. The 2020 review says pumps are generally water-resistant only to 2.5 to 3.5 meters for short periods, and that all insulin pumps should be disconnected and removed before dives and reconnected as soon as possible after. The Omnipod Pod is rated IP28, waterproof to 25 feet for 60 minutes, and the Dexcom G7 sensor is waterproof under 8 feet for up to 24 hours when properly installed, but neither rating covers a dive to 100 feet. DAN Europe research used a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) in a waterproof case during dives, with readings every five minutes, and found real-time values and trend helpful, but the sensor on its own is not a dive instrument: the three fingerstick checks decide whether you dive.

Q: What do I carry on the dive for a low?

A: Oral glucose, carried in a readily accessible form on every dive, and glucagon available at the surface with a buddy or surface person who knows how to use it. If a low is suspected underwater, the DAN rule is to surface with your buddy, establish positive buoyancy, take glucose, and leave the water. The hand signal for a suspected low is an L made with the thumb and index finger. On land I treat lows with sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, but on a dive the rule is DAN's: oral glucose on the dive, glucagon at the surface.

Q: Do I need DAN membership or dive insurance for a dive trip?

A: You do not need DAN membership to dive, but it is the standard safety net for a dive trip. Regular membership costs $40 a year and includes DAN TravelAssist emergency medical transportation up to $150,000 when you are more than 50 miles from home, plus a 24/7 emergency hotline. Membership alone does not cover the medical costs of a dive accident, so dive accident insurance is bought separately, with plans starting at $42 a year, and it covers hyperbaric chamber treatment and hospital charges. Check that your regular travel insurance covers scuba diving too, and carry the DAN hotline number, +1-919-684-9111, in your phone.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your insulin plan, your glucose targets, and your treatment decisions are conversations with your care team, ideally at an appointment four to six weeks before your next trip. DAN, UHMS, and PADI guidance changes over time, so confirm the current details on dan.org, uhms.org, and padi.com before you travel. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not diving, tell your care team. If you have a severe low on a dive trip, tell the people around you, treat it with the plan your care team set, and seek medical care.
