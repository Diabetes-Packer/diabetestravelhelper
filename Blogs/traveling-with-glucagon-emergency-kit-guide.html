Traveling with Glucagon: The Emergency Kit People Forget to Pack (2026)

Updated October 2026 · 10 min read

Picture the scene: it is the holiday season, you are at a family gathering three states or three countries from home, and your blood sugar drops hard. Not the kind of low a sugar packet fixes, but the kind where you cannot help yourself. That is the scenario glucagon exists for, and it is the one piece of diabetes gear that rarely makes the packing list. Insulin, sensors, snacks, travel letters, all packed. The emergency kit for the worst-case low stays home in the medicine cabinet.

This guide fixes that before the holiday travel season and November Diabetes Awareness Month. It covers what glucagon is, the three modern options (Baqsimi, Gvoke, and Zegalogue), how each one actually travels, the TSA rules, and the pre-trip briefing your travel companion needs. Every product fact below is taken from the current FDA-approved label, verified October 2026.

The rule that makes everything else work. Glucagon is given by someone else, not by you. That is the entire point: a severe low means you cannot act for yourself. So the single most important travel step is not buying the device, it is making sure your travel companion knows where it is and how to use it before wheels-up. The ADA (American Diabetes Association) says the same thing in its 2026 Standards of Care: family, caregivers, and others providing support should know glucagon's location and be educated on how to administer it.

What glucagon is, and what it is not

Glucagon is a hormone your body already makes. Its job is the opposite of insulin's: it signals your liver to release stored sugar (glycogen) into your blood, raising blood sugar. Every glucagon emergency product is a copy of that signal, delivered by someone standing next to you.

The threshold that matters is severe hypoglycemia: very low blood sugar so serious you need another person's help to recover. That is how all three FDA labels define it. The everyday low (60 to 70 on your CGM, the continuous glucose monitor on your arm) gets sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. The severe low gets glucagon, because you are not in a state to reach for anything. Travel stacks the odds toward that scenario: red-eye flights, jet lag, unfamiliar food and activity, alcohol at holiday dinners, and routine chaos in general. If you have never needed glucagon, that is the best argument for packing it, not a reason to skip it.

The ADA's position: it belongs on the packing list

The ADA's 2026 Standards of Care are direct on this point. Recommendation 6.16 states that glucagon should be prescribed for all individuals taking insulin or at high risk for hypoglycemia. The same recommendation adds that family, caregivers, school personnel, and others providing support should know its location and be educated on how to administer it, and that glucagon preparations that do not have to be reconstituted are preferred.

That last phrase is the travel case: the old kits required someone to mix powder with liquid under pressure, a hard job for a panicking family member. All three modern options are premixed or preloaded, no reconstitution, no multi-step assembly, so a scared companion or a stranger can use them.

The three options, compared for travel

There are three modern glucagon products, each from a different angle. Which one you carry is a conversation with your care team and pharmacist. What follows is how each one actually behaves in a suitcase, at a checkpoint, and in a hotel room, taken from the current FDA-approved labels.

Baqsimi: the needle-free nasal powder

Baqsimi is glucagon as a nasal powder. Each device holds one 3 mg dose and is single use: it cannot be reused, and the tube gets discarded after use. It is for nasal use only, the dose does not need to be inhaled, and it works even if the person has a cold or is taking cold medicine.

For travel, Baqsimi's advantages are practical. First, no needle, which lowers the confidence barrier for a companion or a stranger who has never given an injection. Second, heat tolerance: the label says to store it in the shrink-wrapped tube at temperatures up to 86F (30C), with no refrigeration required, the most forgiving of the three for warm-weather trips. Third, the moisture rule: do not remove the shrink wrap or open the tube until you are ready to use it, and do not push the plunger or test it beforehand. If the tube has been opened, moisture can get in and it may not work as expected.

The label's after-use sequence is what your companion briefing covers: after giving Baqsimi, call for emergency medical help right away. If the person is unconscious, turn them on their side. When they can safely swallow, give a fast-acting source of sugar such as juice, then a snack. If there is no response after 15 minutes, another dose may be given, if available. Replace the device before the expiration date printed on the tube.

Gvoke: the true room-temperature auto-injector

Gvoke is glucagon as a ready-mixed liquid, injected under the skin (subcutaneous). It comes as the Gvoke HypoPen auto-injector, a prefilled syringe (PFS), or a vial and syringe kit. Each device is a single dose and is not reused. Do not open the foil pouch until you are ready to use it.

Gvoke's travel calling card is its storage: a true room-temperature product. The label specifies storage at 68F to 77F (20C to 25C), with excursions permitted from 59F to 86F (15C to 30C). It says do not refrigerate or freeze, and do not expose it to extreme temperatures. Keep the HypoPen or prefilled syringe in its original sealed foil pouch until use.

The after-use sequence: call for emergency assistance immediately. Turn the person on their side. When they respond, give oral carbohydrates to restore the liver's glycogen and prevent the low from returning. If there is no response after 15 minutes, an additional dose from a new device may be given while waiting for emergency assistance.

Zegalogue: the glucagon analog with a fridge question

Zegalogue is dasiglucagon, an analog of glucagon, in a single-dose autoinjector or prefilled syringe given under the skin. The label's recommended dose is 0.6 mg per device, each device is single use and cannot be reused, and the after-use sequence is the same shape as the others: call for emergency assistance immediately, give another dose after 15 minutes if there is no response and a second device is available.

Zegalogue's travel story is its storage. The label's default is the refrigerator (36F to 46F, do not freeze), but it permits a room-temperature window of 68F to 77F for up to 12 months. Record the date it leaves the refrigerator on the protective case, do not put it back in the refrigerator afterward, and discard it after the 12-month room-temperature period or the expiration date, whichever comes first.

Practically, that means the Zegalogue travel plan needs one extra step before the trip: check with your care team and pharmacist about the fridge plan. If your device already lives at room temperature within the label's 12-month window, it travels like the others.

The travel-day rules

The products differ, but the travel logistics are the same. Glucagon rides with you, not in the cargo hold.

Carry-on, personal item, reachable. Glucagon emergency kits are permitted in carry-on as emergency medical supplies. Keep the kit in your personal item, the bag under the seat in front of you, not in the overhead bin you cannot reach when it matters. Checked luggage is out: cargo holds freeze, heat up, and get lost, and a kit in the belly of the plane helps nobody on board. The site's carry-on packing list shows where it fits in the kit layout.

Original labeled container at the checkpoint. Carry glucagon in its original, pharmaceutically labeled container. Declare your diabetes supplies to the TSA (Transportation Security Administration) officer, and keep the kit where screening is quick. The full checkpoint playbook for diabetes supplies is in the TSA rules guide, including the medically necessary exemption from the 3-1-1 liquid rule.

Heat is the enemy of potency. A hot car, a beach bag, or a sunlit dashboard blows past every storage ceiling in this guide. Keep glucagon in cabin-temperature conditions: with you, in the shade, in the hotel room, the same way you protect your insulin from the same heat.

Brief your companion before wheels-up. This is the ADA's recommendation made concrete. Sit down before the trip, show your travel companion the actual device, walk through the printed instructions, and agree on where it will live: the personal item, the hotel nightstand, the daypack. The briefing covers the what (the device), the where (always the same spot), and the when (call emergency help first, per the label).

Medical ID travels with you. A medical ID or a phone lock-screen note that says type 1 diabetes, the glucagon location, and an emergency contact turns bystanders into helpers. If you cannot speak, the kit and the card speak for you.

The glucagon travel checklist

Here is the full kit, in packing order:

  1. Your glucagon device in its original labeled packaging, with the printed instructions. Know which product you carry and how it is used before you leave.
  2. A second device if space allows. Two is the 20 percent buffer this site runs on, and all three products are sold in two-packs. The second dose also covers the label's 15-minute no-response sequence.
  3. An expiration date check before departure. Replace the device before the date printed on the tube, case, or pouch. Check it every trip, not just once.
  4. Fast sugar on your person for the everyday lows: sugar packets (1 at 60 to 70, 2 below 60), plus juice or another fast-acting carb where it fits.
  5. Your CGM (continuous glucose monitor) charged with low alarms loud, phone and power bank in your personal item, plus a fingerstick meter as backup.
  6. Your diabetes travel letter and prescription list. The travel letter guide has the template.
  7. The TSA plan for getting through security with all of it: the diabetes TSA rules guide.
  8. Medical ID or a lock-screen note: type 1 diabetes, where the glucagon is, emergency contact name and number.
  9. The companion briefing done before the trip: device shown, instructions read, storage spot agreed, emergency call order practiced once.
  10. The heat plan: glucagon stays in cabin-temperature conditions. Never a hot car, never a beach bag, never a sunlit dashboard.
  11. Travel insurance that covers pre-existing conditions, in case a disrupted trip becomes a medical one. See the travel insurance guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What counts as severe hypoglycemia when traveling?

A: Severe hypoglycemia is very low blood sugar that is so serious you need another person's help to recover. That is the threshold at which glucagon enters the picture: ordinary lows you treat yourself with fast sugar, but a severe low means someone else has to act. The FDA labels for Baqsimi, Gvoke, and Zegalogue all define it this way.

Q: Do I still need glucagon if I can treat my own lows?

A: Yes. The ADA's 2026 Standards of Care recommend that glucagon be prescribed for everyone taking insulin, even if they have never had a severe low. Travel is exactly the situation where a first severe low can blindside you: new time zones, unfamiliar food, long active days, and alcohol at family gatherings all move blood sugar in ways home routines never do.

Q: Can I bring glucagon through TSA?

A: Yes. Glucagon emergency kits are permitted in carry-on as emergency medical supplies. Keep it in its original, pharmaceutically labeled container, declare your diabetes supplies to the TSA officer, and keep the kit reachable in your personal item rather than the overhead bin. Medically necessary diabetes supplies are exempt from the 3-1-1 liquid rule.

Q: Which glucagon is best for travel: Baqsimi, Gvoke, or Zegalogue?

A: No one option is best for everyone, and the choice belongs to you and your care team. The travel differences are practical ones: Baqsimi is a needle-free nasal powder stored up to 86F in its tube; Gvoke is a ready-to-use liquid at true room temperature with no refrigeration at all; Zegalogue is a glucagon analog that is refrigerated by default but can be kept at room temperature for up to 12 months.

Q: What if I have never had a severe low?

A: That is the best time to pack glucagon, not the reason to skip it. Emergency supplies exist for the event you have never met: travel adds variables your home routine never has, from red-eye flights to unfamiliar activity to drinking around the holidays. The ADA recommends a glucagon prescription for everyone on insulin regardless of low history.

Q: Does heat ruin glucagon on a trip?

A: Every glucagon product has a storage ceiling, which is why the heat plan matters: Baqsimi keeps up to 86F (30C) in its shrink-wrapped tube, Gvoke allows excursions to 86F and must never be exposed to extreme temperatures, and Zegalogue's room-temperature window is 68F to 77F for up to 12 months. Hot cars, beach bags, and sunlit dashboards exceed those ceilings fast. Keep glucagon with you in cabin-temperature conditions and replace it before the printed expiration date.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Which glucagon product you carry, and when and how it should be used, is a conversation with your care team. Airline and TSA policies change, so confirm your flight's current rules before you travel. If you use glucagon on a trip, tell your care team afterward.
