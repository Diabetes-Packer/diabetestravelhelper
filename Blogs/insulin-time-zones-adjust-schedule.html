Crossing Time Zones with Diabetes: How to Adjust Your Insulin Schedule (2026)

Holiday season means long-haul flights, and the question filling every diabetes forum this time of year is always the same: what happens to my insulin schedule when I cross six time zones? Your insulin routine runs on a clock. A long flight moves that clock. The fix is simpler than the worry.

Updated October 2026  ·  7 min read

I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years, and the time zone question is the one travelers ask most nervously, because it feels like the math should be complicated. It is not. Everything below is about timing: when each dose happens in the new time zone. Dose amounts are a separate conversation, and it belongs to you and your care team, not to a blog post or a planning tool.

The day gets longer or shorter

Here is the whole puzzle in two sentences. Flying east shortens your day. Flying west stretches it. Your long-acting insulin was dosed for a 24-hour day, and the travel day is not a 24-hour day, so the gaps between doses change. That is what you are managing: the gaps, not the insulin itself.

Step zero: talk to your care team first

Do this before you pack, not at the gate. Bring your flight details: departure time, flight length, and arrival local time. That is exactly what diabetes travel guidance says to have on hand, because the advice depends on the direction, the size of the shift, and your regimen. The framework below is the standard timing approach used in published travel guidance; your care team personalizes it to you.

If you use an insulin pump

I wear a pump, so my version of this is the simplest one. When you land, change your pump's clock to local time. Your basal rates, the background insulin the pump drips all day, then run on local time automatically. Published pump travel guidance says exactly this: wait until you arrive, then switch the pump to local time.

Your pump will not do this for you. Most insulin pumps do not adjust their own clocks, so this is one manual step at the gate or the hotel, and it matters: get the clock wrong and every basal rate fires at the wrong hour.

Meal insulin does not change at all. Bolus when you eat, wherever you are. The one pump-specific packing note: bring syringes and a vial as backup, and keep your basal rates written down somewhere that is not the pump.

If you take multiple daily injections

Multiple daily injections means two kinds of insulin: a long-acting background insulin once or twice a day, plus fast-acting meal insulin with food. They follow different rules across time zones.

Meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch. Take it when you eat, no matter what the clock says or what time zone you are flying over. Breakfast at 3 a.m. body time still gets its bolus.

Long-acting insulin follows home time on travel day. Keep your watch or phone on home time while you fly and take your once-daily long-acting dose at its usual time. When you arrive, switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone, for example bedtime. If you take background insulin twice a day, keep the two doses roughly 12 hours apart while traveling.

East vs west: what actually changes

Flying east, the shorter day. The gap between your long-acting doses shrinks, which means the previous dose can still be working when the next one is due. Do not guess this adjustment yourself. It is the exact question to bring your care team before the trip.

Flying west, the longer day. The gap stretches past 24 hours, and you may need coverage for the extra hours. Same rule: your care team decides how, not a blog post.

Small shifts are the easy case. A move of three time zones or fewer barely disturbs the rhythm: once you land, take your long-acting insulin at its usual clock time in the new time zone and move on. The careful planning earns its keep on the big jumps.

If you take diabetes tablets

Tablets are the least fussy part of time zone travel. You are unlikely to have problems at all. Occasionally you might take an extra tablet to cover a longer day westbound, or skip one on a short day eastbound, but talk that through with your care team well before the trip rather than improvising at 35,000 feet.

The travel-day playbook

  • Keep your low alarm loud. Travel days are not the day for a silent sensor. Keep your CGM (continuous glucose monitor) charged and your phone on a charger.
  • Keep one clock on home time. Until you land, home time is what your long-acting insulin runs on.
  • Eat when you are hungry and bolus for it. Do not skip meal insulin because the hour looks wrong.
  • Pack 20% extra supplies. Travel is unpredictable, and extra supplies turn a stressful situation around. Prescription copies live in your phone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I take my long-acting insulin at home time or local time when I travel?

During the travel day, keep your watch on home time and take your once-daily long-acting insulin at its usual time. When you arrive, switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone, for example bedtime. Confirm the plan with your care team before you fly.

Do I need to change my insulin pump's clock when I cross time zones?

Yes, and you do it manually. When you land, change your pump's clock to local time so your basal rates run on the new schedule. Most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, and if the clock is wrong every basal rate fires at the wrong hour.

What changes when I fly east versus west with diabetes?

Flying east shortens your day; flying west stretches it. That changes the gap between long-acting insulin doses, which is why the adjustment differs by direction. Meal insulin is simpler: take it when you eat, no matter what the clock says.

Will a time zone planner tell me how much insulin to take?

No. A time zone planner adjusts the timing of your doses for the new time zone, never the amounts. Dose changes are a conversation with your care team, not something a tool or a blog post should decide.
