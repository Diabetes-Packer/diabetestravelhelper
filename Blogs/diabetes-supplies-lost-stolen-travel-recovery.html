Diabetes Supplies Lost or Stolen on a Trip: The Recovery Playbook (2026)

Nobody wants to need this page. But if your diabetes bag disappears on a trip, stolen from a rental car, forgotten at a hotel, or swallowed by an airline's baggage system, the next few hours decide how the rest of the trip goes. Here is what to do in the first fifteen minutes, the exact order to make the recovery calls, and the packing habits that make this scenario survivable.

Updated October 2026  ·  8 min read

This is general guidance, not medical advice. Every step below ends at the same place: a pharmacist, your care team, or an emergency department making the actual call. Your care team owns your plan.

The first fifteen minutes

1. Check what is on you. An on-body pump and an active sensor buy real time. A continuous glucose monitor (CGM), the small sensor that reads your glucose every few minutes, keeps you informed even when the bag is gone. Sugar in a pocket matters more right now than anything in the lost bag, because a low you cannot treat is the most urgent problem you can have.

2. Retrace and report, in that order. Call the hotel front desk or walk back to where you last had the bag. If the airline has it, file the lost-baggage report at their desk before you leave the airport and keep the reference number. If it was stolen, file a police report. You may need that report number for your travel insurance later.

3. Call your travel companion. If anyone traveling with you carried a backup pouch, this is the moment that decision pays off. Regroup and take stock together before you start the call order below.

The recovery call order

Work these in order. Each one is a complete answer on its own; stop at the first one that solves your problem.

Step 1: Your home pharmacy, for a transfer. If your prescription is active and has refills, your home pharmacy can transfer it to another pharmacy near you. Diabetes pharmacists point travelers at large chains for this: walk into the nearest CVS or Walgreens, show ID, and ask them to pull the transfer. Have photos of your prescription labels ready on your phone; it speeds everything up. This is the fastest path for most US travelers, and it works for insulin, test strips, and most supplies.

Step 2: Your care team's on-call line. No active prescription, or no refills left? Call your diabetes provider and ask for whoever is on call after hours. They can send a prescription to a pharmacy near you, and that works even if you are in another state. Keep your medication list and your usual doses in your phone so you can read them off exactly.

Step 3: A telehealth visit. If you cannot reach your own team, a telehealth visit with a provider can get you a short-term supply sent to a local pharmacy. Same preparation: medication names, doses, and your insurance card photo.

Step 4: Your device manufacturer. For a lost pump, lost pods, or dead sensors, call the manufacturer's support line directly and ask about emergency replacement shipping. Pump companies sometimes send pump supplies overnight for emergencies, and CGM makers routinely replace failed sensors; ask what they can do for lost supplies while you wait. If you wear an insulin pump, ask your care team now, before your next trip, about a backup plan of pens or vials plus long-acting insulin for pump failures. That backup prescription is worth having on file before you ever need it.

Step 5: The pharmacy shelf, no prescription needed. Two kinds of backup in a US pharmacy need no prescription at all. The older human insulins, NPH and Regular (sold as Humulin R and N, and Novolin R and N), are available over the counter. The FDA has said this availability exists so people can get insulin quickly in urgent situations without delays. A basic blood glucose meter and test strips are sold without a prescription too. And there are now over-the-counter CGMs: Dexcom Stelo, FDA-cleared in March 2024, and Abbott Lingo need no prescription. Three cautions, all important. First, these are bridge options, not your regimen: the older insulins work differently from modern rapid-acting and long-acting insulins, so ask the pharmacist and your care team for the bridging plan instead of guessing. Second, the over-the-counter CGMs are not designed to alert for low blood sugar the way prescription CGMs do, so they are a temporary window into your numbers, not a replacement sensor. Third, they are labeled for people who do not use insulin, which is one more reason to treat them as a bridge while the real replacement is on its way.

Step 6: The emergency room. If nothing else works, go. It is the last resort, but it is a real one. An emergency department can stabilize you and write what you need to get through the rest of the trip. Do not let embarrassment or cost worry keep you from this step if you are running out of insulin with no replacement lined up.

If this happens abroad

The call order changes slightly outside the US. Start at a local pharmacy with photos of your prescription labels and the generic name of each insulin you use. The generic name, also called the INN (International Nonproprietary Name), is the standardized name pharmacists worldwide recognize, even when the brand name on your box means nothing to them.

Then the concentration check, which matters more than anything else on this page. In the United States nearly all insulin is U-100, meaning 100 units per milliliter. Some countries still use U-40, which is 40 units per milliliter. Using the wrong concentration with the wrong syringe causes a serious dosing error, so confirm the concentration with the pharmacist and make sure your syringes match. The international travel guide covers this trap, brand-name differences, and which documents to carry, in full.

Three more numbers worth saving before you fly: your travel insurance emergency line (some policies cover medication replacement), the local emergency number at your destination, and the IAMAT directory of English-speaking doctors. The international guide has all three and a pre-travel checklist.

What not to do

Do not ration. Do not skip doses or shrink them to stretch what is left. Too little insulin can lead to diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a dangerous buildup of acids in the blood that needs emergency care. Do not reduce or skip insulin doses without speaking with a clinician. The answer to a supply emergency is a bridge supply, not a smaller dose.

Do not borrow and dose. Do not take another traveler's insulin and dose it as if it were your own. Insulin types, concentrations, and dosing are individual. Anything you did not pack yourself is a question for the pharmacist or your care team, not a guess.

Do not wait until morning. Pharmacies close, on-call lines get slower, and a problem at 8pm is easier than the same problem at 2am. Start the call order the moment you know the bag is gone.

The companion rule. For customs inspection, keep your diabetes supplies together in one dedicated bag, which is faster to inspect and easier to explain. But if you travel with a companion, hand them a sealed backup pouch with a few days of essentials. One bag lost is then not all supplies lost. The pouch never leaves their person until you regroup.

Make this scenario survivable: the prevention list

Every item on this list is cheap compared to one emergency pharmacy run in a strange city.

  • Pack 20% more than the trip math says. That is the standing rule on this site, and lost-bag scenarios are exactly why it exists. Ask your provider to write prescriptions with that buffer built in, so the extra is actually yours to pack.
  • Photograph everything. Every prescription label, your insurance card, your doctor's letter. Keep the photos on your phone and know your home pharmacy's phone number by heart, or saved in your contacts.
  • Insulin flies in the carry-on, never checked. Cargo holds can freeze insulin and render it useless, and checked bags are the ones that go missing. This single habit prevents most versions of this emergency. The carry-on guide has the full setup.
  • Keep sugar in your pocket, not just in the bag. Sugar packets are the lightest low treatment there is: one packet for a reading in the 60s, two below 60. A pocket stash survives any lost bag.
  • Wear medical ID. If a low hits while you are separated from your supplies and your words, a bracelet speaks for you.
  • Save your travel insurance emergency line before you leave. Some policies cover replacing lost medication. Know whether yours does before you need it. The travel insurance guide explains what to look for.
  • Build a go bag. A small bag with a few days of medications, fast sugar, glucagon, a meter with strips, and syringes, packed and ready, turns the next trip's packing from a scramble into a grab. Check its expiration dates every few months.

The recovery checklist

If the bag is gone, work this list top to bottom:

Lost or Stolen Diabetes Supplies: The Recovery Checklist

  • Check what is on you: on-body pump, active CGM sensor, sugar in your pocket
  • Retrace your steps; call the hotel or walk back to where you last had the bag
  • File the airline's lost-baggage report before leaving the airport, or a police report if stolen; keep the reference number
  • Call your travel companion and regroup if they carried a backup pouch
  • Call your home pharmacy: transfer the active prescription to the nearest large-chain pharmacy
  • No active prescription: call your care team's on-call line for a new prescription sent nearby
  • Still stuck: book a telehealth visit for a short-term supply
  • Call your pump or CGM manufacturer and ask about emergency replacement shipping
  • US pharmacy bridges with no prescription: NPH or Regular insulin, a meter with strips, or an over-the-counter CGM as a temporary window

  • Do not skip or shrink doses to stretch supplies; that trades a solvable problem for DKA risk

  • Abroad: local pharmacy with generic (INN) names and prescription photos; confirm U-100 vs U-40 with the pharmacist
  • After the trip: photograph all labels, pack 20% extra, and build the go bag before the next one

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I do first if my diabetes bag is lost or stolen on a trip?

First, check what is on you: an on-body pump, an active sensor, and sugar in your pocket buy time. Then retrace and report: file the airline's lost-baggage report before leaving the airport, or a police report if it was stolen. Then start the recovery call order: your home pharmacy for a transfer, your care team's on-call line, a telehealth visit, and your device manufacturer.

Can a pharmacy replace my insulin if I lose it while traveling in the US?

Usually yes. If your prescription is active and has refills, your home pharmacy can transfer it to a pharmacy near you; a large chain like CVS or Walgreens is the easiest target. If you have no active prescription, your diabetes provider's on-call clinician can send a new one to a nearby pharmacy, and a telehealth visit can get you a short-term supply.

Can I buy insulin without a prescription in an emergency?

In the US, the older human insulins, NPH and Regular (Humulin R and N, Novolin R and N), are sold over the counter without a prescription. The FDA has said this availability exists so people can get insulin quickly in urgent situations. They work differently from modern insulins, so ask the pharmacist and your care team for a bridging plan instead of guessing.

What if I lose my pump supplies or CGM sensors mid-trip?

Call your device manufacturer's support line and ask about emergency replacement shipping; pump companies sometimes send supplies overnight for emergencies. For glucose monitoring, a basic meter and strips need no prescription, and over-the-counter CGMs like Dexcom Stelo and Abbott Lingo need no prescription either, though they are not designed to alert for low blood sugar the way prescription CGMs do.

Should I skip insulin doses to make my supply last?

No. Do not reduce or skip insulin doses without speaking with a clinician. Too little insulin can lead to diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a dangerous buildup of acids in the blood that needs emergency care. The answer to a supply emergency is a bridge supply, not a smaller dose.

What if my supplies are lost while I am abroad?

Start at a local pharmacy with photos of your prescription labels and the generic (INN) name of each insulin. In the US nearly all insulin is U-100, but some countries use U-40, so confirm the concentration with the pharmacist. Your travel insurance emergency line, the IAMAT directory of English-speaking doctors, and the local emergency number are the other three numbers worth having before you fly.
