Traveling with Diabetes in Italy: The Pharmacy, the Food, and the Trip (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Summer is the season this guide is written for: long days in Rome and Florence, the Amalfi Coast, and Sicily, with gelato after dinner and hours of walking between sights. It is also the fourth country on this site to get its own destination guide, after Japan, Mexico, and Spain, and that is not an accident. Italy is one of the few places where the diabetes rules are genuinely different from the ones you already know: a 30-day supply line at the border, a certificate for controlled medicines, a pharmacy system that needs an Italian prescription, and a food label system that lists the carbs in grams per 100 grams. None of it is hard. All of it is specific. This guide covers the import rules, the pharmacy, and the trip itself, in that order, so you can sort the paperwork at home and enjoy the trip once you land.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. I have not traveled to Italy with diabetes. This guide is not a trip report, and it does not pretend to be one. It is built from the published guidance of Italy's Health Ministry, the Ministero della Salute, the ministry that regulates medicine in Italy, the Agenzia delle Dogane e dei Monopoli, the agency that runs customs in Italy, the Agenzia Italiana del Farmaco (AIFA), the agency that regulates medicines in Italy, the United States Department of State, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the European Medicines Agency (EMA), the European Union's food information law, the United Kingdom government guidance for British nationals hospitalised in Italy, and the World Meteorological Organization (WMO). Where a rule comes from one of those sources, this guide says so. Where something is my own travel habit, it is labeled as mine, and it is kept separate from the official rules. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own travel plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not going, tell your care team.

1. The import rule in one paragraph, then the details

Italy does not run a certificate system like Japan's. There is no import form to file before you fly, and no office that stamps your insulin. The paperwork is a prescription and a letter, and the rule is that the amount must match the trip. Italy's customs agency, the Agenzia delle Dogane e dei Monopoli, the agency that runs customs in Italy, sets the traveler rule in its Traveller's Customs Charter: medicines can be brought in with the traveler if the quantity fits the length of the stay and there is a regular medical prescription, at least in English, that preferably shows the dosage, to show to the health and customs officers if they ask. Our customs and border rules guide covers the general version of this system for any country, and our international travel guide covers the wide version of traveling abroad with diabetes. Italy is where the general version gets specific.

The number that matters before anything else is 30 days. The United States Department of State says foreign visitors may bring up to a 30-day supply of personal medicine, for themselves only, with a copy of the prescription carried with it. Do not bring medicine prescribed to someone else, and do not pack a supply far beyond the trip. Carry the prescription too, and keep every medicine in its original pharmacy container with the label on it, so an officer can match the medicine to the paper without a conversation. The CDC makes the same point on its Italy page: bring copies of your prescriptions, and call Italy's embassy to check that each prescription is legal to bring with you, because some prescription drugs may be illegal in other countries. If the trip runs longer than the supply you can carry, our long-stay abroad playbook covers the planning for a trip that outlasts the supply in your bag.

Two more rules from the same State Department page. First, do not ship medicine to Italy. The import of medicines by courier or by mail is strictly regulated by Italian customs, and a mailed shipment clears customs only with a statement signed by a physician licensed in Italy. Carry it with you. Second, no visa is needed for a stay under 90 days, and no vaccine is required to enter Italy. Your passport must be valid for at least three months beyond your planned departure from the Schengen area, and the State Department recommends six months, with two blank pages for entry stamps. Our travel letter guide covers what the letter should say, and our pre-trip appointment guide covers the full appointment, including asking for generic-name prescriptions before you leave. A prescription that lists the generic name is the one that travels. A brand name that only exists in your home country does not.

2. Insulin at the border: pens, syringes, and what to carry

Insulin travels the same way it travels everywhere: in the bag that stays with you, in its original box with the pharmacy label on it. Syringes have one packing rule of their own. Keep the syringes with the injectable medicine they accompany, and make sure the doctor's letter mentions them. Do not plan to bring a loose syringe on its own. Pack the syringes with the insulin they belong to, and list them in the letter, so the letter and the bag agree with each other.

Two habits from my own travel routine, kept separate from the border rules. Everything diabetes-related rides in my carry-on, never in checked luggage, and I pack 20 percent extra of everything: pods, sensors, insulin, test strips, lancets, ketone strips, and the low kit. Our packing list does the counting for the trip length. The 20 percent buffer covers the pod that fails on a hot afternoon in Rome and the sensor that peels off in the humidity on the Amalfi Coast. Bring backup insulin for injections in case a pod fails mid-trip, and keep your insulin plan written down somewhere that is not the pump. The flight there follows the same airport rules as any trip, covered in our TSA rules guide for the United States departure and our airport security abroad guide for the arrival side.

3. What needs a certificate, and what is not sold here

Some medicines need a certificate before you travel, and no letter fixes a missing one. Italy regulates medicines under its narcotics law, Presidential Decree 309/1990, and the traveler rule comes from a Health Ministry decree dated 16 November 2007. It covers medicines containing substances under the two international drug conventions: in practice, opioid painkillers, benzodiazepines and sleeping pills such as zolpidem, and stimulants. If you carry more than one package of such a medicine, the Health Ministry requires a certificate or medical prescription from your home country's competent health authority, stating your personal details, the medicine's trade name, the name and total quantity of the active substance, and the number of doses. The certificate is valid for at most 30 days. If you carry only one package of the medicine, a valid prescription alone covers you. Check every medicine by its active ingredient, not its brand name, before you pack. The active ingredient is what the law sees. The brand name is not.

Adderall is the one that catches travelers by surprise. Adderall, the mixed amphetamine salts used for ADHD, is not sold in Italy. No amphetamine product is authorized there, so an Italian pharmacy cannot supply it. Methylphenidate is sold in Italy as Ritalin and Concerta, among others, under a monitored prescription plan. If you take Adderall, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about switching to a medicine that is sold in Italy, and arrange the certificate for the medicine you carry. The answer cannot be found at the border.

Cannabis is a separate problem. Cannabis is not legal in Italy. Possession of a small amount for personal use is decriminalized, which means it is handled as an administrative matter rather than a crime, and the Prefect can suspend a driving licence or a passport. Selling it or carrying it across the border is a criminal matter. Since April 2025, Italy also bans the sale, transport and delivery of hemp flowers, the product sold as cannabis light. Do not bring cannabis, THC, or CBD products into Italy. Leave them at home.

4. The pharmacy: insulin needs a prescription, and yours from home does not work here

Italy's pharmacy system is regulated by AIFA, the Agenzia Italiana del Farmaco, the agency that does the job the Food and Drug Administration does in the United States. The pharmacy on the corner is called a farmacia, and you find it by the green cross, the croce verde, in the window. Most pharmacies keep split hours, about 9:00 to 1:00 and 4:00 to 7:00 Monday to Saturday, and a rota of on-duty pharmacies, called farmacie di turno, covers nights, Sundays, and public holidays. A closed pharmacy posts the address of the nearest one on duty on its door, so you are never far from an open one. The system works well once you understand it, and the first thing to understand is that it is the opposite of Mexico's in the way that matters most: insulin is not sold over the counter. It needs a prescription. The same rule holds in Spain, our third country guide, where insulin also needs a local prescription.

So the pharmacy visit, if you need one, has a fixed sequence. First, see a doctor in Italy. A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in Italy: an Italian pharmacy does not work from American paper. There is one exception, and it matters if you travel from elsewhere in Europe. A prescription written by a doctor in one European Union (EU) country is valid in all other EU countries, so a prescription from France or Germany, for example, can be filled at an Italian farmacia, although the medicine may have another name or may not be sold in Italy at all. In practice, Italian pharmacies do not always accept a foreign prescription, especially if it is not in Italian, so do not count on it. The reliable route is an Italian doctor. A private doctor writes a private prescription, called a ricetta bianca, and you pay the full price of the medicine. Bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product. Tell the doctor the generic name of your insulin, not just the brand: insulin aspart, insulin lispro, insulin glargine, insulin degludec. The pharmacist and the doctor both work from the generic name, and the generic name is what survives the brand change at the border. NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid almost everywhere else, and both are insulin aspart. Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine. Our finding insulin abroad playbook covers the name game in full, including the concentration check that matters more than the brand name.

Two more things to know before you walk in. First, the familiar insulins are sold in Italy. Lantus, insulin glargine, holds a marketing authorization valid throughout the European Union, granted by the European Commission on 9 June 2000, and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) says it can only be obtained with a prescription. NovoRapid, insulin aspart, was authorized in the European Union on 7 September 1999, and Tresiba, insulin degludec, holds European Commission authorizations covering all European Union member states from January 2013. Most insulin pens in Italy use the same U-100 standard, 100 units in each milliliter, so a pen dial counts the same way it does at home. Tresiba is the exception to watch for: it also comes in a pen at 200 units per milliliter, so check the concentration printed on the box before you use it, because a syringe is calibrated for one concentration only. Second, visitors pay. Medical care is not free for visitors in Italy, and United States Medicare and Medicaid do not cover medical expenses abroad. Keep every receipt for your travel insurance claim, and check your policy before the trip so the clinic visit is not the first time you read it. Our travel insurance guide covers what to declare and what to look for.

5. Your devices in Italy: the app, the sensors, and the sun

Start with the phone, because it holds the one trap that ruins more international trips than any other. The Dexcom G7 app is region-locked at install: Dexcom's own user guide states the app must be installed in your country of residence, and the app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when you first install it. The check happens at install time. An app that is already installed and working keeps working abroad, and travelers report normal operation on international trips as long as nobody deletes the app mid-trip. So install the G7 app and update it to the latest version before you leave, turn off auto-updates before the trip so nothing about your diabetes setup changes mid-trip without your say-so, and do not delete the app while you are in Italy. A deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country, and that is a long trip without CGM data. Our G7 app region-lock guide covers the full prep list, and nothing in it is specific to Italy: the same rule applies everywhere you land.

The receiver is the backup that makes the app problem a non-issue. Dexcom's own international travel guidance says that if you have a receiver, it makes a great backup to your phone app. The receiver has no app store, no region check, and no install step. It pairs with your sensor and shows readings anywhere on earth. Pack it with its USB cable and charger, plus a plug adapter for Italy. Our devices charging guide covers plugs and power on the road. If you do not already own a receiver, ask Dexcom about getting one before a big international trip. Bring the fingerstick meter too, as the final fallback, and keep it in your day bag rather than buried in your hotel room.

Buying replacement sensors in Italy is a pharmacy problem, not a shop problem. The G7 is sold in Italy: the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) lists Italy among the countries where the G7 is distributed, and Dexcom established a direct presence in Italy in March 2025 with its CGM platform built on the G7 sensor. The Omnipod 5 launched in Italy in January 2025, compatible in Italy with the FreeStyle Libre 2 Plus and the Dexcom G6 sensors, and Insulet added Dexcom G7 integration in Italy later in 2025. A replacement sensor bought in Italy still means a foreign pharmacy, a local prescription, and regional compatibility questions you do not want to solve on vacation. Pack more sensors than the trip math says you need, with the spares in your carry-on, and do not plan to shop. The same applies to pump supplies. Carry spare Pods and a backup injection plan with syringes and a vial, and keep your basal rates written down somewhere that is not the pump. Our pod failure backup plan covers the sequence when a Pod fails away from home.

6. Time zones: one clock, and the nine-hour gap

Italy keeps one clock. The whole country, including Sicily and Sardinia, uses Central European Time (CET), which is 1 hour ahead of Coordinated Universal Time, written as UTC+1, in winter, and Central European Summer Time (CEST), UTC+2, in summer. The clocks move on the same European dates: forward on the last Sunday in March and back on the last Sunday in October. The gap between Italy and home changes only because home moves its clocks, not because Italy does. From the United States West Coast, Rome is 9 hours ahead as the standard gap, and from the United States East Coast, Rome is 6 hours ahead as the standard gap. From London, Rome is 1 hour ahead. The gap narrows by one hour for a few weeks each spring and autumn, when the United States and Europe change their clocks on different Sundays, so check the current gap in the week you fly. Either way, it is a large enough jump that the insulin timing needs a plan before the flight, not after landing.

The plan follows the rule in our time zone insulin guide. If you use a pump, change the pump clock to local time when you land, so your basal rates, the background insulin the pump drips all day, run on local time. Most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, so this is one manual step at the gate or the hotel. If you take multiple daily injections, keep your long-acting insulin on home time on the travel day, then switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone when you arrive, for example bedtime. Meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch: take it when you eat, wherever you are. The guide covers the eastbound and westbound cases in full, and the timing shift for an Italy trip is large enough that the careful planning earns its keep. Run your route through our time zone planner before you fly, and confirm the plan with your care team. The planner adjusts timing only, never dose amounts. Dose changes are a conversation with your care team, not something a tool or a blog post should decide.

7. Eating in Italy: the label that lists the carbs

Italy is one of the easiest countries in the world to count carbs in, once you know where to look, and one of the hardest if you do not. The split is between packaged food and restaurant food, and the supermarket sits squarely on the easy side. Under the European Union's food information law, Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, the nutrition declaration is mandatory on pre-packed food, and it must list energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, and salt. The carbohydrate number is in grams, stated per 100 grams or per 100 milliliters, and the carbohydrate line reads carboidrati, with sugars listed under it. The label is in Italian, so learn the words for the nutrition panel, or point your phone's camera at it with a translation app. Packaged food from a supermarket, a bakery, or a train station kiosk all carries the same table, which makes a sandwich, a yogurt, or a pack of crackers a known quantity instead of a guess. This guide gives no carb counts for specific foods, because the number on the package in front of you is the one that counts, and it is printed right there.

Restaurant food has no label, so it works the way eating out works everywhere. Ask what is in the dish, estimate the carbohydrate from the pasta, the bread, or the potatoes, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. I pre-bolus in my usual way before meals. That habit travels with me, and Italy does not change it. Italian meals often run in courses, and pasta usually comes as a first course, called the primo, with a second course after it, so the pasta is rarely the whole meal. A plate of pasta is mostly the pasta, and a plate of gelato scooped at the counter has no label at all, so it works like any restaurant food: estimate, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. Our eating out playbook covers the ordering and estimating routine in full, and our pre-bolus guide covers the timing habit. For lows, keep sugar packets in your day bag. I treat lows with sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, and that is the low kit that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day in any country. Do not let the low kit be something you buy after you need it. Pack it before you leave the hotel.

Water is the other half of eating safely in Italy, and the news is good. Tap water in Italy is safe to drink, and it is treated and tested under national rules based on the European Union's drinking water rules. Rome's street fountains, called nasoni, run with the same drinking water, and they are free to use. Do not drink from a tap or fountain marked acqua non potabile, which means not drinking water, and if a local notice says otherwise, follow it. A stomach bug is a diabetes problem, not just a comfort problem, because vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the plan for that day.

Vaccines are a question for the care-team appointment, not for the airport. No vaccine is required to enter Italy, and the appointment is the place to check that routine vaccines are up to date. Our travel vaccines playbook covers the appointment in full.

8. Summer in Italy: heat, walking, and the hotel fridge

Summer is the season this guide is written for, and heat and walking are the two things to plan around. Rome's July afternoons average a high of 87F, with nights near 64F, on the World Meteorological Organization normals for Rome Fiumicino airport, and heatwaves run hotter than the average. The CDC's Italy page says the same thing in plainer terms: heat-related illness can be deadly, so eat and drink regularly, wear loose and lightweight clothing, and limit physical activity in the heat of the day. If you are outside for many hours in the heat, eat salty snacks and drink water to stay hydrated.

All-day walking is the other half of the summer plan, and it pulls glucose down. A day that starts at the Colosseum, crosses Rome on foot, and ends with dinner across town can mean hours on foot without trying. Activity lowers glucose, so check more often than usual on a walking day, carry sugar packets in your pocket rather than in the bag you left at the hotel, and keep a snack in the day bag for the walk back. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. The low kit is the same one that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day: sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60.

Insulin follows the same heat rule it follows everywhere. Unopened insulin belongs in the fridge at 36 to 46F, and in-use insulin is fine at room temperature below 77F. A Rome afternoon in July sits well above 77F, so in-use insulin needs its cooling wallet from the moment you leave the hotel. Our insulin cooling guide covers the methods, and our hotel insulin storage guide covers the minibar trap in detail: hotel minibar fridges can freeze items at the back, so keep unopened insulin away from the cooling element and check that it has not frozen. Insulin freezes at 32F, and frozen insulin must be discarded.

Then there is the sun, which is the one place where your devices meet a real limit on an Italy trip. Dexcom publishes exact temperature numbers for the G7. The sensor's operating range is 50F to 107.6F, and its storage range is 36F to 86F. A worn sensor sits against warm skin, so a normal day does not push it out of range, but a long day in direct sun can push a spare past its storage ceiling. Keep sun exposure short where you can, and check the readings against your fingerstick meter after a long hot day. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. Heat loosens adhesive too. Press the sensor and pod edges down firmly after swimming, and pack extra overpatches. Our G7 heat and cold guide has the full temperature table, and our adhesion playbook covers keeping a sensor and a pump stuck through holiday trips.

If the trip is a winter trip instead, the Dolomites and the Alps bring the cold routine: a worn sensor sits against warm skin under your clothes, so normal winter days do not push it out of its operating range, but spare sensors must be stored between 36F and 86F, so do not leave them in a freezing car overnight. Our skiing playbook covers the cold and altitude routine for a ski trip, and the same habits apply to a cold morning walking around Rome or Florence.

9. If something goes wrong: numbers, clinics, and the insurance call

Save two numbers in your phone before you land. Dial 112 for ambulance, fire, and police. It is the single European emergency number, it works nationwide, and it is free from any phone. Dial 118 if you need an ambulance, the medical emergency line. If you need a doctor outside normal hours but it is not an emergency, call 116117 for the guardia medica, the out-of-hours doctor service, which covers nights, weekends, and public holidays. If you need the United States Embassy, call +39-06-4674-1 in Rome, or the Consulate General in Milan at +39-02-290-351. If you travel from the United Kingdom, bring your Global Health Insurance Card (GHIC) or European Health Insurance Card (EHIC): either card covers state-provided healthcare in Italy, but you may still pay a co-payment, called a ticket, for out-patient follow-up checks, specialist referrals, diagnostic tests, and prescription medicines, and a private hospital, called a clinica, may not accept the card unless it has an agreement with the public health authority. Neither card is a replacement for travel insurance, and neither card covers getting flown home. If you travel from the United States, there is no equivalent card: medical care in Italy is not free for visitors, and United States Medicare and Medicaid do not cover care abroad. Check your policy before the trip, so the call is not the first time you read it.

If you develop symptoms of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous complication where the blood turns acidic when insulin runs too low, get urgent help right away. Vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation, and that is a hospital visit, not a wait-and-see day. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the ketone checks and the plan for that day. Carry glucagon in your day bag, and make sure your travel companion knows where it is and how to use it. Our glucagon emergency kit guide covers what is in the kit and how to keep it travel-ready. If the trip goes wrong in a bigger way, a bag lost or stolen, our lost or stolen supplies playbook covers the exact call order, and the travel insurance emergency line is the call that arranges care, including a local doctor visit. If the trip involves driving, get an International Driving Permit (IDP) before you leave: the CDC says to get an IDP and to carry the IDP and a United States-issued driver's license at all times. Our driving abroad guide covers licenses, rental cars, and the rules of the road.

  • Book the care-team appointment first: confirm your insulin plan, your travel timing, and your backup plan before you book the trip

  • Check every medicine by its active ingredient: controlled medicines need a Health Ministry certificate if you carry more than one package, and Adderall is not sold in Italy
  • Pack 20% extra supplies; our packing list does the counting; keep everything in your carry-on, never in checked luggage
  • Install and update the Dexcom G7 app before you leave; do not delete it while you are abroad; pack the receiver, its cable, and a plug adapter for Italy
  • Carry spare Pods and sensors, extra overpatches, and a backup injection plan with syringes and a vial, with your basal rates written down somewhere that is not the pump
  • Run your route through the time zone insulin guide before you fly: Italy is CET in winter and CEST in summer, 9 hours ahead of the United States West Coast and 6 hours ahead of the East Coast as the standard gap, so change the pump clock to local time when you land
  • Pack a FRIO cooling wallet or an insulated case for in-use insulin on warm days; unopened insulin belongs in the fridge at 36 to 46F; Rome July afternoons average 87F, so keep spares cool and carry sugar packets for walking days

  • Check that your travel insurance covers the trip and keep the emergency number where you can find it; keep every receipt from any clinic or pharmacy visit

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I buy insulin over the counter in Italy?

A: No. Insulin needs a prescription in Italy. Lantus, insulin glargine, holds a marketing authorization valid throughout the European Union, and the European Medicines Agency says it can only be obtained with a prescription. See a doctor in Italy for an Italian prescription, called a ricetta bianca, and bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product.

Q: Will Italian customs accept my syringes and my prescription from home?

A: Yes, for your own personal use. Italy's customs charter lets you bring in medicine in a quantity that fits the length of your stay, with a regular medical prescription, at least in English, that preferably shows the dosage. The United States Department of State puts the figure at up to a 30-day supply, for yourself only, with a copy of the prescription carried with it. Keep the syringes with the insulin they accompany, and list them in your doctor's letter, so the letter and the bag agree with each other.

Q: Can an Italian pharmacy fill my prescription from home?

A: A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in Italy: an Italian pharmacy does not work from American paper. A prescription written by a doctor in another European Union country is valid in Italy, although the medicine may have another name or may not be sold there, and in practice Italian pharmacies do not always accept a foreign prescription. If you run out of insulin in Italy, see a doctor in Italy, show your prescription and doctor's letter so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the Italian prescription, called a ricetta bianca, to a farmacia.

Q: What medicines need a certificate, and what about Adderall?

A: Medicines that contain a narcotic or psychotropic substance need a certificate from your home country's competent health authority if you carry more than one package, and the certificate is valid for at most 30 days. That covers opioid painkillers, benzodiazepines and sleeping pills such as zolpidem, and stimulants. Adderall is not sold in Italy: no amphetamine product is authorized there, so an Italian pharmacy cannot supply it. Methylphenidate is sold in Italy as Ritalin and Concerta. If you take Adderall, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about switching to a medicine that is sold in Italy. Cannabis is not legal in Italy, so do not bring cannabis, THC, or CBD products.

Q: Will my Dexcom G7 app work in Italy?

A: Yes, if it was already installed and set up at home. The Dexcom G7 app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when it is first installed, so install and update it before you leave and do not delete it while you are abroad: a deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country. Bring the Dexcom receiver as backup. It has no app store and no install check, and it pairs with your sensor anywhere. The G7 is sold in Italy, and the Omnipod 5 launched there in January 2025.

Q: What are the emergency numbers in Italy?

A: Dial 112 for ambulance, fire, and police. It is the single European emergency number, it works nationwide, and it is free from any phone. Dial 118 if you need an ambulance. If you need a doctor outside normal hours but it is not an emergency, call 116117 for the guardia medica, the out-of-hours doctor service. If you need the United States Embassy, call +39-06-4674-1 in Rome, or the Consulate General in Milan at +39-02-290-351. If you travel from the United Kingdom, a GHIC or EHIC covers state-provided healthcare, but it is not a replacement for travel insurance.
