Traveling with Diabetes in France: The Pharmacy, the Food, and the Trip (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Summer is the season this guide is written for: long days in Paris and Provence, the French Riviera, and the Loire Valley, with hours of walking between sights and long dinners after dark. It is also the fifth country on this site to get its own destination guide, after Japan, Mexico, Spain, and Italy, and that is not an accident. France is one of the few places where the diabetes rules are genuinely different from the ones you already know: an import rule tied to the treatment on your prescription, a pharmacy system that needs a French prescription, and a food label system that lists the carbs in grams per 100 grams, in French. None of it is hard. All of it is specific. This guide covers the import rules, the pharmacy, and the trip itself, in that order, so you can sort the paperwork at home and enjoy the trip once you land.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. I have not traveled to France with diabetes. This guide is not a trip report, and it does not pretend to be one. It is built from the published guidance of France's customs administration, the Direction Générale des Douanes et Droits Indirects (DGDDI), the agency that runs customs in France, the National Agency for the Safety of Medicines and Health Products (ANSM), the agency that regulates medicines in France, the United States Department of State, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the European Medicines Agency (EMA), the European Union's food information law, the United Kingdom government guidance for British nationals in France, Météo-France, the French national weather service, and the French government information portal service-public.gouv.fr. Where a rule comes from one of those sources, this guide says so. Where something is my own travel habit, it is labeled as mine, and it is kept separate from the official rules. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own travel plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not going, tell your care team.

1. The import rule in one paragraph, then the details

France does not run a certificate system like Japan's. There is no import form to file before you fly, and no office that stamps your insulin. The paperwork is a prescription and a letter, and the rule is that the amount must match the treatment. France's customs administration, the Direction Générale des Douanes et Droits Indirects (DGDDI), the agency that runs customs in France, sets the traveler rule on its page for travelers carrying medicines: for ordinary medicine, the quantity must not exceed personal use, which means the treatment duration written on your prescription, or three months of treatment if you carry no prescription. If the treatment runs longer than three months, you must show the doctor's prescription to customs. Our customs and border rules guide covers the general version of this system for any country, and our international travel guide covers the wide version of traveling abroad with diabetes. France is where the general version gets specific.

A doctor's letter is not a legal requirement for ordinary medicine in France, but carry one anyway: it states your name, your diagnosis, and your medicines by their generic name, and it is the document a French doctor will want to see if you need a local prescription. The CDC makes the same point on its France packing list: bring copies of all prescriptions, make sure the prescriptions include generic names, and pack extras of important health supplies in case of travel delays. If the trip runs longer than the supply you can carry, our long-stay abroad playbook covers the planning for a trip that outlasts the supply in your bag.

Two more rules from the State Department page. First, no visa is needed for a stay under 90 days, and no vaccine is required to enter France. Your passport must be valid for at least three months beyond your planned departure from the Schengen area, and the State Department recommends six months, with one blank page for entry stamps. Our travel letter guide covers what the letter should say, and our pre-trip appointment guide covers the full appointment, including asking for generic-name prescriptions before you leave. A prescription that lists the generic name is the one that travels. A brand name that only exists in your home country does not.

2. Insulin at the border: pens, syringes, and what to carry

Insulin travels the same way it travels everywhere: in the bag that stays with you, in its original box with the pharmacy label on it. Syringes have one packing rule of their own. Keep the syringes with the injectable medicine they accompany, and make sure the doctor's letter mentions them. The CDC says needles and syringes for diabetes need a letter from your doctor on letterhead stationery. Do not plan to bring a loose syringe on its own. Pack the syringes with the insulin they belong to, and list them in the letter, so the letter and the bag agree with each other.

Two habits from my own travel routine, kept separate from the border rules. Everything diabetes-related rides in my carry-on, never in checked luggage, and I pack 20 percent extra of everything: pods, sensors, insulin, test strips, lancets, ketone strips, and the low kit. Our packing list does the counting for the trip length. The 20 percent buffer covers the pod that fails on a hot afternoon in Paris and the sensor that peels off in the humidity on the French Riviera. Bring backup insulin for injections in case a pod fails mid-trip, and keep your insulin plan written down somewhere that is not the pump. The flight there follows the same airport rules as any trip, covered in our TSA rules guide for the United States departure and our airport security abroad guide for the arrival side.

3. What needs extra paperwork, and what is not sold here

Some medicines need extra paperwork before you travel, and no letter fixes a missing prescription. France regulates narcotic and psychotropic medicines at the border through its customs administration. The rule for those medicines is plain: you must carry them yourself, the quantity must fit your personal treatment, and the original valid prescription is mandatory. That covers opioid painkillers, benzodiazepines and sleeping pills such as zolpidem, and stimulants. Check every medicine by its active ingredient, not its brand name, before you pack. The active ingredient is what the law sees. The brand name is not. Do not confuse this with the Schengen Article 75 certificate, which covers carrying a controlled medicine between Schengen countries. It is not the document for arriving in France from the United States or the United Kingdom.

Adderall is the one that catches travelers by surprise. Adderall, the mixed amphetamine salts used for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), is not authorized in France. No French pharmacy can supply it, and no French pharmacy can dispense any medicine on a prescription from the United States. Methylphenidate is sold in France as Ritalin and Concerta, under tight controls, prescribed by a specialist on a secure prescription form. If you take Adderall, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about switching to a medicine that is sold in France, and arrange the paperwork for the medicine you carry. The answer cannot be found at the border.

Cannabis is a separate problem. Cannabis is not legal in France. Using it, carrying it, growing it, buying it, or selling it are all criminal offenses. Since September 2020, police can settle a small personal amount with a fixed fine of 200 euros instead of sending the case to court, but that is an enforcement shortcut, not legalization. Medical cannabis exists in France only through a tightly limited pilot program, and no new patients have been admitted since March 2024. Do not bring cannabis or tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) products into France. Leave them at home.

4. The pharmacy: insulin needs a prescription, and yours from home does not work here

France's pharmacy system is regulated by the ANSM, the National Agency for the Safety of Medicines and Health Products, the agency that does the job the Food and Drug Administration does in the United States. The pharmacy on the corner is called a pharmacie, and you find it by the green cross in the window. Most pharmacies keep hours of about 8:30 in the morning to 7:30 in the evening, Monday to Saturday, and in smaller towns some close for lunch. A rota of on-duty pharmacies, called pharmacies de garde, covers nights, Sundays, and public holidays. A closed pharmacy posts the address of the nearest one on duty on its door, and you can also call 3237 to find the one on duty. The system works well once you understand it, and the first thing to understand is that it is the opposite of Mexico's in the way that matters most: insulin is not sold over the counter. It needs a prescription. The same rule holds in Spain and Italy, our third and fourth country guides, where insulin also needs a local prescription.

So the pharmacy visit, if you need one, has a fixed sequence. First, see a doctor in France. A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in France: a French pharmacy does not work from American paper. There is one exception, and it matters if you travel from elsewhere in Europe. A prescription written by a doctor in one European Union (EU) country is valid in all other EU countries, so a prescription from Italy or Germany, for example, can be filled at a French pharmacie, although the medicine may have another name or may not be sold in France at all. In practice, French pharmacies do not always accept a foreign prescription, especially if it is not in French, so do not count on it. The reliable route is a French doctor. A doctor writes a prescription, called an ordonnance, and you pay the full price of the medicine. Bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product. Tell the doctor the generic name of your insulin, not just the brand: insulin aspart, insulin lispro, insulin glargine, insulin degludec. The pharmacist and the doctor both work from the generic name, and the generic name is what survives the brand change at the border. NovoLog in the United States is NovoRapid almost everywhere else, and both are insulin aspart. Humalog is insulin lispro, and Lantus is insulin glargine. Our finding insulin abroad playbook covers the name game in full, including the concentration check that matters more than the brand name.

Two more things to know before you walk in. First, the familiar insulins are sold in France. Lantus, insulin glargine, holds a marketing authorization valid throughout the European Union, granted by the European Commission on 9 June 2000, and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) says it can only be obtained with a prescription. NovoRapid, insulin aspart, was authorized in the European Union on 7 September 1999, and Tresiba, insulin degludec, holds European Commission authorizations covering all European Union member states from January 2013. Most insulin pens in France use the same U-100 standard, 100 units in each milliliter, so a pen dial counts the same way it does at home. Tresiba is the exception to watch for: it also comes in a pen at 200 units per milliliter, so check the concentration printed on the box before you use it, because a syringe is calibrated for one concentration only. Second, visitors pay. Medical care is not free for visitors in France, and United States Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for medical care outside the United States. Keep every receipt for your travel insurance claim, and check your policy before the trip so the clinic visit is not the first time you read it. Our travel insurance guide covers what to declare and what to look for.

5. Your devices in France: the app, the sensors, and the sun

Start with the phone, because it holds the one trap that ruins more international trips than any other. The Dexcom G7 app is region-locked at install: Dexcom's own user guide states the app must be installed in your country of residence, and the app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when you first install it. The check happens at install time. An app that is already installed and working keeps working abroad, and travelers report normal operation on international trips as long as nobody deletes the app mid-trip. So install the G7 app and update it to the latest version before you leave, turn off auto-updates before the trip so nothing about your diabetes setup changes mid-trip without your say-so, and do not delete the app while you are in France. A deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country, and that is a long trip without CGM data. Our G7 app region-lock guide covers the full prep list, and nothing in it is specific to France: the same rule applies everywhere you land.

The receiver is the backup that makes the app problem a non-issue. Dexcom's own international travel guidance says that if you have a receiver, it makes a great backup to your phone app. The receiver has no app store, no region check, and no install step. It pairs with your sensor and shows readings anywhere on earth. Pack it with its USB cable and charger, plus a plug adapter for France. Our devices charging guide covers plugs and power on the road. If you do not already own a receiver, ask Dexcom about getting one before a big international trip. Bring the fingerstick meter too, as the final fallback, and keep it in your day bag rather than buried in your hotel room.

Buying replacement sensors in France is a pharmacy problem, not a shop problem. The G7 is sold in France: Dexcom launched its Dexcom ONE sensor in France in 2023 with national reimbursement, and France added the Omnipod 5 system paired with the Dexcom G7 to its reimbursement list in early 2026. The Omnipod 5 launched in France in June 2024, compatible at launch with the Dexcom G6 sensor, and it received reimbursement from the French National Authority for Health (HAS), the authority that decides what the French health system pays for. A replacement sensor bought in France still means a foreign pharmacy, a local prescription, and regional compatibility questions you do not want to solve on vacation. Pack more sensors than the trip math says you need, with the spares in your carry-on, and do not plan to shop. The same applies to pump supplies. Carry spare Pods and a backup injection plan with syringes and a vial, and keep your basal rates written down somewhere that is not the pump. Our pod failure backup plan covers the sequence when a Pod fails away from home.

6. Time zones: one clock, and the nine-hour gap

Mainland France keeps one clock. Paris, Provence, the Riviera, and the Loire all use Central European Time (CET), which is 1 hour ahead of Coordinated Universal Time, written as UTC+1, in winter, and Central European Summer Time (CEST), UTC+2, in summer. The clocks move on the same European dates: forward on the last Sunday in March and back on the last Sunday in October. In 2026 that means 29 March and 25 October. France's overseas departments and territories run on different clocks, so this section covers mainland France only. The gap between France and home changes only because home moves its clocks, not because France does. From the United States West Coast, Paris is 9 hours ahead, and from the United States East Coast, Paris is 6 hours ahead. From London, Paris is 1 hour ahead. The gap narrows by one hour for a few weeks each spring and autumn, when the United States and Europe change their clocks on different Sundays, so check the current gap in the week you fly. Either way, it is a large enough jump that the insulin timing needs a plan before the flight, not after landing.

The plan follows the rule in our time zone insulin guide. If you use a pump, change the pump clock to local time when you land, so your basal rates, the background insulin the pump drips all day, run on local time. Most pumps do not adjust their own clocks, so this is one manual step at the gate or the hotel. If you take multiple daily injections, keep your long-acting insulin on home time on the travel day, then switch to taking it at your usual clock time in the new time zone when you arrive, for example bedtime. Meal insulin follows your stomach, not your watch: take it when you eat, wherever you are. The guide covers the eastbound and westbound cases in full, and the timing shift for a France trip is large enough that the careful planning earns its keep. Run your route through our time zone planner before you fly, and confirm the plan with your care team. The planner adjusts timing only, never dose amounts. Dose changes are a conversation with your care team, not something a tool or a blog post should decide.

7. Eating in France: the label that lists the carbs

France is one of the easiest countries in the world to count carbs in, once you know where to look, and one of the hardest if you do not. The split is between packaged food and restaurant food, and the supermarket sits squarely on the easy side. Under the European Union's food information law, Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, the nutrition declaration is mandatory on pre-packed food, and it must list energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, and salt. The carbohydrate number is in grams, stated per 100 grams or per 100 milliliters, and the carbohydrate line reads glucides, with sugars listed under it as sucres. The label is in French, so learn the words for the nutrition panel, or point your phone's camera at it with a translation app. Packaged food from a supermarket, a bakery, or a train station kiosk all carries the same table, which makes a sandwich, a yogurt, or a pack of crackers a known quantity instead of a guess. This guide gives no carb counts for specific foods, because the number on the package in front of you is the one that counts, and it is printed right there.

Restaurant food has no label, so it works the way eating out works everywhere. Ask what is in the dish, estimate the carbohydrate from the bread, the potatoes, or the pastry, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. I pre-bolus in my usual way before meals. That habit travels with me, and France does not change it. French meals often run in courses, so the main dish is rarely the whole meal, and the bread on the table counts too: estimate it with the rest of the meal, and pre-bolus in your usual way before eating. Our eating out playbook covers the ordering and estimating routine in full, and our pre-bolus guide covers the timing habit. For lows, keep sugar packets in your day bag. I treat lows with sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60, and that is the low kit that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day in any country. Do not let the low kit be something you buy after you need it. Pack it before you leave the hotel.

Water is the other half of eating safely in France, and the news is good. Tap water in France is safe to drink, and it is treated and tested under national rules based on the European Union's drinking water rules. In Paris, the green Wallace fountains provide free drinking water, and in a restaurant you can ask for une carafe d'eau, a free jug of tap water, instead of a bottle. Do not drink from a tap or fountain marked eau non potable, which means not drinking water, and if a local notice says otherwise, follow it. A stomach bug is a diabetes problem, not just a comfort problem, because vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the plan for that day.

Vaccines are a question for the care-team appointment, not for the airport. No vaccine is required to enter France, and the appointment is the place to check that routine vaccines are up to date. Our travel vaccines playbook covers the appointment in full.

8. Summer in France: heat, walking, and the hotel fridge

Summer is the season this guide is written for, and heat and walking are the two things to plan around. Paris July afternoons average a high of 78F, with nights near 61F, on the Météo-France normals for the Paris-Montsouris station, and heatwaves run hotter than the average. The CDC's heat guidance says the same thing in plainer terms: drink plenty of fluids, wear loose, lightweight, light-colored clothing, plan outdoor activities for the cooler parts of the day, and rest often in the shade. If you are outside for many hours in the heat, eat salty snacks and drink water to stay hydrated.

All-day walking is the other half of the summer plan, and it pulls glucose down. A day that starts at the Eiffel Tower, crosses Paris on foot, and ends with dinner across town can mean hours on foot without trying. Activity lowers glucose, so check more often than usual on a walking day, carry sugar packets in your pocket rather than in the bag you left at the hotel, and keep a snack in the day bag for the walk back. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. The low kit is the same one that rides in my pocket on a normal travel day: sugar packets, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60.

Insulin follows the same heat rule it follows everywhere. Unopened insulin belongs in the fridge at 36 to 46F, and in-use insulin is fine at room temperature below 77F. A Paris afternoon in July can sit above 77F, so in-use insulin needs its cooling wallet from the moment you leave the hotel. Our insulin cooling guide covers the methods, and our hotel insulin storage guide covers the minibar trap in detail: hotel minibar fridges can freeze items at the back, so keep unopened insulin away from the cooling element and check that it has not frozen. Insulin freezes at 32F, and frozen insulin must be discarded.

Then there is the sun, which is the one place where your devices meet a real limit on a France trip. Dexcom publishes exact temperature numbers for the G7. The sensor's operating range is 50F to 107.6F, and its storage range is 36F to 86F. A worn sensor sits against warm skin, so a normal day does not push it out of range, but a long day in direct sun can push a spare past its storage ceiling. Keep sun exposure short where you can, and check the readings against your fingerstick meter after a long hot day. If the numbers do not match how you feel, do not make treatment decisions off them. Heat loosens adhesive too. Press the sensor and pod edges down firmly after swimming, and pack extra overpatches. Our G7 heat and cold guide has the full temperature table, and our adhesion playbook covers keeping a sensor and a pump stuck through holiday trips.

If the trip is a winter trip instead, the French Alps bring the cold routine: a worn sensor sits against warm skin under your clothes, so normal winter days do not push it out of its operating range, but spare sensors must be stored between 36F and 86F, so do not leave them in a freezing car overnight. Our skiing playbook covers the cold and altitude routine for a ski trip, and the same habits apply to a cold morning walking around Paris or Lyon.

9. If something goes wrong: numbers, clinics, and the insurance call

Save these numbers in your phone before you land. Dial 112 for any emergency. It is the single European emergency number, it works nationwide, and it is free from any phone. Dial 15 for SAMU, the Service d'Aide Médicale Urgente, the medical emergency service, 17 for the police, and 18 for the fire brigade. If you are deaf or hard of hearing, text 114. If you need a doctor outside normal hours but it is not an emergency, call 116117 where it operates, or SOS Médecins at 3624, a service that sends a doctor to your hotel or apartment around the clock. If you need the United States Embassy, call +33-1-43-12-22-22 in Paris. If you travel from the United Kingdom, bring your Global Health Insurance Card (GHIC) or European Health Insurance Card (EHIC): either card covers state-provided healthcare in France, but you may still pay upfront and claim the money back, and state healthcare in France is not free, so a co-payment may apply. Neither card is a replacement for travel insurance, and neither card covers getting flown home. If you travel from the United States, there is no equivalent card: medical care in France is not free for visitors, and United States Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for medical care outside the United States. Check your policy before the trip, so the call is not the first time you read it.

If you develop symptoms of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the dangerous complication where the blood turns acidic when insulin runs too low, get urgent help right away. Vomiting with type 1 diabetes is a ketone situation, and that is a hospital visit, not a wait-and-see day. Our sick-day travel playbook covers the ketone checks and the plan for that day. Carry glucagon in your day bag, and make sure your travel companion knows where it is and how to use it. Our glucagon emergency kit guide covers what is in the kit and how to keep it travel-ready. If the trip goes wrong in a bigger way, a bag lost or stolen, our lost or stolen supplies playbook covers the exact call order, and the travel insurance emergency line is the call that arranges care, including a local doctor visit. If the trip involves driving, get an International Driving Permit (IDP) before you leave: a United States license on its own is not enough in France, because the license must be in French or accompanied by an official translation or an IDP, and the IDP is the simple version. Carry the IDP and a United States-issued driver's license at all times. Our driving abroad guide covers licenses, rental cars, and the rules of the road.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I buy insulin over the counter in France?

A: No. Insulin needs a prescription in France. Lantus, insulin glargine, holds a marketing authorization valid throughout the European Union, and the European Medicines Agency says it can only be obtained with a prescription. See a doctor in France for a French prescription, called an ordonnance, and bring your prescription, your doctor's letter, and the empty box or pen of the insulin you use, so the doctor sees the exact product.

Q: Will French customs accept my syringes and my prescription from home?

A: Yes, for your own personal use. French customs lets you bring in medicine in a quantity that fits the treatment duration on your prescription, or up to three months of treatment if you carry no prescription, and you must show the prescription if the treatment runs longer than three months. Keep the syringes with the insulin they accompany, and list them in your doctor's letter, so the letter and the bag agree with each other.

Q: Can a French pharmacy fill my prescription from home?

A: A prescription from the United States cannot be filled in France: a French pharmacy does not work from American paper. A prescription written by a doctor in another European Union country is valid in France, although the medicine may have another name or may not be sold there, and in practice French pharmacies do not always accept a foreign prescription. If you run out of insulin in France, see a doctor in France, show your prescription and doctor's letter so the doctor can match your insulin by its generic name, and take the French prescription, called an ordonnance, to a pharmacie.

Q: What medicines need extra paperwork, and what about Adderall?

A: Medicines that fall under the narcotic or psychotropic rules need the original prescription carried with you, and you must carry the medicine yourself in a quantity that fits your personal treatment. That covers opioid painkillers, benzodiazepines and sleeping pills such as zolpidem, and stimulants. Adderall is not authorized in France, so a French pharmacy cannot supply it. Methylphenidate is sold in France as Ritalin and Concerta, under tight controls. If you take Adderall, talk to your prescriber well before the trip about switching to a medicine that is sold in France. Cannabis is not legal in France, so do not bring cannabis or THC products.

Q: Will my Dexcom G7 app work in France?

A: Yes, if it was already installed and set up at home. The Dexcom G7 app checks that your location matches the country on your Dexcom account when it is first installed, so install and update it before you leave and do not delete it while you are abroad: a deleted app may refuse to reinstall outside your home country. Bring the Dexcom receiver as backup. It has no app store and no install check, and it pairs with your sensor anywhere. The G7 is sold in France, and the Omnipod 5 launched there in June 2024.

Q: What are the emergency numbers in France?

A: Dial 112 for any emergency. It is the single European emergency number, it works nationwide, and it is free from any phone. Dial 15 for SAMU, the medical emergency service, 17 for the police, and 18 for the fire brigade. If you are deaf or hard of hearing, text 114. If you need a doctor outside normal hours but it is not an emergency, call 116117 where it operates, or SOS Médecins at 3624 for a doctor to come to your hotel. If you need the United States Embassy, call +33-1-43-12-22-22 in Paris. If you travel from the United Kingdom, a GHIC or EHIC covers state-provided healthcare, but it is not a replacement for travel insurance.
