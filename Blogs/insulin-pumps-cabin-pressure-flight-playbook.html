Insulin Pumps and Cabin Pressure: What Changes at 35,000 Feet (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 9 min read

Here is the in-flight diabetes fact most pump users have never heard: the air pressure in the cabin changes how much insulin your pump delivers. On takeoff, as the pressure falls, the pump gives a little extra. On landing, as the pressure rises again, it gives a little less. The effect is small, and for most adults it passes without a symptom. But it is real, it is measured, and one pump maker has now put it in a safety notice. Thanksgiving week is the right moment to learn it, with pump users boarding long flights in the same few days. This guide covers what happens, how big it is, what each pump maker says, and the simple playbook that covers it.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets. I have never had a pressure-related low on a flight, and this guide does not describe one. Every number below comes from the published chamber study, the pump makers' own warnings, or the American Diabetes Association (ADA), and every place where the evidence runs out is marked as such. This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. 1. What cabin pressure does on a plane

A commercial plane cruises at 30,000 to 42,000 feet, but the cabin is not at that pressure. Airliners pressurise the cabin to about 560 millimeters of mercury (mmHg), the pressure you would feel at about 8,000 feet of elevation. On the ground, before the door closes, the pressure is about 750 mmHg. So every flight runs the same pressure swing: down from 750 to about 560 mmHg on the climb, steady at cruise, and back up to 750 on the descent. The swing is gentle, and it happens on every flight, short or long.

The swing matters to a pump because of the air inside it. Two pieces of physics do the work, and both are simple. First, Henry's law: air dissolved in a liquid comes out of solution as bubbles when the pressure around it falls. As the cabin pressure falls on takeoff, dissolved air in the insulin forms tiny bubbles, and those bubbles push insulin out of the cartridge. Second, Boyle's law: bubbles already inside the cartridge expand as the pressure falls, pushing out more insulin. On landing the reverse happens. Pressure rises, bubbles shrink, and the pump delivers less than it should. The researchers who measured this say the pattern explains lows reported 60 to 105 minutes after takeoff in both adults and children on pumps.

One more thing to know before the numbers: the effect is about the cartridge and the tubing, not the pump brand. The study tested three brands and all three behaved the same way. The size of the effect depends on how much air is in the system and how full the cartridge is, not on which logo is on the pump. That is why the practical steps in this guide work the same way for every tubed pump, and why the tubeless Omnipod gets a different step of its own.

2. The numbers: what the chamber study found

The numbers come from a study presented at the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) 2024 Annual Meeting in Madrid, then peer-reviewed in the journal Diabetologia in 2025. Gillian L. Garden, Ka Siu Fan and Megan Paterson were joint first authors, and the presenter was Professor Bruce King, a pediatric endocrinologist. The team put 26 pumps in Europe's largest hypobaric chamber, a room that can reproduce cabin pressure faithfully: 10 Medtronic MiniMed 780G pumps, 10 Tandem t:slim X2 pumps, and 6 Insulet Omnipod DASH pumps. Each pump was set to deliver basal insulin, the background insulin a pump drips all day, at 0.6 U per hour, a rate typical for adults and children. The chamber ran a standard flight: 20 minutes climbing to 550 mmHg, 30 minutes at cruise, and 20 minutes descending back to 750 mmHg.

Here is what they measured. With full cartridges, every pump over-delivered 0.60 U during the 20 minute ascent, compared with its delivery on the ground. During the descent, every pump under-delivered 0.51 U. During cruise, delivery was steady. The numbers are small: 0.60 U is roughly the amount a pump at that basal rate would deliver in an hour. For most adults, that is a small extra dose, and the researchers say so plainly. The people most likely to feel it are the ones the Medtronic notice names: people on small total daily doses of insulin and people with high insulin sensitivity, including children.

The study also ran the real-world half. Seven pilots with insulin-treated diabetes who used pumps recorded their blood sugar across 1,081 flights over seven and a half years. None of them had an episode of in-flight hypoglycemia, and every median reading stayed in the acceptable range. Three readings landed in the low amber zone, and all three came from the same pilot, which the authors say points to that pilot's own management rather than the cabin. The authors are also honest about the limits of the chamber numbers. The pumps were tested in an open system, with no skin and tissue pushing back against the cannula, and the authors write that the true effect in real life is likely much smaller, or possibly none at all. Read the numbers as the maximum the effect can be, not as what happens on every flight.

3. Medtronic's safety notice: the maker speaks first

In January 2025, Medtronic issued an Urgent Medical Device Correction to users of its MiniMed pumps, covering the Paradigm series and the 600 and 700 series, including the 780G. The notice says, in plain terms, that changes in atmospheric pressure can cause unintentional insulin delivery. When pressure decreases, as during takeoff, more insulin than expected may be released. When pressure increases, as during landing, less insulin may be released than expected. The part that surprises most pump users is the next line: the extra insulin may be released even if the pump is stopped or programmed to zero units per hour. Stopping the pump does not stop the bubbles.

The notice gives the scale of what Medtronic had seen. Between July 2003 and May 2024, the company received 138 complaints that could be related to this issue, 19 of which reported serious injuries, though none were confirmed to be related to the issue. The notice asks users to check glucose frequently when traveling by plane and in any situation where sudden or extreme changes in pressure, altitude, or gravity may occur, to carry an emergency kit with fast-acting glucose and backup insulin, and to talk to a healthcare professional about how to prepare. It also repeats the point from the study: people with lower daily insulin doses and those with high insulin sensitivity may see greater changes in glucose during pressure changes than people with higher doses or lower sensitivity.

That notice matters for two reasons. First, it is a pump maker saying, in writing, that the effect is real and that its own pumps are affected. Second, it is the only source in this guide that tells you to do nothing to the pump at all: no setting change, no suspend, no disconnect. Its advice is to watch your glucose, carry fast-acting carbs, and talk to your care team. The disconnect playbook in section 5 comes from the study researchers, not from Medtronic, and the two approaches are easy to combine: use the researchers' steps, and keep Medtronic's watching and carrying rules as the base.

4. What each pump maker says

Omnipod users get the clearest written warning of any pump. The Omnipod 5 User Guide says not to use the system at low atmospheric pressure, below 700 hectopascals (hPa), and explains that a change in atmospheric pressure can also occur during takeoff with air travel, that unintended insulin delivery can occur if tiny air bubbles inside the Pod expand, that this can result in hypoglycemia, and that it is important to check glucose frequently when flying. The same guide also says the system is safe to use at the pressures typically found in airplane cabins, and that it can be used at pressures as low as 700 hPa, which is lower than typical cabin pressure. So the warning is not "do not fly with an Omnipod." It is "fly, and check your glucose often, because takeoff can push a little extra insulin out of the Pod." The Omnipod DASH user guide carries the same flight warning and the same instruction to check blood glucose frequently while flying. Our Omnipod 5 guide covers the 700 hPa floor and the takeoff and landing checks in full.

Tandem's answer is different in kind. The company's chief medical officer has said that the t:slim X2 pump's microdelivery technology limits how much extra insulin pressure changes can deliver, because of the mechanism between the tubing and the contents of the bag inside the cartridge. The Tandem Mobi user manual includes a warning about significant pressure changes in specific air travel situations, with guidance to disconnect. That is the whole of what the public record supports for Tandem: the t:slim X2 design limits the extra delivery, and the Mobi manual tells users to disconnect when a big pressure change is expected. If you use a Tandem pump, the disconnect steps in section 5 are the ones that fit, and your own user guide is the place to check the exact wording for your model.

Medtronic's user guide adds a limit of a different sort. The 780G system has not been tested at altitudes higher than 10,150 feet. That number is about the mountain, not the cabin: a pressurised cabin at 8,000 feet sits below it, so a normal flight is inside the tested range. The number matters on the ground at altitude, which is why our skiing playbook covers it in full, with the backup plan for a pump that alarms above its rating. For the flight itself, the Medtronic document that matters is the January 2025 safety notice in section 3, not the altitude rating.

5. The takeoff and landing playbook

This is the practical heart of the guide, and it comes from the study's lead researchers, as reported at EASD 2024. The steps are different for tubed pumps and for the tubeless Omnipod, because only a tubed pump can be disconnected.

If you use a tubed pump, disconnect just before takeoff. The insulin will still come out of the line as the pressure falls, but it will not be delivered to you. Reconnect about 20 minutes after the plane reaches cruising altitude, once the cabin pressure has settled. Before you reconnect, check the cartridge and the line for bubbles and prime the line, so the air that formed during the climb is out of the system before delivery restarts. On descent, disconnect again after landing and prime the line to clear the shortfall that built up on the way down. That is the whole protocol: disconnect, reconnect at cruise, clear the bubbles, disconnect after landing, prime.

Two honest caveats come with that protocol. First, the researchers' own discussion says the same thing in softer words: users may consider disconnecting before takeoff and removing air bubbles before reconnecting at cruising altitude. It is a suggestion to consider with your care team, not a rule from your pump maker. Second, one endocrinologist commenting on the study called the effect minor and warned that the real risk of disconnecting is forgetting to reconnect, which can push glucose high. If you disconnect, make reconnecting part of your cruise routine, the same way putting your seatbelt on is part of takeoff. Check your glucose before takeoff and before landing either way, keep sugar packets in your pocket, not in the overhead bin, and treat a low with your usual plan: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60.

If you use an Omnipod, you cannot disconnect, so the playbook is different. Eat a small snack on takeoff, and eat another small snack on landing, and bolus for the landing snack to cover the insulin the Pod held back on the way down. That is the researcher's own recommendation for the tubeless pump: food covers the extra insulin on the way up, and the bolus covers the shortfall on the way down. Check your glucose before takeoff and before landing, the same as with a tubed pump, and keep the low kit at your seat. The Omnipod 5 guide has the same two checks in its own checklist, and the glucagon guide covers the emergency kit that rides with the low kit.

6. Pens, vials, and checked bags

Cabin pressure reaches insulin pens too, in a smaller way. NHS Tayside, the Scottish health board, answers the question directly in its travel FAQ: air bubbles can accumulate in pen cartridges because of cabin pressure changes, so it is best to remove the needle from the pen immediately after the injection. That is the whole pen rule for pressure: take the needle off after every injection on a flight day, so bubbles cannot build up in the cartridge between doses. The pens and vials guide covers the rest of flying with pens, including how to inject on the plane.

The bigger rule for pens and vials is the one that applies to every form of insulin. The ADA says not to place insulin in checked baggage, because it could be damaged by severe changes in pressure and temperature, and to inspect insulin for signs of damage after traveling, calling your doctor if it is damaged and you have no backup insulin with you. The cargo hold is the worst place on the plane for insulin: pressure swings are bigger, and the temperature can freeze it. All insulin rides in the carry-on, in the bag at your seat, never in a checked bag. Pack 20 percent more supplies than the trip math says, because a Thanksgiving week delay stretches the trip, and the extra supplies turn a late flight into an inconvenience instead of a crisis. Our insulin cooling guide covers the temperature half of the same rule, and the pat-down rights guide and the scanner guide cover getting the bag through the checkpoint.

7. Sudden loss of cabin pressure

There is one scenario where the numbers stop being small. If the cabin loses pressure suddenly, the same physics runs much harder. The study team tested it: with infusion sets and reservoirs only, no pumps, a rapid decompression released fluid equal to 5.6 U. That is roughly nine times the normal takeoff effect, and it is enough to cause a serious low. These events are rare. The study's presenter put the number at about 40 to 50 a year across commercial aviation, and the widely reported Alaska Airlines door incident in January 2024 was one of them.

If it happens on your flight, the crew's instructions come first. Put your own oxygen mask on before helping anyone else, exactly as the crew directs. If you can do so safely, disconnect your pump, since the published guidance points to disconnecting when a rapid change in altitude is expected. Then check your glucose as soon as things settle, and tell the crew you use an insulin pump. Treat a low with your usual plan once you are safe to do so, and if you cannot treat yourself, the severe-low playbook covers what cabin crew can and cannot do. This is the one scenario in this guide where the amount is large enough to matter for anyone, and it is also the one you are least likely to meet.

What to do on your next flight

Here is the full list, in this order:

Insulin Pumps and Cabin Pressure: The Flight Checklist

  • Before the trip: ask your care team to confirm the takeoff and landing plan for your pump model, and read your pump's user guide for its own pressure warning
  • Pack 20% extra supplies; all insulin and pump supplies in the carry-on at your seat, never in checked baggage; inspect insulin for damage after landing
  • Low kit at your seat: sugar packets (1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60), fingerstick meter, glucagon device
  • Check your glucose before takeoff and before landing
  • Tubed pump: disconnect just before takeoff, reconnect about 20 minutes after reaching cruising altitude, clear bubbles and prime the line before reconnecting, disconnect after landing and prime the line
  • Omnipod: eat a small snack on takeoff and another small snack on landing, and bolus for the landing snack
  • Pens: remove the needle from the pen immediately after each injection on a flight day
  • If the cabin loses pressure suddenly: follow the crew's instructions first, disconnect the pump if you can do so safely, check your glucose as soon as things settle, and tell the crew you use an insulin pump
  • After landing: check your glucose again, reconnect or re-prime as above, and reset your pump clock to local time with the time zone guide

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can cabin pressure change how much insulin my pump delivers?

A: Yes, a little. In a chamber study of 26 pumps, full cartridges delivered 0.60 U more than expected during a 20 minute climb to cabin pressure, and 0.51 U less than expected on the way back down. The cause is air: dissolved air comes out of the insulin as bubbles when pressure falls, and bubbles already in the cartridge expand, pushing insulin out. On landing the reverse happens and delivery falls short. The effect is small for most adults, and the study authors note the real world effect is likely smaller than the chamber numbers.

Q: Should I disconnect my pump for takeoff and landing?

A: If you use a tubed pump, the published playbook is to disconnect just before takeoff, reconnect about 20 minutes after the plane reaches cruising altitude, clear any bubbles before you reconnect, and disconnect again after landing to prime the line. If you use an Omnipod, you cannot disconnect, so eat a small snack on takeoff and another small snack on landing, and bolus for the landing snack. Either way, check your glucose before takeoff and before landing, and ask your care team to confirm the plan before your trip.

Q: What did Medtronic's safety notice say about flying with a pump?

A: In January 2025 Medtronic issued an Urgent Medical Device Correction for its Paradigm, 600 series, and 700 series pumps. It said changes in air pressure can cause unintended insulin delivery: when pressure falls, as on takeoff, more insulin than expected may be released, even if the pump is stopped or set to zero units per hour, and when pressure rises, as on landing, less may be released. Between July 2003 and May 2024 Medtronic received 138 complaints that could be related, 19 of which reported serious injuries, though none were confirmed to be related.

Q: Can I use an Omnipod 5 on a plane?

A: Yes. Insulet says the Omnipod 5 System is safe to use at the pressures typically found in airplane cabins, and it can be used at pressures as low as 700 hPa, which is lower than typical cabin pressure. The user guide still warns that pressure changes during takeoff can expand tiny air bubbles inside the Pod and cause unintended insulin delivery, which can lead to a low, so check your glucose frequently while flying. The same warning appears in the Omnipod DASH user guide.

Q: Does cabin pressure affect insulin pens too?

A: Yes, in a smaller way. NHS Tayside advises that air bubbles can accumulate in pen cartridges because of cabin pressure changes, so remove the needle from the pen immediately after each injection. The bigger pen rule is the same as for pumps: keep insulin in your carry-on, never in checked baggage, where pressure and temperature extremes can damage it, and inspect insulin for signs of damage after traveling.

Q: What should I do if the cabin suddenly loses pressure?

A: A sudden loss of cabin pressure is rare, about 40 to 50 events a year across commercial aviation, but the effect on a pump is large: in the chamber simulation, fluid equal to 5.6 U was released. If it happens, follow the crew's instructions first, disconnect your pump if you can do so safely, and check your glucose as soon as things settle. Tell the crew you use an insulin pump, and treat a low with your usual plan once you are safe to do so.
