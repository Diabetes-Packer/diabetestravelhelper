Travel Vaccines with Type 1 Diabetes: The Pre-Trip Shot List for Holiday Travel (2026)

Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

Every diabetes packing list starts with supplies. Almost none of them start with the shots, and the calendar is the reason to fix that now. Thanksgiving is 27 days away, Christmas is eight weeks out, and November is Diabetes Awareness Month, which means the holiday trips are booked, the airports will be crowded, and flu season is already here. A vaccine appointment is the one piece of trip preparation that cannot be rushed at the last minute, because immunity takes weeks to build and one certificate takes 10 days to become valid. This guide is the shot list: the routine vaccines, the diabetes list, the flu shot timing, the travel clinic window, the destination vaccines, and the yellow card.

I have had type 1 diabetes (T1D) for 20 years and I fly with an Omnipod 5 and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. This guide names the vaccines to ask about, not the vaccines to get: which shot, which product, and which schedule are care-team decisions, and the guide says so wherever the answer depends on your age, your history, or your destination. The pre-trip appointment guide covers the visit where this list gets turned into a personal plan, the sick-day playbook covers what happens if illness finds you anyway, and the travel insurance guide covers the policy that pays for the hospital visit the vaccines are trying to prevent.

Talk to your care team before any trip decision that touches your medicine. This guide explains which vaccines to ask about, not which vaccines you personally need. Vaccine choices depend on your age, your vaccine history, your destination, and your health, and those decisions belong in a conversation with your care team, not in a blog post. This guide is general information, not medical advice, and vaccine recommendations and destination requirements change, so confirm the current details for your own trip before you travel.

1. The two lists: routine vaccines and destination vaccines

Travel vaccines fall into two lists, and confusing them is how a trip gets the wrong preparation. The first list is routine vaccines: the shots recommended for everyone in the United States, depending on age and vaccine history. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says to make sure you are up to date with all of your routine vaccines before travel, and gives the reason plainly: many vaccine-preventable diseases, such as measles, mumps, or chickenpox, are not common in the United States, but international travel increases your chances of getting and spreading diseases that are not common here, and many popular destinations still have outbreaks of measles and other vaccine-preventable diseases. A crowded holiday airport is exactly the place a routine gap meets exposure.

The routine vaccinations related to travel, from the CDC list, may include COVID-19, chickenpox (varicella), hepatitis A, hepatitis B, influenza, measles mumps rubella (MMR), meningococcal, pneumococcal, polio, rotavirus, and tetanus diphtheria pertussis (Tdap, where pertussis is whooping cough). Which of those you need depends on your age, your health, and your vaccine history, and the CDC notes you may need an accelerated dose or a booster dose before traveling. That is a question for the clinic, not a checklist to self-complete: a booster given at the travel visit is routine, and a gap found at the travel visit is the whole point of the visit.

The second list is destination vaccines: shots tied to where you are going, not to who you are. Section 4 covers that list, and the rule that governs it is simple. The CDC page for your specific destination is the current source, and this guide does not replace it. A guide that names a destination vaccine for a country without checking that country's current page is guessing, and a shot list is the wrong place to guess.

2. The diabetes list: flu, pneumococcal, and hepatitis B

On top of the routine list sits a shorter list that exists because of diabetes itself. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) 2026 Standards of Care, recommendation 4.5, says to provide routinely recommended vaccinations for children, adolescents, and adults with diabetes as indicated by age. Three items on that list deserve their own lines, because travelers ask about them by name.

  • Influenza, every year. The CDC says annual flu vaccination is especially important for people with diabetes because they are at higher risk of developing serious flu complications, and the ADA Standards advise all people with diabetes to receive a trivalent influenza vaccine, meaning a three component vaccine, and not to receive live attenuated influenza vaccine. Section 3 covers the flu shot in full, including which form to accept.
  • Pneumococcal, kept up to date. The CDC says people with diabetes should be up to date with pneumococcal vaccination, because having flu increases the risk of getting pneumococcal disease, and pneumococcal pneumonia is a serious flu-related complication that can cause death. If you are due for the pneumococcal vaccine, the CDC says you can get the one your provider recommends when you get a flu vaccine. Which product and how many doses are provider questions, and this guide does not answer them, because the answer depends on your age and history.
  • Hepatitis B, by age. The ADA Standards say that compared with the general population, people with type 1 or type 2 diabetes have higher rates of hepatitis, and because of the higher likelihood of transmission, hepatitis B vaccine is recommended for adults with diabetes aged under 60 years. For adults aged 60 years and older, the vaccine may be given at the discretion of the treating clinician, based on the person's likelihood of acquiring hepatitis B infection. If you are under 60 and have never had the hepatitis B series, that is a line item for the travel visit, not an assumption to carry onto the plane.

One more item belongs on the diabetes list, and it is not a shot. The CDC flu guidance for people with diabetes says to plan to maintain sufficient supplies of regular medications for chronic conditions, and to have at least a two-week supply of necessary medications on hand. Vaccines reduce the odds of illness, and the medication buffer covers the illness that gets through anyway. Pack the buffer with the same 20% extra supplies rule this site runs on for every trip, so a delayed flight home never becomes a medication gap.

3. The flu shot and the holiday calendar

The flu shot is the vaccine with a deadline on it, and the deadline is this week. The CDC says that for most people who need only one dose of influenza vaccine for the season, September and October are generally good times to be vaccinated, and ideally everyone should be vaccinated by the end of October. It takes about two weeks after vaccination for antibodies to develop in the body and provide protection, which is why the window sits before flu activity rises, not during it. A flu vaccine is also needed every year, for two reasons the CDC states plainly: immune protection declines over time, and influenza viruses change, so the vaccine composition is reviewed annually.

October 30 is still inside the useful window for a Thanksgiving trip, and the math shows why. Thanksgiving is 27 days out, and two weeks of antibody building fits inside that gap with room to spare, which means a shot this week is protection in place before the crowded airport, not protection still building on the plane. If October passes without the shot, the CDC guidance still applies: vaccination should continue through the season while flu viruses are circulating and vaccine is available, so a late shot still beats no shot. The worst version of this plan is waiting until flu is already spreading heavily in your destination to start thinking about it.

The diabetes reason for the flu shot is stronger than the general reason. The CDC says people with type 1, type 2, or gestational diabetes, even when well managed, are at higher risk of developing serious flu complications, and those complications can result in hospitalization and sometimes even death. The complications named are pneumonia, bronchitis, sinus infections, and ear infections, and data reported to CDC show about 30% of adults hospitalized with flu have diabetes. Flu vaccination has been associated with reduced flu-related hospitalizations among people with diabetes (79%). Acute illnesses like flu can also make managing blood sugar levels harder: flu may raise blood sugar levels, and it can also cause blood sugar levels to fall if you cannot eat or drink as much as usual, which is why the CDC says people with diabetes need to follow sick-day guidelines if they become ill.

Two practical rules complete the flu section. First, the form matters: the CDC says injectable influenza vaccines (flu shots) are recommended for people with diabetes, and people with diabetes should generally not receive the nasal spray flu vaccine, which is the live attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV). If a clinic offers you the spray, say you have diabetes and ask for the injectable vaccine. Second, the response plan matters as much as the shot: if you experience flu symptoms, the CDC says to contact your healthcare provider right away, because antiviral drugs can treat flu illness and may prevent serious flu complications, and the CDC recommends prompt flu treatment for people with diabetes who have flu or suspected flu. The sick-day playbook covers the testing rules and red flags that sit behind that call.

4. The travel clinic visit: 4 to 6 weeks before departure

The CDC traveler guidance sets one window for the whole vaccine question: make an appointment with your healthcare provider or a travel health specialist that takes place at least 4 to 6 weeks before you leave. They can help you get destination-specific vaccines, medicines, and information, and discussing your health concerns, itinerary, and planned activities with your provider allows them to give more specific advice and recommendations. The CDC adds the reason the window is weeks long: because some vaccines require multiple doses, it is best to see your health care provider as soon as possible.

That math is what makes late October the moment for holiday trips. A Thanksgiving flight 27 days out is inside the 4 to 6 week window but at its short end, which means the appointment belongs this week, not next week, and a multi-dose series may already need its accelerated version or a plan for the doses that fall after departure. A Christmas flight eight weeks out is comfortably inside the window, which means the full series fits if the appointment happens now. If your trip is inside the window already, the priority order at the visit is the flu shot first, because its two-week antibody clock is the shortest, then the routine gaps, then the destination list.

Bring three things to the visit. First, your itinerary and activity list, because the provider's advice depends on them: a beach resort and a rural trek in the same country can produce different vaccine lists. Second, your vaccine history, because the clinic can only find the gaps in a record it can see. Third, the question list from the pre-trip appointment guide, including the travel letter, the generic-name prescriptions, and the sick-day plan, so one visit produces the paperwork as well as the shots. Some vaccines are not available at every clinic: if yellow fever vaccine is recommended or required for your destination, the CDC says you will need to go to a vaccine center authorized to give yellow fever vaccinations, and many of those centers also provide other pre-travel health care services.

5. Destination vaccines: examples only, your destination page decides

The CDC travel vaccines page lists examples of vaccines you may need for the first time or as boosters before you travel: COVID-19, chickenpox, chikungunya, cholera, flu, hepatitis A, hepatitis B, Japanese encephalitis, MMR (measles, mumps, rubella), meningococcal, monkeypox, pneumococcal, polio, rabies, shingles, Tdap (tetanus, diphtheria, pertussis), tick-borne encephalitis, typhoid, and yellow fever. Read that list as a menu of possibilities, not as a prescription: no traveler needs all of them, most travelers need a few, and which few depends on the destination, the season, the activities, and the traveler's history.

The working method is one page per trip. Open the CDC destination page for your destination and read its vaccines and medicines list, then take that list to the travel visit and ask the provider to confirm it against your history. Two habits make that method work. First, check the page close to departure, not months out, because destination requirements change with outbreaks and seasons. Second, check the layover countries too, not just the final destination: section 6 shows why a brief layover can create a vaccine requirement of its own. The international travel guide covers the wider paperwork that travels with the vaccine list, and the airport security abroad guide covers the checkpoint conversation at the far end.

One boundary belongs here because it is easy to cross by accident. This guide names no vaccine for any specific destination on purpose, for the same reason the pre-trip appointment guide names none: the right list depends on where you are going, and the CDC page for your destination is the current source. If a general travel article tells you that a country requires a shot, treat that as a prompt to check the CDC destination page and the destination embassy, not as the rule itself. The embassy check matters because entry requirements are set by countries, and a requirement can exist even where the health recommendation does not, which is exactly the yellow fever situation in section 6.

6. The yellow card: valid 10 days after the shot, for life

Yellow fever is the vaccine with paperwork attached, and the paperwork is the point. The International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis (ICVP), also called the yellow card, is the official, internationally recognized document that travelers use to document proof of vaccination for diseases included under the International Health Regulations (IHR). Vaccination against yellow fever must be documented using the ICVP, and the ICVP must be validated with the Uniform Stamp of the center where the vaccine was given. A letter from a clinic, a pharmacy printout, or a photo of a vial does not substitute for it at a border.

The timing rule is the one that ruins trips. Certificates of yellow fever vaccination are valid beginning 10 days after the date of vaccination, and the certificate is entered with the date 10 days after the original vaccination plus the wording "life of person vaccinated," because yellow fever vaccines are valid for a person's lifetime, even if ICVP cards have an expiration date. A shot given 9 days before arrival is not a valid certificate on arrival, no matter how recently the needle went in. Travelers who do not provide an ICVP documenting valid yellow fever vaccination may be denied entry, quarantined, or asked to get revaccinated at the point of entry to a country, which is a high price for a one-week scheduling miss.

Two more yellow card rules catch travelers who did everything else right. First, the layover rule: some countries require all arriving travelers to show proof of yellow fever vaccination before entering, while other countries require proof from travelers arriving from countries with a risk of yellow fever virus exposure, even for a brief layover. For a trip through multiple countries, the order of travel can matter, so check every country on the itinerary, including the connection, not just the final destination. Second, the records rule: the CDC does not issue ICVPs and does not have copies of vaccination records, so a lost yellow card is replaced through the original vaccine provider, or through the state health department in the state where the vaccine was administered if the provider is unavailable. Photograph the card, keep the paper original with your travel documents, and carry the documentation trio from the customs guide alongside it: original packaging, prescription copies, and the doctor's letter.

7. After the shots: records, supplies, and the sick-day plan

The appointment is not finished when the needle comes out. The CDC instruction for the trip itself is simple: bring a copy of your official immunization records with you when you travel. Keep the paper copy with your travel documents, photograph every card, and keep a paper backup as well as a phone copy, so a dead battery or a lost bag does not take the proof with it. Ask the clinic what to watch for in the days after each shot, and write the answer down with the sick-day plan, because a sore arm and a fever on day two of a trip are easier to read with the clinic's answer in hand than without it.

Then connect the vaccine plan to the rest of the trip plan. Tell your travel companion where the glucagon is and how it is used before wheels-up, using the briefing in the glucagon guide, because the companion briefing and the vaccine appointment are both pre-trip conversations that are hard to have mid-trip. Pack the 20% extra supplies buffer, keep every diabetes supply in the carry-on, and keep the care team's on-call number saved offline, so a vaccine-preventable illness that still breaks through meets a plan instead of a scramble. The sick-day playbook covers the testing rules, the fluids, and the red flags for that scenario, including the rule that insulin is never stopped during illness, even when you are not eating.

8. The travel vaccine checklist

Run this list at the travel visit, not at the airport:

  1. Book the travel clinic visit at least 4 to 6 weeks before departure, and this week if Thanksgiving is the trip.
  2. Check the routine list: influenza, COVID-19, measles mumps rubella, tetanus diphtheria pertussis, chickenpox, hepatitis A, hepatitis B, meningococcal, pneumococcal, and polio, as age and history require.
  3. Check the diabetes list: the annual flu shot as an injection, not the nasal spray; pneumococcal vaccination kept up to date; hepatitis B vaccination if you are under 60 and have not had the series.
  4. Get the flu shot by the end of October when possible, and remember the two-week antibody clock when counting back from departure.
  5. Open the CDC destination page for every country on the itinerary, including layover countries, and take its vaccines and medicines list to the visit.
  6. If yellow fever vaccine is required or recommended, go to an authorized vaccine center, get the stamped yellow card, and confirm the certificate date is at least 10 days before arrival.
  7. Bring a copy of your official immunization records on the trip, photograph every card, and keep a paper backup.
  8. Ask the clinic what to watch for in the days after each shot, and write the answer down with the sick-day plan.
  9. Pack 20% extra supplies, keep the care team's on-call number saved offline, and carry the medication buffer the CDC advises for people with diabetes.
  10. If flu symptoms start on the trip, contact your healthcare provider right away: the CDC recommends prompt flu treatment for people with diabetes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Which vaccines should a traveler with type 1 diabetes check before a trip?

A: Start with three checks. First, the routine vaccines for everyone in the United States, which the CDC says to keep up to date before travel: influenza, COVID-19, measles mumps rubella, tetanus diphtheria pertussis, chickenpox, hepatitis A, hepatitis B, meningococcal, pneumococcal, and polio, depending on age and vaccine history. Second, the diabetes list in section 2: the annual flu shot, pneumococcal vaccination kept up to date, and hepatitis B vaccination, which the American Diabetes Association recommends for adults with diabetes aged under 60 years. Third, the destination list in section 4, which comes from the CDC page for your specific destination, not from a general guide. Your care team turns those three lists into one personal list.

Q: Should I get the flu shot or the nasal spray flu vaccine with diabetes?

A: The shot. The CDC says injectable influenza vaccines are recommended for people with diabetes, and people with diabetes should generally not receive the nasal spray flu vaccine, which is the live attenuated influenza vaccine. The American Diabetes Association 2026 Standards of Care say the same thing: all people with diabetes are advised to receive a trivalent influenza vaccine, meaning a three component vaccine, and not to receive live attenuated influenza vaccine, every year. If a clinic offers you the spray, say you have diabetes and ask for the injectable vaccine.

Q: When should I get the flu shot before holiday travel?

A: For most people who need only one dose, September and October are generally good times, and ideally everyone should be vaccinated by the end of October, according to the CDC. It takes about two weeks after vaccination for antibodies to develop and provide protection, which is why the window sits before flu activity rises. October 30 is still inside the useful window for a Thanksgiving trip 27 days out, because two weeks of antibody building fits before the flight. If October passes, vaccination should continue through the season while flu viruses are circulating and vaccine is available, so a late shot still beats no shot.

Q: How far before a trip should I see a travel clinic about vaccines?

A: At least 4 to 6 weeks before you leave, according to the CDC traveler guidance. The appointment covers destination-specific vaccines, medicines, and information, and discussing your itinerary and planned activities lets the provider give more specific advice. The window is weeks, not days, because some vaccines require multiple doses spaced out, and the CDC says to see a provider as soon as possible for that reason. A Thanksgiving trip 27 days out is inside the window but at its short end, so book the appointment this week rather than next. The pre-trip appointment guide covers what else that visit should produce.

Q: What is the yellow card, and when does it become valid?

A: The yellow card is the International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis, known as the ICVP. It is the official, internationally recognized document for proof of vaccination for diseases included under the International Health Regulations, and vaccination against yellow fever must be documented using it. The certificate must be validated with the Uniform Stamp of the center where the vaccine was given, and yellow fever vaccine is given only at authorized vaccine centers. A yellow fever certificate becomes valid beginning 10 days after the date of vaccination, and it stays valid for the life of the person vaccinated, even if the card shows an expiration date. Travelers without a valid ICVP may be denied entry, quarantined, or asked to get revaccinated at the point of entry.

Q: What vaccine records should I carry on the trip?

A: Bring a copy of your official immunization records with you when you travel, which is the CDC instruction in plain words. Keep the paper copy with your travel documents, photograph every card including the yellow card, and keep the photo where a dead phone battery cannot take it away from you, which means a paper backup as well as a phone copy. The CDC does not keep individual vaccination records and does not issue ICVPs, so if a yellow card is lost, the replacement comes from the original vaccine provider, or from the state health department where the vaccine was given. The customs guide covers the rest of the border paperwork, and the travel letter guide covers the doctor's letter.

General guidance only, not medical advice. Your insulin type, schedule, and supplies are conversations with your care team, ideally at an appointment four to six weeks before your next trip. Vaccine recommendations, destination requirements, and entry rules change, so confirm the current details for your destination with your care team before you travel. If you have flu symptoms or suspected diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA, the emergency where ketones build up because the body cannot use glucose) on a trip, get medical help and tell your care team afterward.
