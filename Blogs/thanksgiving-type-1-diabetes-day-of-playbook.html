Thanksgiving with Type 1 Diabetes: The Day-Of Playbook (2026)

Thanksgiving is the hardest meal of the year for type 1 diabetes: late, enormous, carb-heavy, and served at a table where everyone watches what you eat. The good news is that it is also the most plannable meal of the year. The dishes are the same every time, the carb counts are published, and the timeline is yours to run. Here is the full day-of playbook.

Updated October 2026  ·  8 min read

I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years, and I have eaten a lot of Thanksgiving dinners in that time. The ones that went well all had the same shape: a normal morning, a plan for the plate, and a second check after dessert when the fat-heavy dishes finally showed up. None of this requires skipping anything. It requires knowing the numbers before the gravy boat reaches you. As always, this is general guidance, not medical advice. Your care team owns your plan.

Do not skip breakfast

The most common Thanksgiving mistake happens before the turkey is even out of the oven: skipping breakfast to "save" carbs for dinner. Breakthrough T1D flags this directly in its holiday guide, because the pattern is predictable. You go low from the missed meal, you arrive starving, you overeat, and then you spend the evening high. It is the worst version of the day, and it starts with good intentions.

Eat your normal breakfast. Mine is low-carb and high-protein on any travel or holiday morning, because it keeps the day stable from the start, and Thanksgiving is no exception. If dinner timing is uncertain, which it usually is, add a small snack in the afternoon so you are walking into the meal steady instead of shaky. You want to meet the mashed potatoes on your terms.

Morning movement helps. A UCLA endocrinologist recommends cardio and strength exercise on Thanksgiving morning to improve glucose metabolism for the rest of the day, and getting up to move every 30 minutes during the meal itself. A walk after dinner does more for your numbers than any post-meal strategy.

The carb counts for the classics

Here is why Thanksgiving is secretly the easiest holiday meal to dose for: the dishes barely change year to year, and the carb counts are published. The table below comes from Breakthrough T1D's holiday carb counting guide for type 1 diabetes. Treat every number as a starting estimate, because recipes and portions vary, and the cook's heavy hand with brown sugar is not in the table.

DishPortionCarbs
TurkeyAny0g
Mashed potatoes1 cup30g
Stuffing1/2 cup20g
Cranberry sauce1/4 cup25g
Dinner roll120g
Gravy1/2 cup5g
Green beans1/2 cup4g
Green bean casserole1 1/2 cup9g
Sweet potato1 medium28g
Candied yams1/2 cup45g
Pumpkin pie1/8 slice35g
Pecan pie1/8 slice60g

Two numbers do most of the work here. Turkey is zero carbs, so it is the anchor of the plate. And the three quiet budget-killers are the ones people forget to count: the roll (20g), the cranberry sauce (25g), and the candied yams (45g for half a cup). A plate that feels like "mostly turkey and sides" can land at 100g before dessert without anyone noticing.

For a planning target, a UCLA endocrinologist suggests one moderate meal of at most 60g of carbs for women and 75g for men, with dessert at 30g or less. That is a suggestion, not a rule, and your own ratios and care team decide your numbers. But it gives the table above a job: pick the dishes that fit the budget before you pick up the serving spoon.

The plate strategy

Count the carbs, then build the plate in this order. Protein and non-starchy vegetables first: turkey, green beans, salad. Then the carb-heavy sides, and keep them to about a third of the plate to start. You can always go back. Breakthrough T1D's Thanksgiving guide uses exactly this order, because eating the protein and vegetables first blunts the spike from everything that follows.

Pre-bolus the way you do at home. I take mine without a timer and it works fine; the point is giving the insulin a head start before the food lands, not the method. A Thanksgiving plate is bigger than a normal dinner, so the head start matters more, not less. If your care team has given you a split or extended approach for high-fat meals, this is the meal it was made for.

Watch for the delayed spike

This is the part that ambushes people. Thanksgiving dishes are high in fat as well as carbs: butter in the potatoes, crust on the pie, gravy on everything. Fat slows digestion, so the blood sugar rise from a Thanksgiving plate can show up later than a normal meal. Breakthrough T1D calls this out in its holiday guide as the thing to watch for, and it is the reason the two-hour check can look fine while the bedtime check does not.

The fix is just checking on a schedule instead of on a feeling. Check before the meal, check two hours after, and check before bed even if the earlier numbers looked good. Keep your CGM (continuous glucose monitor) low alarm on its normal setting through the evening; the risk tonight is the high you did not see coming, not the low. Dessert counts as part of the meal, not a separate event: a slice of pumpkin pie at 35g and pecan at 60g are already in the table above, so count them with the plate, not after it.

The drink and the questions

If wine or beer is part of your Thanksgiving, the rules are the same as any holiday: never drink on an empty stomach, and keep it moderate and with food. Alcohol can lower blood sugar hours later, so the overnight low alarm stays loud. The full alcohol playbook, including why you bolus for beer but not for hard alcohol or dry wine, is in the Halloween party guide, and it applies word for word at the Thanksgiving table.

As for the relatives watching your plate: you do not owe anyone a diabetes lecture over dinner. "I have got it handled" is a complete sentence. If someone insists the pie is sugar-free and therefore free, remember the rule: sugar-free does not mean carb-free. The crust still counts.

Thanksgiving travel

For a lot of us, Thanksgiving starts at an airport. The travel rules do not take the holiday off: pack 20% more supplies than the trip math says, keep every supply in your carry-on, and keep fast-acting glucose at your seat on the flight. Sugar packets are my low treatment everywhere, including at 35,000 feet: one packet at 60 to 70, two below 60. The full holiday travel playbook covers the airport side of the trip.

Thanksgiving day checklist

Here is the full list, in this order:

  1. Eat a normal low-carb, high-protein breakfast. Do not skip it.
  2. Get morning movement in: cardio or strength, plus a walk after dinner.
  3. If dinner timing is unknown, eat a small afternoon snack so you arrive steady.
  4. Review the carb table and pick your dishes before you serve yourself.
  5. Build the plate protein and vegetables first, carb sides to a third of the plate.
  6. Pre-bolus like you do at home, before the first bite.
  7. Count dessert with the meal. Pumpkin pie is about 35g a slice, pecan about 60g.
  8. Check two hours after the meal, then again before bed. Watch for the delayed fat spike.
  9. Keep the CGM low alarm on through the night, especially if alcohol was involved.
  10. Keep sugar packets in a pocket all day: 1 at 60 to 70, 2 below 60.
  11. Flying home? 20% extra supplies, everything in the carry-on, glucose at your seat.
General guidance only, not medical advice. Carb counts are estimates from published sources and vary by recipe and portion. Insulin dosing is personal: work with your care team on your ratios and your plan for high-fat holiday meals.
