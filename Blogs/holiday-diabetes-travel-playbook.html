Flying Home for the Holidays with Diabetes: The Thanksgiving/Christmas Travel Playbook (2026)

Every holiday diabetes article is about the food. This one is about everything before it: the crowded airports, the delays, the weird travel-day blood sugars, and landing straight into the biggest carb meal of the year.

September 2026  ·  6 min read  ·  By Diabetes Travel Helper

The holidays are the hardest travel days of the year with diabetes. Not because of the pie. Because of everything before the pie.

One of my favorite holiday travel memories is from Bruges. We went for the Christmas markets, and Christmas Eve dinner was ribs. I just stuck with a nice high-protein meal, and for the first time my blood sugars were perfect through a whole holiday dinner. That trip is where I learned the protein trick, and I have used it every holiday season since.

Thanksgiving week and the Christmas rush mean the most crowded airports of the year, the longest security lines, and the highest chance your flight does not leave when it is supposed to. You eat at weird hours, sit for longer than usual, and then you land and walk straight into the biggest carb meal of the year. It is a lot to manage, and almost every diabetes article about the holidays only talks about the meal.

This is the playbook for the rest of it. The travel day, the delays, the landing. Keep it simple.

1. TSA when the airport is packed

Holiday crowds mean long lines and rushed officers, and that is exactly when diabetes supplies get flagged and slow you down. The fix is to declare before your bags go through the scanner. Tell the officer you have diabetes supplies and medical liquids in your bag. It takes ten seconds and it saves ten minutes.

Medically necessary liquids and gel packs are allowed in your carry-on, frozen, slushy, or melted, as long as you declare them. Juice boxes or gel for treating lows count. Keep your medical stuff together in one pouch or bag so it is easy to show. I wrote a whole post on how your medical bag works as a carry-on, and holiday travel is when that matters most.

2. Delays are a diabetes problem, not just an annoyance

Everyone hates delays. For us, a three-hour delay is three extra hours your supplies have to cover. That is why I always pack 20% more than I calculate I will need. Holiday travel is the exact reason that rule exists.

And none of it goes in the checked bag. Ever. Checked bags get lost, get delayed, and sit in freezing cargo holds. You cannot treat a low or change a failed pod from a bag that is somewhere over the Midwest. Insulin, pods, sensors, and fast sugar ride in your carry-on or personal item, full stop.

For lows on the move, I keep sugar packets on me. One at 60 to 70, two below 60. I grab extras at coffee shops, because travel days burn through them faster than normal days.

3. The time-zone hop

Most holiday trips are short and cross a zone or two, which means your normal schedule mostly carries over. But if you are flying coast to coast for Christmas, your pump clock and your long-acting insulin timing do not move themselves.

Run your dates through the Time Zone Planner before you leave. It tells you exactly when your timing shifts, so you are not doing that math in a crowded airport.

4. Landing into the big meal

This is the part nobody writes about. You have had a weird travel day: irregular food, lots of sitting, maybe some stress highs or a random low in the airport. Then you walk into Thanksgiving or Christmas dinner, the biggest carb meal of the year, and your blood sugar is already somewhere it should not be.

My travel food rule keeps the day boring on purpose. Low-carb, high-protein breakfast to stabilize the day, then protein-focused choices for the rest of the day. A stable landing matters more than a perfect meal.

I will be honest with you. I am the ultimate holiday guy. I start watching Christmas movies and listening to Christmas music in September. You can judge me, it is fine. But the holidays and type 1 diabetes can be rough.

The food is carb heavy, and it is usually more homemade food, which makes carb tracking and counting even harder. For years I struggled with the big meal. Most years I ended up going low or high from underestimating or over-guessing how many carbs I ate and how much insulin to take.

Over the last few years I have gotten better at it. Two things made the difference. First, I focus on the higher protein foods on the table. Second, instead of one big dose before the meal, I take smaller doses throughout the meal. It is not perfect, but it beats the old guessing game.

Do not chase perfection on the meal. The goal is to stay in the game through dessert, not to have a flat line on the biggest eating day of the year.

5. Cold weather is a supply problem

If you are flying somewhere cold for the holidays, remember that insulin must never freeze. Frozen insulin is ruined insulin, and it does not look different enough to notice.

Keep insulin close to your body, not in a cold car trunk, not in the hotel room's unheated luggage area, and never in a checked bag. Your body heat is the safest storage you have on a cold travel day.

6. New Year's Eve

If your holiday season ends with a party, know this: alcohol can lower blood sugar, and the drop can show up hours after you stop drinking. Eat real food with drinks, keep fast sugar on you all night, and set your CGM low alarms loud. Nobody wants to spend January 1st explaining a seizure to their family.

Also, test or glance at your sensor before you drive anywhere. Every year, every state, every holiday. Do not be the story.

Frequently asked questions

Can I bring juice, gel packs, and snacks through TSA?

Yes. Medically necessary liquids and gel packs are allowed in your carry-on, frozen, slushy, or melted, as long as you declare them at the checkpoint. Juice boxes or gel for treating lows count as medically necessary. Tell the TSA officer you have diabetes supplies before your bags go through the scanner.

Should I pack diabetes supplies in my checked bag?

No, never. Checked bags can be lost, delayed, or exposed to freezing cargo holds, and you cannot treat a low or a pump failure from the overhead bin of the terminal. Keep all insulin, supplies, and fast sugar in your carry-on or personal item.

How much extra insulin and supplies should I pack for a holiday trip?

My rule is 20% more than you calculate you will need. Holiday travel is the exact reason the rule exists: flights get delayed, trips get extended, and extra supplies turn a stressful travel day around.

How do I handle the time zone change on a short holiday trip?

Short trips usually mean small time shifts, so your normal schedule mostly carries over. For bigger jumps, run your dates through the free Time Zone Planner on this site before you leave so you know when your pump clock and long-acting insulin timing actually move.
