Flying with a Diabetes Alert Dog: The DOT Rules, the Forms, and the Flight (2026)

Updated November 2026 · 9 min read

A diabetes alert dog, often shortened to DAD, is a dog trained to notice when your blood sugar is rising or falling and to tell you about it before you feel it yourself. For some people with type 1 diabetes it is the layer that catches the low a continuous glucose monitor (CGM), a small sensor that reads your glucose every few minutes, misses: at night, in a noisy terminal, or on a long flight when the phone is in airplane mode. Taking that dog on a plane is possible, and the dog rides in the cabin with you, but the rules are strict and they are not the rules most people expect. Air travel runs on a different law than hotels and restaurants, the paperwork is one federal form with a 48-hour clock, and the dog that flies free is the trained one, not the comforting one. This guide walks through each rule as it is published, so you can book with confidence and keep the dog working through the whole trip.

One honest note about who is writing this. I have had type 1 diabetes for 20 years and I travel with an Omnipod 5 insulin pump and a Dexcom G7, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that reads glucose every few minutes. I pre-bolus in my usual way before eating, and I treat lows with sugar packets: 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. I do not travel with a diabetes alert dog, and this guide does not describe a personal dog story. It is built from the published rules of the U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT), the Transportation Security Administration (TSA), the U.S. Department of Justice, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). This guide does not tell you what medicine to take or what amount to use. Those decisions belong to you and your care team.

Talk to your care team before your next trip about your own travel plan. This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not change any medicine, any insulin amount, or any treatment decision. If a low keeps happening during travel, or fear of a low is the reason you are not flying, tell your care team.

1. What a diabetes alert dog actually does

A diabetes alert dog is trained to notice the scent changes in breath and sweat that come with a blood sugar swing, and to give one set signal when it smells one, such as a nose nudge or a paw on your leg. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) gives diabetes as its own example of a service animal task: a dog that is trained to alert its handler when blood sugar reaches high or low levels. That trained alert is the whole point of the dog, and it is also what makes the dog a service animal under the law. A pet dog that happens to notice your lows, however loyal, is not one, because the dog has not been trained to take a specific action tied to your disability.

Two limits matter. First, the dog is a backup layer, not a replacement for the tools you already use. A continuous glucose monitor (CGM), fingerstick checks, and insulin stay essential, and the dog works alongside them, not instead of them. Second, training is not uniform: programs train dogs differently and with different levels of rigor, and no single standard covers them all. If you are thinking about getting a dog, talk to your care team and to a training program directly, and ask how they train the alert, how long it takes, and what it costs. This guide does not recommend a program, a breed, or a price, because none of those were verified on an official source read today.

2. Two different laws: the plane runs on the ACAA, the hotel runs on the ADA

The most useful thing to know before you book is that air travel and everything after landing run on two different laws. On a plane, the law is the Air Carrier Access Act (ACAA), enforced by the U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT). In a hotel, a restaurant, or a shop, the law is the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). The two laws use the same core definition, a dog individually trained to do work or perform tasks tied to a disability, but they ask for different proof and they give you different rights.

Under the DOT rule for air travel, a service animal is a dog, regardless of breed or type, that is individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a qualified individual with a disability. Animal species other than dogs, emotional support animals, comfort animals, companionship animals, and service animals in training are not service animals for air travel. That last line is the one that surprises people: since the DOT final rule took effect on January 11, 2021, an emotional support animal no longer counts as a service animal on a plane. An animal that helps by being near you travels as a pet now, with the pet fee, the carrier, and the size limits that come with it.

Under the ADA, the definition is the same shape, and the ADA goes further on proof. Staff in a public place may ask only two questions: is the dog a service animal required because of a disability, and what work or task has the dog been trained to perform. They may not ask for documentation, require the dog to demonstrate its task, or ask about the nature of your disability. A service animal does not have to wear a vest, an ID tag, or a specific harness, and no business may require proof of certification, training, or licensing as a condition for entry. The ADA also says the handler may train the dog, no professional program is required, and any breed may be a service animal. The airline rules are stricter than that, which is the next section.

3. The DOT form: the one document that matters

For air travel, the airline may require one document, and only one document, for most flights: the U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) Service Animal Air Transportation Form. The current version was updated in September 2024, and it is a short form with five sections, all self-attested. You fill in your name, phone, and email, and you attest to four things: the dog is vaccinated for rabies and free of fleas, ticks, or disease; the dog has been individually trained to perform a task; the dog has been trained to behave in public; and the dog has not behaved aggressively or caused serious injury to another person or animal. You also give the name and phone number of the trainer and the veterinarian. Neither one has to sign the form, and no training certificate is required, but the airline may call the trainer or the veterinarian to check what you wrote.

The form also asks you to confirm that you understand the rules that ride along with it. The dog must be harnessed, leashed, or tethered at all times in the airport and on the aircraft. The handler is responsible for the dog at all times, including toileting and feeding. If the dog causes damage, the airline may charge you for the repair, the same as it would charge any passenger for similar damage. And the warning at the top is real: it is a federal crime to make materially false, fictitious, or fraudulent statements on the form, under 18 U.S.C. 1001.

For a flight segment scheduled at 8 hours or longer, the airline may also require a second form, the DOT Service Animal Relief Attestation Form, on which you attest that the dog either will not need to relieve itself during the flight or can do so in a way that does not create a health or sanitation problem. That is the whole list. Airlines are not permitted to require other documentation from service animal users, except to comply with requirements from a federal agency, a U.S. territory, or a foreign jurisdiction. A vest, an ID card, a registry entry, or a certificate bought online is not on the list, and no airline may ask for one.

4. The 48-hour rule, and the last-minute booking protection

The form has a clock on it, and the clock depends on when you booked. If your reservation was made more than 48 hours before departure, the airline may require the completed form up to 48 hours in advance of the flight. Send it as soon as you book, through the airline's own process, and keep a copy on your phone. American Airlines, as one example, asks handlers to submit the form electronically to its Special Assistance Desk at least 48 hours before the flight, or to present the completed form at the airport on travel day.

If you bought your ticket within 48 hours of the flight, the airline is not permitted to require the form in advance. It must allow you to submit the completed form at the gate on the date of travel, so arrive early and bring the paper copy. The form goes to the airline, never to the DOT, and the airline may require it once per trip, not once per flight, so one form covers a round trip. If you miss the advance window on a booking made more than 48 hours out, the airline cannot refuse to transport the dog without first trying reasonable efforts to accommodate you, but do not plan on that. The 48-hour window is the safe plan, and the gate submission is the backup for a last-minute booking.

5. At the checkpoint: TSA screening with a dog

At the checkpoint, the dog changes the screening routine in a few specific ways, and the TSA rules for it are published on tsa.gov. You and your service dog are screened by a walk-through metal detector, and you may walk through together or lead the dog through separately on a leash. If you opt not to be screened by the Advanced Imaging Technology (AIT) body scanner, the booth where you raise your arms, or the walk-through metal detector, you will undergo a pat-down instead. If the metal detector alarms, you and the dog undergo additional screening, including a pat-down. During that additional screening, do not make contact with the dog, other than holding the leash, until the officer has completed inspection of the dog. TSA will not separate you from your service animal at any point in the process, and you can ask to speak with a supervisor or a passenger support specialist if you have concerns.

The dog's gear is screened too. Collars, harnesses, leashes, backpacks, vests, and other items are subject to security screening, but items that are necessary to maintain control of the dog or show that the dog is on duty do not require removal to be screened. Medication for the dog must go through X-ray or inspection screening, so separate it and tell the officer you carry it for your service dog. If you have to leave the checkpoint to relieve the dog, you and the dog go through the screening process again, and you may request to move to the front of the line on your return. Ask an airport or airline staff member where the nearest service animal relief area is before you go through, not after. Our TSA rules guide covers the checkpoint for travelers without a dog, and our pat-down rights guide covers the full screening choice for your pump and CGM, including the exact sentences to say.

Your pump and CGM still follow their own rules in the same lane. In a PreCheck lane, additional screening of your insulin pump or CGM is only required if the device alarms in the walk-through metal detector or the body scanner, and the opt-out still works: you can ask for a pat-down instead of the body scanner, with no reason, doctor's note, or permission needed. Our PreCheck guide covers the lane in full. If you want help at the checkpoint, call TSA Cares at 1-855-787-2227 within 72 hours of your flight.

6. On the plane: where the dog sits, and what can get you denied

Once you board, the DOT rules are plain about where the dog goes. Your service animal must be permitted to accompany you in the space under the seat in front of you. Certain small service animals may be permitted to sit on your lap, if it can be done safely. The dog cannot block a space that must stay clear for safety reasons, such as an aisle or access to an emergency exit. The airline is not required to upgrade you to a different class of service to fit the dog, so if the dog is large, book a seat with enough foot space and talk to the airline before the trip, not at the gate.

The dog must behave properly on the plane. An animal that barks or snarls, runs around, or jumps onto other passengers without being provoked will not be accepted as a service animal, and the DOT form says the same thing in plainer terms: if the dog shows it has not been trained to behave in public, the airline may treat it as a pet, charge a pet fee, require a carrier, or deny transport. Airlines cannot refuse a service animal because other passengers or flight crew are uncomfortable with it, but they may deny transport for a short list of reasons: the dog is too large or heavy to fit in the cabin, it poses a direct threat to the health or safety of others, it causes a significant disruption in the cabin or at the gate, it violates a health requirement, or the required DOT forms were not provided.

If you believe your rights under the Air Carrier Access Act (ACAA) are being or have been violated, ask to speak with a Complaints Resolution Official, known as a CRO. The CRO is the airline's expert on disability accommodation issues, and the airline is required to make one available to you, at no cost, in person at the airport or by telephone during the times the airline is operating. On a long flight, remember the dog is working too: bring water and a collapsible bowl, food for the dog, waste bags, and the dog's medication, and plan the low plan for yourself as usual, with sugar packets in your pocket, 1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60. 7. Hotels, restaurants, and the rest of the trip

Once you land, the ADA takes over, and the rules get simpler. A hotel may not assign you a pet-friendly room because you have a service animal: you must be given the same opportunity to reserve any available room as other guests. A hotel may not charge a cleaning fee for the hair or dander shed by a service animal, though if the dog damages the room, the hotel may charge the same fee for damages as it charges other guests. In a restaurant or a shop, the two-question rule from section 2 applies, and no documentation may be required. Our hotel insulin storage guide covers the other hotel problem, keeping insulin cool in a room with a minibar fridge, and our child travel playbook covers the family version of the trip, including the one-page plan for grandparents and caregivers, which is the same kind of plan the adults on the trip should know if the dog alerts and your child is the handler.

One more ADA point worth knowing: service animals in training are not service animals under the ADA, so a dog still in training does not have the same access rights in public places. Some state or local laws cover animals in training, so check the law where you are traveling, but do not assume a dog in training flies as a service animal. Under the DOT rule for air travel, service animals in training are also excluded. If the dog is still learning the alert, finish the training before you book the flight.

8. Flying abroad with the dog

International trips add a second set of rules, and the destination country is the one that decides. The DOT warns that U.S. airlines flying to foreign countries are subject to that country's requirements on service animals, and not all countries permit service animals from other countries. Check whether your destination permits the dog and what it requires to enter and exit legally, before you book, because an airline cannot override a country's border rules. Our international travel guide covers the rest of the trip abroad, from insulin at the border to the time zone reset on landing, and our finding insulin abroad playbook covers what to do if you run low on insulin in another country.

For the trip home, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has its own form, and it applies to every dog, service dog included. A CDC Dog Import Form is required for each dog you bring into the United States. You complete it online, confirm your email, and receive a receipt, which you show to the airline before boarding and to U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) on arrival. If the dog has been only in dog rabies-free or low-risk countries in the 6 months before entry, the form is the only requirement, and the receipt is valid for one dog to enter multiple times from the same country within six months of issuance. If the dog has been in a high-risk country for dog rabies in the last 6 months, there are extra requirements: a rabies vaccination certificate, a microchip, a photo of the dog, and for foreign-vaccinated dogs a reservation at a CDC-registered animal care facility. The one exception to the animal care facility rule is a service animal arriving at a U.S. seaport that meets all entry requirements, including a valid rabies serology titer.

9. The holiday plan: before you book

Thanksgiving falls on Thursday, November 26, and the Christmas flights come right after it. Holiday airports are the worst place to learn a new rule, so the dog paperwork belongs in the same week as the ticket booking, not the week of the flight. Here is the full list, in this order:

Flying with a Diabetes Alert Dog: The Trip Checklist

  • Confirm the dog is a trained service animal: individually trained to alert to high or low blood sugar, trained to behave in public, and harnessed, leashed, or tethered at all times
  • Complete the DOT Service Animal Air Transportation Form, and the relief attestation if any flight segment is scheduled at 8 hours or longer; send it to the airline, never to the DOT, up to 48 hours before departure if you booked more than 48 hours out, or bring it to the gate if you booked inside 48 hours
  • Book a seat with enough foot space for the dog under the seat in front of you; the airline is not required to upgrade you, and the dog cannot block an aisle or an emergency exit
  • Find the service animal relief area at each airport before you go through security; if you exit the checkpoint to relieve the dog, you go through screening again and may ask to move to the front of the line
  • At the checkpoint: walk through the metal detector with the dog, together or separately on a leash; if it alarms, expect a pat-down for you and an inspection of the dog, and do not touch the dog other than holding the leash until the officer finishes
  • Call TSA Cares at 1-855-787-2227 within 72 hours of the flight if you want a passenger support specialist at the checkpoint; if a rights problem comes up, ask for a Complaints Resolution Official (CRO)
  • Pack the dog's kit in your carry-on: water and a collapsible bowl, food, waste bags, and the dog's medication, separated for X-ray or inspection screening
  • Pack 20% extra diabetes supplies; our packing list does the counting; sugar packets in your pocket (1 packet at 60 to 70, 2 below 60); keep the low kit at your seat, not in the overhead bin; bring your glucagon kit and tell your seatmate and the crew where it is
  • For an international trip: check the destination country's service animal rules before you book, and complete the CDC Dog Import Form for the trip home, with the rabies certificate, microchip, and photo ready if the dog has been in a high-risk country in the last 6 months
  • At the hotel: any available room, not a pet-friendly room only, and no cleaning fee for shed hair or dander; damage is charged the same as for any other guest

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Does a diabetes alert dog count as a service animal on a plane?

A: Yes, if the dog is individually trained to do a task tied to your diabetes. The U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) defines a service animal for air travel as a dog, regardless of breed or type, that is individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a qualified individual with a disability, and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) gives diabetes as the example: a dog trained to alert when blood sugar reaches high or low levels. A pet dog that happens to notice your lows is not a service animal, and neither is an emotional support animal, which has not been trained to perform a specific task. The training is what makes the dog a service animal, not a vest, a card, or a certificate.

Q: What paperwork does the airline need for my diabetes alert dog?

A: The airline may require one form, and only one form, for most flights: the U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) Service Animal Air Transportation Form. On it you attest that the dog is vaccinated for rabies and free of fleas, ticks, or disease, that it has been individually trained to perform a task, that it has been trained to behave in public, and that it has not behaved aggressively or caused serious injury. You also give the name and phone number of the trainer and the veterinarian, though neither one has to sign it. For a flight segment scheduled at 8 hours or longer, the airline may also require the DOT Service Animal Relief Attestation Form. No vest, ID card, registration, or training certificate may be required. False statements on the form are a federal crime under 18 U.S.C. 1001.

Q: How far in advance do I send the DOT form?

A: If you booked more than 48 hours before departure, the airline may require the completed form up to 48 hours in advance, so send it as soon as you book and keep a copy on your phone. If you bought your ticket within 48 hours of the flight, the airline cannot require advance submission and must let you hand the completed form in at the gate on travel day. The form goes to the airline, never to the DOT, and the airline may require it once per trip, not once per flight, so one form covers a round trip. If you miss the advance window, the airline cannot refuse the dog without first trying reasonable efforts to accommodate you, but do not plan on that: send it early.

Q: What happens at TSA security with a service dog?

A: You and your service dog are screened by a walk-through metal detector, and you may walk through together or lead the dog through separately on a leash. If you opt not to be screened by the Advanced Imaging Technology (AIT) body scanner or the walk-through metal detector, you will get a pat-down instead. If the metal detector alarms, you and the dog get additional screening, including a pat-down, and during that screening you should not touch the dog other than holding the leash until the officer has finished inspecting the dog. TSA will not separate you from your service animal at any point. The dog's collar, harness, leash, backpack, and vest are screened, but items needed to keep control of the dog or show it is on duty do not have to be removed. If you have to leave the checkpoint to relieve the dog, you go through screening again and may ask to move to the front of the line on your return.

Q: Does my diabetes alert dog need a vest, ID card, or certificate?

A: No. Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), a service animal does not have to wear a vest, ID tag, or specific harness, and no business or hotel may require documentation, proof of certification, training, or licensing as a condition for entry. Mandatory registration of service animals is not permitted under the ADA, though local dog licensing and vaccination rules still apply to every dog. For air travel, the rule is narrower: the airline may require the DOT Service Animal Air Transportation Form, and for flights of 8 hours or longer the relief attestation, and nothing else except requirements from a federal agency, a U.S. territory, or a foreign jurisdiction. Any website selling a service dog certificate or registry is selling a piece of paper the law does not ask for.

Q: Can I take my diabetes alert dog abroad?

A: Sometimes, and the answer depends on the destination, not on the airline. The DOT warns that U.S. airlines flying to foreign countries are subject to that country's rules on service animals, and not all countries permit service animals from other countries, so check the destination's rules before you book. For the trip home, every dog entering the United States needs a CDC Dog Import Form receipt, which you show to the airline before boarding and to U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) on arrival. If the dog has been only in dog rabies-free or low-risk countries in the 6 months before entry, the form is the only requirement, and the receipt is good for multiple entries from the same country within six months. Dogs that have been in a high-risk country for dog rabies in the last 6 months face extra requirements, including a rabies vaccination certificate, a microchip, a photo, and for foreign-vaccinated dogs a reservation at a CDC-registered animal care facility.
