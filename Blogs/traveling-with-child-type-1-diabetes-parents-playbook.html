Traveling with a Child Who Has Type 1 Diabetes: The Parent's Playbook (2026)

On diabetes forums, parents ask this question every week: we are taking our child with type 1 diabetes on a trip, what do we need to know? The short answer is that the trip runs on the same rules as adult diabetes travel, plus one parent-sized layer: paperwork, packing for two adults, the airport with a nervous kid, theme park lines, and a written plan for anyone else who watches your child. Here is the full playbook.

Updated October 2026  ·  9 min read

Traveling with type 1 diabetes (T1D) as an adult is mostly a packing and logistics problem. Traveling with a child who has T1D is a logistics problem where you are also the pancreas, the pharmacy, and the calm voice. The good news from every parent thread is the same: the first trip is the scary one, and the plan below is what makes the second trip boring. As always, this is general guidance, not medical advice. Your child's care team owns the medical plan; this page owns the travel plan.

The paperwork kit

Put these in one folder, physical or on your phone, before every trip:

1. The travel letter. The TSA (Transportation Security Administration) does not require any documentation for diabetes supplies, but bring the letter anyway. It helps at foreign airports, with large quantities of supplies, and any time someone questions the needles or devices. The letter should be on letterhead and list the supplies your child carries.

2. Prescriptions and the dose list. A copy of every prescription plus a one-page medication list with exact doses. If supplies are lost or stolen, this is what the pharmacist or the on-call line needs to replace them fast. The full replacement playbook is in the lost and stolen supplies guide.

3. Travel insurance that names the diabetes. Most policies treat type 1 diabetes as a pre-existing condition. Declare your child's T1D when you buy, and check that the policy covers medical care and medication replacement abroad. The travel insurance guide covers the 14 to 21 day waiver rule.

4. The TSA Cares call. If the airport part worries you, and it worries most parents the first time, call TSA Cares at (855) 787-2227 at least 72 hours before you fly. TSA's own passenger-support page lists traveling with a child who has a disability or medical condition as one of the examples for requesting a Passenger Support Specialist, a trained officer who walks your family through screening.

Pack like there are two of you

The site's packing rule applies double here: pack 20% more supplies than the trip math says, because travel eats supplies. Then split everything between the two adults. If one bag is lost or one parent is delayed, the child still has insulin, pump supplies, sensors, and fast carbs. One bag lost is never all supplies lost.

Build your child a low kit that lives on their person, not in a bag across the room: juice boxes or glucose tablets in a small pouch they carry. Juice boxes are the kid-friendly pick because they are fast, pre-measured, and easy to drink when a child feels shaky. Keep a second low kit in each adult's day bag. Every supply flies in the carry-on, never in checked luggage, and on the plane the fast carbs sit at the child's seat, not in the overhead bin.

Label everything. Prescription labels, the child's name on the meter case and the low kit, and a card in the supply bag with your phone number. Labeled supplies come back faster, and a labeled bag is easier to explain at any checkpoint.

Put the numbers on both parents' phones

If your child wears a Dexcom, the Dexcom Follow app lets parents see glucose readings on their own phones, which means the numbers are visible even when your child is at the kids' club, on a ride, or asleep in the hotel bed next to you. Sharing needs an internet connection to work, so test it at home before the trip, not at the airport. Dexcom's own guidance is to confirm readings on the child's app or receiver before making treatment decisions, and that applies on vacation too. Other CGM (continuous glucose monitor) systems have their own family-sharing apps, so check yours before you go.

Agree on the night plan before the first hotel night: one parent keeps their phone unmuted with the low alarm on, and you trade off. A new bed, a long travel day, and different food make the first night the one to watch, not the third.

At the airport

Tell the TSA officer at the start of screening that your child has diabetes and is wearing medical devices, and say which screening you prefer. Keep the routine boring and predictable for your child: same order every time, supplies in the same pouch, low kit in the same pocket. If you called TSA Cares, your Passenger Support Specialist meets you at the checkpoint.

On the plane, keep mealtimes as close to home routine as you can. Bring familiar snacks, because an unfamiliar airport meal plus excitement is a hard combination to dose for, and your care team can advise on the travel-day plan. If the flight crosses time zones, the time zone guide covers how to shift the schedule: timing only, never doses, and the care team decides the details.

The theme park day

Theme parks are the ultimate kid trip, and they hit diabetes with three things at once: heat, hours of walking, and long lines with no easy exit. Parents on diabetes forums report that park days often run lower than usual, so check more often than you would at home and treat early.

Disney's line pass, explained honestly. The Disability Access Service (DAS) lets eligible guests request a return time for an attraction instead of waiting in the standby line. Registration happens through a live video chat with a Disney cast member, which you can start between 60 days and 2 days before your park visit, and no doctor's note is required. Here is the honest part: Disney describes DAS as intended for guests with developmental disabilities, and diabetes decisions are made case by case. Families report both approvals and denials. The conversation is about functional impact, not the diagnosis, so explain why your child cannot wait in a conventional line: a low in a long outdoor queue with no place to sit and treat is unsafe. If DAS is declined, ask the cast member what other accommodation fits your child's needs. Other major parks run their own line-accommodation programs, so check the park's accessibility page before you go, because the rules change.

The park-day kit. A small backpack with the low kit, a water bottle, sunscreen, and a cooling pouch for insulin if it is hot. Plan shade and sit-down breaks into the day instead of waiting for someone to feel shaky. Agree in advance on the low plan: alert the nearest staff member, sit somewhere safe, treat with fast carbs, and do not rejoin a line or board a ride until the number is back up and your child feels normal.

Handing the plan to grandparents or another caregiver

The family trip where someone else watches your child for an afternoon is its own skill. Write the one-page plan and hand it over in person:

What goes on the page. The medication list with exact doses and times, the hypo steps in order (what a low looks like in your child, what to give, when to call you, when to call for emergency help), the hyper plan your care team gave you, the emergency numbers, and where the low kit and the spare supplies live. Keep the language plain enough that a grandparent who has never dosed insulin can follow it.

The fridge check. Show them where the insulin lives: in the fridge, between 36 and 46F, never in the freezer and never somewhere it can freeze. Insulin that has frozen must be discarded. If the room only has a minibar-style cooler, confirm it actually holds temperature before trusting it with insulin.

Keep the routine. Meals, checks, and bedtime as close to home as the trip allows. Kids do better on routine, and so do their numbers. Walk the caregiver through one full day before you leave them in charge.

The parent's pre-trip checklist

Here is the full list, in this order:

  1. Travel letter, prescription copies, and the one-page dose list in one folder.
  2. Travel insurance bought with your child's type 1 diabetes declared.
  3. Call TSA Cares at (855) 787-2227 at least 72 hours before the flight if you want checkpoint support.
  4. Pack 20% more supplies than the trip math says.
  5. Split all supplies between the two adults; everything flies in the carry-on.
  6. Low kit on your child (juice boxes or glucose tablets), backup low kits in each adult's bag.
  7. CGM sharing tested at home; night watch plan agreed for hotel nights.
  8. Fast carbs at the child's seat on the plane, not in the overhead bin.
  9. Theme park day: DAS video chat done 2 to 60 days ahead if you want it; park-day kit packed; low plan agreed.
  10. One-page written plan for any other caregiver, fridge check done, routine walked through.
General guidance only, not medical advice. Every child's diabetes is different: work with your child's care team on dosing, low treatment, and the travel-day plan. Park programs and TSA procedures change, so confirm the current rules before you travel.
